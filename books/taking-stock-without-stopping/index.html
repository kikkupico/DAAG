Taking Stock Without Stopping

Based on “Distributed Snapshots: Determining Global States of Distributed Systems” by K. Mani Chandy and Leslie Lamport (1985)

How do you take stock of a whole system that never stops moving, when no one can see all of it at once? And what can such a count reliably tell you?

Chapter I

The Archon's Question

Kleitos, archon of Arche in a winter when trade across the island multiplied, asked the trading houses a question that sounded simple: how much grain and oil does the island hold? Not how much the houses had bought from and sold into their three port towns, which the house ledgers recorded, but how much was there, at one moment, everywhere at once.

Where is it? In two kinds of place. Some sits in the storehouses of the three houses, House 1, House 2 and House 3. The rest is on the ring road between them, in carts and pack-trains that take as long as they take*.

Why not send an auditor to each house, count each storehouse, and add the three counts? That was the scribes' first proposal. Chandyos, who kept the island's customs rolls, sent the men out, walked down to the cliff road to watch the carts pass for an hour, and came back to say it would not do. Two mornings show why.

Suppose House 3's auditor counts at dawn and House 2's late in the day. In between, a cart carries two hundred jars of oil from House 3 to House 2. House 3 counted them; House 2 counted them again when they arrived. The total is two hundred jars too high: the island has made oil merely by asking after it.

Now reverse the cart. The counts are taken as before, but in between a cart carries two hundred jars from House 2 to House 3. They had left House 2's storehouse before its auditor looked, and had not reached House 3 when its auditor closed his slate. Nobody counted them. Two hundred jars have vanished, and there is no thief.

The error, stated once

Counting the houses one after another produces a total that is wrong by exactly the goods that moved along the ring road between one counting and the next. The error can run in either direction, cannot be bounded in advance, and nothing in the three local ledgers reveals that it has occurred.

Counting the houses one after another counts the road wrongly, and nothing in the ledgers shows by how much. Then why not stop the road, so that there is nothing on it to count?

Chapter II

Why the Road Is Not Stopped

The archon proposed exactly that: from a given morning no cart moves, every storehouse is counted while everything stands still, and then the trains move again. With nothing in transit, the three counts add up. What is wrong with it?

First, it is costly. The houses feed the three port towns, and a road that stops moving food stops serving the island. A census that halts all trade will be tolerated once and never repeated.

Second, and this is the objection that matters: can the road even be stopped all at once? The order to stop travels by messenger along the same road, and takes as long as the road takes. House 2 may receive it an hour before House 3, and in that hour House 3, which has heard nothing, will quite properly send a cart down the road. That does no harm, as it happens. Each house stops sending when the order reaches it, keeps taking in whatever arrives, and counts when its incoming roads are empty. Houses that stop at different hours still give a true total, so long as each waits until nothing more is coming.

But how is a house to know that nothing more is coming? It cannot see the road, and a cart may be hours out, round the next headland, behind a lame mule. The only way is for each house, the moment it stops, to send a last messenger down each of its roads who means nothing behind me. Nobody overtakes on the road, so when that messenger comes in, the road behind him is empty. So stopping the island needs a marker on every road anyway. And once the houses have such markers, Chandyos saw, why stop at all?

Stated formally The system is a set of processes (the houses) connected by directed channels (the one-way tracks of the ring road). A channel delivers messages and goods in the order sent (FIFO), without loss or corruption, after an arbitrary but finite delay. There is no shared clock, and no process can directly observe another's state. A global state consists of the local state of every process together with the state of every channel.

Lamportios, who had come across from House 1 with his tallies, asked a different question. Must the census catch the island at one instant? What the archon needs is a total that is sound: one into which no cart has been counted twice, and from which none has gone missing. Whether that total was ever true of the island at a single moment is a separate question, and one the archon has no practical need to ask.

A sound total, then, taken while the carts keep moving. But the goods on the road are exactly what the auditors got wrong. How can a house that never sees the road count what is on it?

Chapter III

What the Road Is Holding

Start with what is being counted. Is the cargo on the road part of the island's stock? Each house thinks of its stock as what sits under its own roof. Oil in a cellar is stock; oil in jars lashed to a mule on the cliff road is in no house's storehouse and on no house's slate. Yet it exists, it is owned, and it is precisely the quantity that the auditors counted twice or lost.

So the island is to be counted not as three quantities but as nine: the storehouses of House 1, House 2 and House 3, and, separately, what each road segment is holding. There are six directed tracks on the ring road, one for each ordered pair of houses, and each carries only its own traffic*.

The nine quantities

A complete account of Arche's goods consists of: what sits in each of the three house storehouses, plus what is in transit along each of the six road segments between them. A method that records only the storehouses does not give an incomplete account of Arche; it gives a complete account of a different island, one in which carts take zero time to travel.

Can a scribe at a desk count a road? He cannot see it: a cart rounding a headland is hidden behind rock, and nobody can post men every ten paces along the surf. What a house can do is record what comes in at its own gate. Chandyos saw that this is enough, provided the house knows exactly when to start writing down arrivals, and when to stop.

When to start is easy: when the house counts its own storehouse. But when to stop? What could tell a house, standing at its gate, that the last cart sent before the count has now come in?

Chapter IV

The Red Sash

The same marker the stopped road needed: a messenger wearing a plain red sash. He carries no goods, no orders and no tallies. His whole purpose is to divide what was sent before him on a track from what was sent after him.

Two rules govern him, one for sending and one for receiving, and they are the entire method.

The rule of sending
  1. A house records the inventory of its own storehouse.
  2. Immediately, and before dispatching any further carts or orders along any outgoing road segment, it dispatches one messenger bearing the red sash down that road.
The rule of receiving
  1. A red sash messenger arrives along a road segment from another house. If the receiving house has not yet recorded its own storehouse — it records its storehouse immediately, writes down that this incoming road segment holds nothing, and then obeys the rule of sending, dispatching a red sash messenger down all its outgoing road segments.
  2. If the receiving house has already recorded its storehouse — it writes down, as that road segment's holding, all goods that arrived along that road between the moment it recorded its own storehouse and the arrival of this red sash messenger.

Who starts it? Any house, and it makes no difference which. Is anything halted? No cart is delayed and no mule stopped, and no house needs to know the state of any other.

The second clause of the receiving rule does the work. A house that has recorded its own storehouse goes on taking in carts as usual, but from that moment it keeps a slate for each incoming road, listing every jar and sack that arrives along it. When the red sash comes through the gate, that road's slate is closed, and its sum is the road's holding. The house never saw the road. It only needed to know when to open the slate and when to close it, and the sash marked both.

What does the method rely on? Two properties of the ring road*:
(1) Strict FIFO ordering. Nothing overtakes on a track. A red sash dispatched after a cart cannot pass that cart, and a cart dispatched after the sash cannot pass the sash.
(2) Reliable delivery. No cart or messenger falls off the cliff into the sea. If either property fails, the census fails.

Each slate is closed by a sash, and every house records at its own hour. Do the nine numbers then fit together, or can the auditors' error come back in another form?

Chapter V

Cuts That Hold and Cuts That Do Not

To see whether they fit, draw the island's morning. Each house runs down its own lifeline; each cart is an arrow from departure to arrival. The census draws a line across the three lifelines, through each house at the moment it recorded its storehouse. Chandyos called this line a cut. The houses record at different moments, so the line is not level; it slopes across the page.

Above the cut is what the census counts as past; below it is what comes after. An arrow that crosses the cut going downward is a cart sent before its sender recorded and received after its receiver recorded: exactly the goods in transit that the slates catch.

What about an arrow that crosses the cut going upward? That is the fatal error.

Does anyone have to watch for it? No: the rule of sending rules it out. A house sends the red sash down a track before any further cart. No cart can overtake the messenger on the cliff road, so no cart sent after a house records can reach a neighbour before the sash does, and so none can be counted as having arrived before the neighbour recorded.

Proof — that no cart crosses the cut upward
  1. Call an event at a house early if it occurred before that house recorded its own storehouse, and late otherwise.
  2. Suppose some cart were dispatched late at House 2 and arrived early at House 1. This is the upward crossing.
  3. House 2 recorded its storehouse before dispatching that cart, since the dispatch was late. By the rule of sending, House 2 dispatched a red sash messenger down the track to House 1 at the moment of recording — prior to dispatching any further carts down that track.
  4. The ring road is strict FIFO: carts and messengers cannot pass one another. Therefore, the red sash messenger reached House 1 before that cart did.
  5. By the rule of receiving, House 1 recorded its storehouse no later than the arrival of that red sash messenger — either it had already recorded, or the sash forced it to record then.
  6. Thus, House 1 had recorded before the cart arrived, making the cart's arrival late, not early — contradicting step 2.∎

Does the census ever finish? Every house has a road to every other, so a sash sent by any one house reaches both the others. Each house that receives one records and sends sashes down all its outgoing tracks. The island is finite and every messenger arrives in the end, so every house records and every slate is closed.

No cart counted twice, none lost, and the census ends. Then the nine numbers describe the island. But at what moment? Each house recorded at a different hour.

Chapter VI

A Morning Nobody Lived Through

At none. The nine numbers came in, Chandyos summed them for the archon, and then showed that the total described a morning the island had never had.

House 1 recorded first and House 2 some while later. Soon after House 1 recorded, it sold forty jars of oil into its own port town. Later, and still before House 2 recorded, House 2 milled eighty measures of barley into flour. Neither touched a road, a cart or a sash. So the recorded total describes an island in which House 1 still holds the forty jars and House 2 has already milled its barley. Was the island ever in that state? No: while House 1 still held the jars, the barley was not yet milled, and by the time it was, the jars were gone.

Is the total false, then? It is not, and why it is not is the central theorem of the census.

Theorem 1 (Chandy & Lamport 1985)

Let Sbegin be the global state of the island when the census was initiated, S* the global state recorded by the scribes, and Send the global state when the last road slate was closed. Then:

(1) S* is reachable from Sbegin by a valid execution sequence consisting of the exact events that actually occurred on the island, taken in an order that preserves causality.

(2) Send is reachable from S* by the remainder of those same events.

Hence S* lies on a valid path between Sbegin and Send. It is not an idle fiction; it is a state Arche could have passed through had independent events on opposite sides of Mount Phyle unfolded in a different, equally lawful order.

Why may the census reorder the sale and the milling? Because nothing joins them. Two events at different houses with no chain of messengers between them stand in no natural order*, and the census may take either as first. What it may never do is reverse events that one chain of messengers joins, and the red sash sees to it that it never does.

Proof — that the recorded state lies on a valid execution path
  1. Write down the complete sequence of actual events that occurred on the island during the census: e1, e2, …, em. Each event is classified as early or late, depending on whether the house at which it occurred had already recorded its storehouse.
  2. In the actual execution, some late events may precede early events (for example, if House 1 records early while House 2 continues normal operations before recording).
  3. Claim: If a late event ei is immediately followed by an early event ei+1, they may be transposed in the sequence without altering any resulting state.
  4. They cannot occur at the same house: a house transitions from unrecorded to recorded exactly once, so at any single house, every early event precedes every late event.
  5. Neither can depend on the other. ei cannot have caused ei+1: that would require a cart dispatched after recording at one house to arrive before recording at another, which we proved impossible. And ei+1 cannot have caused ei, because it follows it in physical time.
  6. Because ei and ei+1 are concurrent events at different houses, they commute: executing them in reverse order produces the identical state at all houses and on all roads.
  7. Repeatedly transpose adjacent (late, early) pairs. Each swap shifts an early event to the left. This sorting terminates in a valid sequence where all early events precede all late events.
  8. Execute the early events from Sbegin. Because every early event has occurred and no late event has occurred, each house reaches its recorded local state, and each road segment contains exactly the items recorded on its slate. This state is precisely S*.
  9. Executing the remaining late events from S* leads directly to Send.∎

Chandyos put it to the archon in words the customs house remembered: “You asked for a morning, and I have given you a morning; it is only not the particular morning you lived through.”

A morning that could have happened and did not. What can the archon do with a total like that?

Chapter VII

What Such a Total Is Good For

Start with what it cannot do. If a merchant swears that House 2 held three hundred jars of oil at the moment his ship dropped anchor, can the census settle it? No: the census was taken at no one moment, so it cannot speak for his.

Which questions can it answer, then? Those whose answer, once it becomes true, stays true for ever. Chandyos called such conditions settled properties (or stable properties).

Settled properties

A property y is settled if for every state S where y(S) holds, y(S') holds for all states S' reachable from S.

Examples on Arche, counting only the trade between the houses, with the port towns' buying and selling set aside, include:
— Distribution termination: every cart has arrived and all houses are idle.
— Deadlock: every house is blocked waiting for carts from another.
— Exhaustion of supply: a vintage has run out, and no trade between the houses can bring it back.
— Deficit: an accounting imbalance exists that no internal trade can restore.

For these, the census decides:

The decision rule

Evaluate the settled property y on the recorded state S*.

If y(S*) is true: then y is true of the island now, at Send. Because Send is reachable from S*, and a settled property, once true, remains true.

If y(S*) is false: then y was false when the census began, at Sbegin. Because S* is reachable from Sbegin; had y been true at the start, it would still hold at S*.

Either way, the census gives a sure answer about a real state of the island. That the recorded state itself never happened does not matter, because a settled property cannot tell the path the island took from a path it could have taken.

And the archon's own question, how much is there? The census gave a sound inventory, with no jar lost and none counted twice.

Is it ever better than sound? On a day when nothing is bought from or sold into the port towns, goods only move between the houses, and every move takes jars from one of the nine quantities and adds the same jars to another. The total is then the same at every moment of the day. The recorded state has that same total, so on such a day the census gives the exact figure for any moment the archon cares to name. On a day of buying and selling, the recorded total is true of the recorded state and of no moment anyone lived through.

What was true at one particular moment, nothing on an island without a common hour will ever tell.

Chapter VIII

In the Book, In the Paper

So that the reader may verify the correspondence between the cliff road of Arche and the formal mechanics of the algorithm.

The ideas, plainly

A distributed system has no single vantage point from which its total state can be observed simultaneously. Halting all processes and waiting for the channels to drain does work, but it is costly, and detecting the drain already needs a marker on every channel. The Chandy–Lamport snapshot algorithm records a consistent global state — both process states and channel states — while normal computation proceeds uninterrupted.

By dispatching markers along all outgoing channels immediately upon recording local state, and before sending further application messages, processes partition each channel's stream. Because channels are FIFO, markers cannot overtake messages, and messages cannot overtake markers. This guarantees that no message sent after a sender records can be received before a receiver records — the fundamental condition of cut consistency.

The resulting snapshot S* may never have occurred in physical time. However, it is causally valid: reachable from the initial state Sbegin, with the final state Send reachable from it. This makes it sufficient to evaluate stable properties like deadlock, garbage collection, and termination: if the property holds in S*, it holds in the running system.
