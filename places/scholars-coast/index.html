The Scholars' Coast

Libraries up and down a coast, each with its own copies, where nothing ever waits for a vote.

Read this first, and any book set on the scholars' coast can be read next, in any order. It describes the libraries, the couriers between them and the words every book here uses.

↓ Scroll. Things unfold.

The Island of Paxos

Paxos is shaped like a Y. Two arms reach north and face each other across a bay: the western arm holds the Chamber of the Paxon Parliament, the eastern arm the city of Schedia and its Tholos. The arms join at a fork, where the hall of two doors stands, and below the fork the stem widens into the body of the island, the scholars' coast. The island road runs up the stem, forks, and runs out along both arms.

Paxos can read the sun. Unlike Arche in its trading season, the island tells time by day, and the Chamber has a meridian line. Word goes by messenger and by courier along the island's roads, and takes as long as it takes.

Homonoia

The body of Paxos, below the fork, is the scholars' coast. The scholars call it Homonoia, "concord": everyone here ends of one mind, by merging rather than by assembly. It is a coast of scholars and copyists in libraries within casual reach of one another, each holding copies of the works in its keeping.

The scholars hold no assembly, and nothing here waits for a vote, though the Chamber is a morning's walk away. The Parliament passes the law of Paxos, but a scholar's copy of a work is not law, and no decree settles which copy is right.

Couriers and Copies

Letters, corrections and fresh copies travel between the libraries by courier and by word of mouth. Couriers take routes of different lengths, are delayed or stopped, and libraries close. A courier who is stopped cuts one part of the coast off from the rest for as long as he is stopped. An inlet cuts a few libraries off from the rest of the coast by land, and the only crossing is a ferry, which many routes share.

A librarian answers a reader from whatever copy he holds, as a matter of course, and lives with copies that differ for a while. An out-of-date line is put right at the next collation, and what matters is that every copy agrees in the end. That is the line between this coast and the rest of the series: at the camps of Arche, answering from a stale note is done knowingly and only while birds are lost, because two men told different gates is a real harm; here, copies that differ for a while are the ordinary state of things.

Greek scholarship already did everything this coast needs: collating manuscripts line by line, and spreading the epics by rhapsode. Most of what happens here happens in the colonnades. There is a stoa where travelling scholars pass on news, a festival ground where copyists once a year compare their whole collections, and, at the far southern tip of the island, as far from the assemblies as the island goes, the far terraces.

The Words Used Here

Every book on the coast uses a few words the same way. A library is one place that keeps copies. A work is one thing kept, and an edition is one version of it; which edition is newer is settled by a tally, the day and hour by the sun at which it was written, with the library's number to break a tie. A courier carries word between libraries, and a stopped courier cuts the coast in two. A reader is whoever asks a library for a work, and libraries lend: a reader may borrow a copy for a term, and while it is out the library does not have it. The libraries are alike; nothing sets one apart but the part an example gives it.

Reality Checks

What each part of this place has in common with the real Greek world, so that a reader can tell what is borrowed from what is made up. Close means the real thing is much like it; loose means it is borrowed and changed; invented means the setting made it up; anachronism means the real thing is of another age.
