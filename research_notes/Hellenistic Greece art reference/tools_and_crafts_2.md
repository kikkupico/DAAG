## Building tools and methods (chisels, lifting, cranes, levels, clamps, quarrying, brick, mortar, stucco)

### Takeaway
Hellenistic builders used the same iron point/tooth/flat chisel kit as earlier Greeks, shear-leg cranes with compound pulleys and a windlass/capstan, lewis irons or tongs in cut sockets, and lead-poured iron fittings; the best textual description (Vitruvius 10) is Roman-period but explicitly Greek in terminology. Treadwheels are described by Vitruvius and shown only on Roman reliefs; they should not be the default for a Greek site. Level and plumb tools are barely documented for Greece in anything retrieved.

### Cited Findings
**Stone tools and quarrying**
- Stone carving is conservative: the tooth (claw) chisel "has a long history around the Aegean and Italy" and tools "changed little over time"; metal chisels with pointed or flat ends plus picks/axes are the base kit. — [Wootton, Russell and Rockwell 2013, 'Stoneworking tools and toolmarks', Art of Making in Antiquity](https://artofmaking.ac.uk/media/uploads/uploads/stoneworking_tools_and_toolmarks-wootton_russell_rockwell-v1.0.pdf) (essay is about the Roman world; flag Roman)
- Point chisel: metal shaft 20-30 cm long, 1-2.5 cm diameter, pyramidal point; basic roughing tool; the essay quotes that at least 85% of stone removed in carving a statue is cut with it and calls it "the oldest, the simplest and the most versatile of the masons' iron tools". Tooth chisel is used between roughing-out and finishing to remove point marks; on soft stones carvers often went straight from point to flat chisel. — same source
- Flat chisel: shaft 15-25 cm long, 1-2 cm diameter, edge perpendicular to shaft, struck with a metal hammer or sometimes a wooden mallet; leaves near-smooth parallel lines and was often the final surface. Roundel (bullnose) is the same with a curved edge; a flat chisel can be reshaped into a roundel or tooth chisel. — same source
- Quarry pick: wooden handle up to 70-80 cm, metal head pointed at one or both ends, held two-handed. Wooden wedges are driven into rectangular point-cut holes and soaked in water; metal wedges are hammered into cuts; plug-and-feathers (central wedge plus two shims) is traced to Egypt; wedge sockets visible at Aphrodisias quarries. — same source (Roman-period examples such as Felsberg; Aphrodisias is Roman/late Hellenistic)
- Greek marble came from Paros and Naxos; blocks were rough-hewn at the quarry, then cut and bedded precisely; column drums were dry-laid and centred with pins; clamps/dowels of iron or bronze set in lead. — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture) (carried over from pass 1)
- Iron pivots dovetailed into column-shaft ends and "run with lead" so the shaft could be rolled by oxen (Chersiphron and son Metagenes at Ephesus; Paeonius rolled a Temple of Apollo base with two 15-ft wheels) — this is a 6th c. BC story (earlier than Hellenistic) told by a Roman; use only as a method precedent. — [Vitruvius 10.2.11-14 (Gwilt trans., LacusCurtius)]({V10})

**Lifting, cranes, scaffolding**
- Lifting bosses (protrusions left on blocks for ropes and levers) were often left in place after use or kept as stylistic remnants; Propylaia on the Acropolis and Segesta have examples. — [Wikipedia: Lifting boss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lifting_boss)
- A lewis is a stonemason's lifting device that fits a specially shaped lewis hole; chain, split-pin and three-legged types exist; it is used when slings cannot be used because of the stone's shape or delicate projections; holes in upper courses were neatly plugged after setting. (Generic article, not Greece-specific.) — [Wikipedia: Lewis (lifting appliance)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_(lifting_appliance))
- Pierattini (2019, Annual of the British School at Athens, doi 10.1017/S0068245419000030) argues from twin rope grooves on 200-400 kg ashlar blocks at the early temples of Isthmia and Corinth (700-650 BC) that lifting frameworks were in use in the first half of the 7th c. BC, 150 years before the crane c. 515 BC; winch-and-hoist cranes came later (Coulton). — [Phys.org / Notre Dame report](https://phys.org/news/2019-08-greek-temple-machines-centuries-earlier.html)
- Classical temples such as the Parthenon use blocks under 15-20 tonnes, unlike the larger archaic ones; the Romans adopted the Greek crane and added treadwheels; the Haterii tomb relief (late 1st c. AD) is the detailed pictorial source for a Roman treadwheel crane; a polyspastos with winch lifts 3,000 kg with four men, treadwheel doubles that. — [Wikipedia: Crane (machine)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crane_(machine)) (Roman items flagged)
- Vitruvius' simple crane: three timbers joined at the top by a pin and splayed at the feet, raised and held by ropes; a block (rechamus) with two pulleys at the top; a lower block with iron 'shears' whose teeth enter holes cut in the stone; rope wound on an axle turned by levers pushed into holes in the axle; guy ropes fixed to the shoulders and staked to sloping piles. Block with 3 pulleys = trispastos, 2 below and 3 above = pentaspastos. For very heavy loads a drum-wheel (Greek amphiresis or peritrochos) is fixed on the axle; a bigger drum "in which men may walk" gives more power than a capstan. — [Vitruvius 10.2.1-7]({V10}) (Roman text, Greek terminology)
- Vitruvius' polyspaston: a single pole held by four guy ropes, cheeks fixed below the guy attachment, a block with three ranks of pulleys, a third block (Greek epagon, Latin artemo) near the foot; "three sets of men" work without a capstan; the load can sit in front of or beside the pole; the machines also load and unload ships. — [Vitruvius 10.2.8-10]({V10})
- Search summary (not verified on the page): Coulton 1974 showed the standard Greek building crane was a simple shear-legs derrick with compound-pulley tackle, worked by winch or capstan, with evidence in lewis holes, rope channels and clamp cuttings. — [search summary of Coulton, 'Lifting in Early Greek Architecture', JHS 94 (1974)](https://drmsh.com/PaleoBabble/LiftinginEarlyGreekArchitecture.pdf) (PDF scanned; not read)
- Scaffolding: no source was found on Greek scaffolding. (Gap.)

**Levels, plumb, compass, square**
- The chorobates is described by Vitruvius (Wikipedia places it in Book VIII) as a table-like levelling rule about 20 Roman feet (5.92 m) long, with plumb lines against marks and a water groove for windy days; mostly linked to Roman aqueducts; reconstructions differ (table versus 'vertical' type). — [Wikipedia: Chorobates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorobates) (Roman context, flagged; Vitruvius 8 not fetched)
- Plumb bob use is attested "since at least ancient Egypt"; the earliest caliper is reported from the Greek Giglio wreck and calipers were used by Greeks and Romans. — [Wikipedia: Plumb bob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plumb_bob); [Wikipedia: Dividers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dividers)
- Vitruvius uses compasses to divide the ends of a beam when laying out an Archimedean screw. — [Vitruvius 10.6.1]({V10})

**Brick, mortar, stucco**
- Greek brick sizes per Vitruvius: didoron (1 ft by 1/2 ft, the Roman standard), pentadoron (5 palms each way, for public buildings) and tetradoron (4 palms, for private buildings); half-bricks bond so alternate faces show whole and half bricks; best bricks are at least 2 years old, made in spring or autumn from white chalky, red or strong sandy clay; bricks of Pitane in Asia are light enough to float. — [Vitruvius 2.3]({V2}) (Roman text on Greek types)
- Stucco: three sand-lime coats followed by three marble-dust coats, last coat worked until it does not stick to the trowel; colours applied on damp stucco keep their brilliance. Floors: marble dust strewn over a ground, then lime and sand. — [Vitruvius 7.3 and 7.1]({V7}) (Roman text)
- Pergamene moulded terracotta appliques of ivy leaves and berries (200-100 BC) are listed as Hellenistic architectural/decorative fittings. — see image list

### Inferences
- A Hellenistic site crane for the artist: shear-legs (three poles with a pinned head), a pulley block, a windlass or capstan with push-bars, guy ropes staked down, and a lewis/tongs hung from the lower block (Vitruvius 10.2 plus Coulton by summary). Treadwheel: only Vitruvius' passing mention and Roman reliefs, so avoid or mark it as speculative.
- Because the Wootton essay is Roman-oriented, its tool dimensions (15-25 cm shafts) should be treated as plausible, not Hellenistic-certain; the Greek tooth chisel's Classical-Hellenistic use is attested only generally ('long history around the Aegean').
- A palm (doron) is c. 7.4 cm by standard metrology, so a tetradoron brick is c. 30 cm and a pentadoron c. 37 cm square (my arithmetic; the unit value is not from a fetched source).

### Gaps
- No retrievable source on Greek (as opposed to Roman) scaffolding, mortar-making, set squares, level instruments, or on when the claw chisel and drill were introduced; the 'Greeks pioneered claw tools in the Classical period' claim came from a commercial marble blog and is excluded.
- Lead-clamp pouring and dovetail (swallow-tail) clamp shapes: only Wikipedia/Vitruvius general statements; Oleson's Handbook and Coulton 1974 text not accessed.
- Mallet: the essay's wooden-mallet description is for Egypt/NW Europe; no Greek-specific example found.


## Agriculture, fishing and beekeeping

### Takeaway
Aegean farming was ard-and-hoe cultivation of cereals, vine and olive; Hellenistic-date machine evidence is strongest for hopper-rubber (Olynthus) mills, trapetum olive mills with beam presses (Argilos), and Ptolemaic-Egyptian water-lifting wheels. Wine treading, terraces, island livestock and fishing gear came only from weaker sources.

### Cited Findings
**Ploughing, digging, reaping, threshing**
- Ard (scratch plough) use in Ancient Greece was described by Hesiod (Works and Days, c. 700 BC; earlier than Hellenistic but the ard stayed standard); ards are still found around the Mediterranean. — [Wikipedia: Ard (plough)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ard_(plough))
- Search summary of the Oxford Classical Dictionary entry 'agricultural implements, Greek': the typical plough was an all-wood ard drawn by oxen or donkeys, metal shares were rare, it scratched without inverting the soil so three passes were needed, and a hoe and mallet broke clods; mattocks (usually two-pronged) and hoes dug light rocky soils; cereals were reaped with a curved sickle and vines and olives pruned with an almost identical tool. Hesiod (Op. 427 ff.) describes the plough-tree of holm-oak with share-beam doweled to the pole. — [OCD / Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Classics entry](https://oxfordre.com/classics/display/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.001.0001/acrefore-9780199381135-e-210) (paywalled; read only via search summary)
- Mattock (Greek makella) is the commonest tool in Byzantine manuscripts of Hesiod's Works and Days; its shape was established by the Bronze Age in Asia Minor and Greece. — [Wikipedia: Mattock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mattock) (Byzantine illustrations, flagged late)
- Iron sickle examples exist in the Kerameikos museum collection (Wikipedia caption); sickles were also found in the Delos/Rheneia purification pit, but those are Geometric-period burials (flag: much earlier). — [Wikipedia: Sickle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sickle); image below
- Threshing: sledges with grooved bottoms, trampling by animals or flails; the ancient Greeks used similar methods; Pliny (1st c. AD) lists threshing board, trampling by horses and flails. — [Wikipedia: Threshing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threshing) (Pliny is Roman)

**Grain mills**
- Saddle quern (push-pull handstone) is the oldest type; beehive and Pompeian (hourglass) mills are Roman-era. — [Wikipedia: Quern-stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddle_quern)
- Search summary of Frankel and other papers: the Olynthus mill (hopper-rubber) has a triangular-section hopper in a rectangular upper stone worked by a lever; earliest securely dated examples Athens 425-400 BC and Olynthus; Greek sequence is saddle quern, hopper-rubber, small rotary (Morgantina type), then large hourglass (Pompeian); rotary mills reached the eastern Mediterranean only in the 1st c. AD, and hopper mills stayed in use to Byzantine times. — [Frankel, 'The Olynthus Mill, Its Origin, and Diffusion' (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/274116643_The_Olynthus_Mill_Its_Origin_and_Diffusion_Typology_and_Distribution) (search summary only)
- Vitruvius describes a water mill (wheel with paddles, toothed drum-wheel, vertical gear driving millstones, hopper) as a near-copy of the water wheel. Philo of Byzantium's Pneumatics has the earliest water mill (mid-3rd c. BC). — [Vitruvius 10.5]({V10}) (Roman); [Wikipedia: Philo of Byzantium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo_of_Byzantium)

**Olive and wine**
- The earliest Greek presses come from Olynthos; millstones were used to crush olives; a Hellenistic example at Argilos has a winch added. First-pressing oil is best; oil settles in stone tanks and is stored in terracotta jars and amphorae. — [Cartwright, World History Encyclopedia, 9 Sept 2016](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/947/the-olive-in-the-ancient-mediterranean/)
- Argilos (Hellenistic, after Philip II): a trapetum mill found in place, two semicircular (lens-shaped) stones on wooden beams that rotate to make 'magma' paste; flat circular press beds with grooves; a hot-water room; a pithos cut lengthwise to hold olives; small cistern. — [Argilos excavation page, 2020](https://argilos.net/2020/02/07/the-olive-pressing-installation-on-the-acropolis/)
- Search summary: early beam presses had a beam anchored to a wall plus a stone weight (6th c. BC); the trapetum 'must have been introduced to Greece in Hellenistic times'; six large pithoi (up to 4,000 l oil) at a pressing workshop on Delos. — [Olive Press Museum, Naxos](https://www.olivemuseum.com/olive-mills.php) (low-authority museum site; trapetum date unverified)
- A lever-beam press weight could reach 400 kg (footnote in an article that draws on Jewish sources; weak for Greece). — [Wikipedia: Olive oil extraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_oil_extraction)
- Wine: Rhodes and Kos wine demand 'reached a peak during Hellenistic and Roman times'; Chios, Lesbos, Thasos and Rhodes were the main island regions; Thasos wine travelled well. Treading in a stone or wooden basin (lenos) with spout, then a beam press for the pulp. — [Wines of Greece article](https://winesofgreece.org/articles/historic-vineyards-of-the-aegean-islands/); [MDPI Beverages 8(1):10](https://www.mdpi.com/2306-5710/8/1/10) (search summaries); the treading/lenos detail is from [youblob blueprint page](https://youblob.com/us/blueprints/greek-wine-making) which is a low-authority site; treat as unverified.

**Irrigation and water-lifting (more in the Everyday section)**
- Shadoof: Mesopotamian or Egyptian origin, in Egypt after 2000 BC; Egyptian tomb drawings c. 1250 BC. — [Wikipedia: Shadoof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadoof) (not Greek; Egypt, flag)
- Saqiya (animal-driven compartment wheel): Callixenus of Rhodes mentions one under Ptolemy IV (late 3rd c. BC); a 2nd c. BC Faiyum papyrus mentions an irrigation water wheel; a 2nd c. BC Alexandria fresco shows one; wheels 2-5 m diameter. — [Wikipedia: Sakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sakia) (Ptolemaic Egypt)
- Terraces: Wikipedia has only Levant dates (Hellenistic to Ottoman OSL dates at Ramat Rachel). — [Wikipedia: Terrace (agriculture)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrace_(agriculture))

**Island crops and livestock**
- Thasos produced honey, almonds, walnuts, olives and wine and its people fished and herded sheep and goats. — [Wikipedia: Thasos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thasos) (search summary)

**Fishing**
- Search summary: lines, spears, tridents, fish weirs/traps and nets were used from shore and boats; hooks were bronze on lines of hair or plant fibre; lead sinkers, floats; wicker traps; tuna were taken with fixed nets and iron spears using cliff-top lookouts (thunneia, skopia). — [AKMED, 'Fishing in the Ancient Greek and Roman Worlds'](https://akmed.ku.edu.tr/en/fishing-in-the-ancient-greek-and-roman-worlds/) (403 on fetch; summary only)
- Oppian's Halieutica (177-180 AD, Roman-era Greek poem) is the earliest surviving sea-fishing treatise. — [Wikipedia: Fishing net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fishing_net)
- Trident: three tines increase hit chance; Greek name triaina; Poseidon's tool. — [Wikipedia: Trident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trident)

**Beekeeping**
- Most Greek hives were terracotta; horizontal hives 40-60 cm long with a 28-39 cm mouth, found in Attica; Attic (Hymettus) honey was thought best by Aristotle and Theophrastus; at Isthmia 'Type 2' hives were used in Hellenistic times into the Early Roman period. — [search summary of Jones et al. 1973, Anderson-Stojanovic and Jones 2002 (Hesperia 71), Hatjina, Mavrofridis and Jones eds., 'Bees in Antiquity'](https://www.apiservices.biz/documents/articles-en/beekeeping_in_mediterranean.pdf) (PDF text two-column and garbled; confirmed present: Attica, Isthmia, Delos, Chios hive finds and a 4th c. BC hive from the Mesogeia, Attica)
- Beekeeping discussed at length by Aristotle. — [Wikipedia: Beekeeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beekeeping)

### Inferences
- For a Hellenistic island farm scene: wooden ard with a single stilt and pole, oxen or donkeys; two-pronged mattock and hoe; small curved iron sickle; a stone treading basin and a lever/beam press with a stone weight; a lens-stone trapetum if the estate is wealthy; hand hopper-rubber mills in the house; terracotta hives. All are supported separately above, but nobody retrieved an integrated Hellenistic island farm source.
- Ptolemaic water wheels belong to Egypt; for the Aegean the safer lift devices are the well sweep/bucket and the chain or screw pump, not the saqiya.

### Gaps
- No source on Hellenistic wine-press form (the beam press with a rope-and-windlass is inferred), threshing floors in the Aegean, or terrace construction on Greek islands.
- No sourced list of island livestock and crops beyond Thasos and the wine regions.
- No museum image of a Greek hoe or mattock was found; Kerameikos iron tool photos by Dall'Orto carry an 'Attribution' tag only, so were excluded.
- Fish weirs and hooks: no Hellenistic image found with an acceptable licence; the Sitia image is a stone tool of uncertain date.


## Crafts (pottery, lamps, glass, metalwork, textiles, wood, leather)

### Takeaway
Hellenistic pottery was mass-produced with mould-made relief bowls (Megarian) and painted West Slope and plain black-gloss wares; Rhodian amphorae carry stamps naming an annual eponym and fabricant. Mould-made lamps appear c. 300 BC; Hellenistic glass is core-formed and cast/mosaic. Textiles use the warp-weighted loom, spindle with whorl; wood, metal and leather detail was not retrievable.

### Cited Findings
**Pottery**
- Vase painting declined and pottery became an industry with standardisation; categories include Megarian ware, West Slope, Gnathia, Canosa, Centuripe wares and Tanagra figurines; Megarian bowls are mould-made with relief decoration, 'doubtless in imitation of vases made of precious metals'; West Slope ware (330-300 BC kantharos in the Kerameikos) replaced red-figure in Athens by the end of the 4th c. BC, named from finds on the west slope of the Acropolis. — [Wikipedia: Hellenistic pottery / Hellenistic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_pottery)
- Gnathia vases began in Apulia c. 370/360 BC. — [Wikipedia: Gnathia vases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnathia_vases)
- Three-stage firing (oxidation, reduction, reoxidation) and wheel plus mould use. — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_pottery) (carried over from pass 1; applies to black-gloss technique)
- Met object descriptions: Rhodian amphora handle, circa 180 BC, with a large circular stamp with central rose emblem, surrounding inscription naming the annual official Xenophon and the month Sminthios, and a small square side stamp with letter B (number 2). A Rhodian pointed transport amphora, late 3rd-mid 2nd c. BC. A mould fragment for a Megarian bowl, Asia Minor, c. 165-100 BC. A fragmentary Megarian bowl with inscribed Iliadic figures, 2nd c. BC. — Commons description fields copied from the Met catalogue (see image list)
- Rhodian amphorae carry stamps naming a fabricant and an annual eponymous magistrate; stamps appear from the late 4th c. BC and form the largest group of Greek amphora stamps; symbols include the rose and head of Helios; a month name may appear. — search summary of [Alkac and Tomay, Adalya 26](https://www.academia.edu/112930386/E_Alka%C3%A7_B_Tomay_Amphora_Stamps_of_Hellenistic_and_Roman_Periods_from_Myra_and_the_Harbour_Neighborhood_Andriake_Adalya_26) and [Elaiussa study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/290187586_Hellenistic_Rhodian_Amphorae_and_Amphora_Stamps_Excavated_at_Elaiussa) (not opened; Met description confirms the rose/eponym/month pattern)
- Potter and kiln scenes: Penteskouphia plaques (Corinth; archaic votive plaques, date not given on the Commons page) show potters at a kiln and a wheel (ARCHAIC, flag).

**Lamps**
- First mould-made lamps appear at the start of the 3rd c. BC, competing with wheel-made lamps used since the 7th c. BC; mould-made lamps persist to the 1st c. AD; made in two clay halves pressed into a two-part mould. — search summary of [Getty Ancient Lamps (Bussiere and Lindros Wohl 2017; CC BY 4.0)](https://www.getty.edu/publications/ancientlamps/typological-classification/) and [Split museum catalogue](https://www.academia.edu/59993508/Oil_Lamps_from_the_Graeco_Hellenistic_Collection_of_the_Archaeological_Museum_in_Split)
- Getty typology for Hellenistic: 12 wheel-made types (including Howland 22 B, Broneer I, Howland 40 A 'Cnidian'); mould-made groups: Ephesus lamps (triangular bevelled nozzle, or round nozzle with flat plate around the wick-hole, Howland 49 A, grey fabric with dark glaze imitating bronze), Asia Minor lamps with two side lugs. — [Getty Ancient Lamps, Typological Classification](https://www.getty.edu/publications/ancientlamps/typological-classification/)
- Lamps fuelled with olive oil; oil also for strigil cleaning, perfume and ritual. — [Cartwright](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/947/the-olive-in-the-ancient-mediterranean/)

**Glass**
- Hellenistic glass is soda-lime-silica; production mainly Syro-Palestinian coast, Egypt (Alexandria, founded 332 BC); core-formed opaque vessels; mosaic glass bowls (including 'network' bowls from Canosa) are mould-made; from the early 2nd c. BC monochrome hemispherical and sub-conical bowls; gold-sandwich glass and gold-band alabastra are late Hellenistic. — [Wikipedia: Hellenistic glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_glass)

**Metalwork**
- Hellenistic metalwork and luxury arts overshadowed pottery; the Walters footless cup type originated in the Near East and became popular in Macedonia after Alexander's conquests (Walters caption). — [Wikipedia: Hellenistic pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_pottery); Commons description of the Walters cup.

**Textiles**
- Warp-weighted loom: warp bundles tied to weights keep threads taut; used near-vertical; two weavers shown working side by side on Greek vase paintings; Bronze Age loom weights at Miletos; weight sets can give fabric density. — [Wikipedia: Warp-weighted loom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warp-weighted_loom)

## Everyday objects and Hellenistic inventions (furniture, vessels, writing, weights, clocks, water-lifting, machines)

### Takeaway
Dated mechanisms: Ctesibius' force pump and water clock (c. 285-222 BC), Archimedes' screw (first mentioned between 250 and 220 BC), Philo's water mill (c. 250 BC) and chain pump, the Antikythera mechanism (2nd-1st c. BC), and the Tower of the Winds (c. 50 BC). Furniture is best seen in Hellenistic funerary banquet reliefs (kline with three-legged table and chairs). Hero is Roman-era.

### Cited Findings
**Inventors and dates**
- Ctesibius of Alexandria, born c. 290 BC, active c. 285-222 BC: force pump, improved water clock, hydraulis, air catapult. — [Wikipedia: Ctesibius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ctesibius); [EBSCO research starter](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/ctesibius-alexandria)
- Vitruvius: Ctesibius' machine has two brass cylinders with pistons worked by levers, forked pipes meeting in a basin with valves, an inverted-funnel cover and a vertical 'trumpet' pipe; it lifts water to fountains. Ctesibius discovered air pressure from a counterweight mirror cord in his barber father's shop, and made water clocks with a gold or gem orifice, an inverted float bowl (phellos) and toothed wheels. — [Vitruvius 10.7 and 9.8]({V10}); [{V9}]({V9})
- Vitruvius' list of dial inventors: Berosus (hollowed semicircle), Aristarchus of Samos (scaphe, bowl), Eudoxus or Apollonius (arachne), Scopas (plinthium), Parmenion, Theodosius and Andrias, Patrocles (pelicinon), Dionysodorus (cone), Apollonius (quiver). — [Vitruvius 9.8.1]({V9}) (Roman source for Greek inventors)
- Archimedes' screw: first mentioned 250-220 BC (Moschion, bilge pump of Hiero II's ship); Vitruvius describes a wooden screw with eight channels made with pitched willow slips on a round beam. — [Wikipedia: Archimedes' screw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_screw); [Vitruvius 10.6]({V10}) (Roman)
- Vitruvius' water machines: tympanum (drum wheel with eight radial partitions), treadwheel with pitched-and-waxed buckets, chain of bronze buckets over a wheel, river wheels with paddles. — [Vitruvius 10.4-5]({V10})
- Philo of Byzantium (c. 280-220 BC): earliest water mill (mid-3rd c. BC), chain drive in a repeating crossbow, gimbal ink pot, chain pump. Hero of Alexandria is 1st c. AD (c. 10-70 AD) and is Roman-era. — [Wikipedia: Philo of Byzantium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo_of_Byzantium); [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_technology); Hero dates from [search summary of Wikipedia/AQUA project](https://aqua.ciuhct.org/hydraulic-treatises/pneumatica-of-hero-of-alexandria)
- Antikythera mechanism: bronze gears in a wooden case, found 1901; dated variously 205 BC, 150-100 BC, c. 87 BC; shipwreck c. 70-60 BC. — [Wikipedia: Antikythera mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antikythera_mechanism) and pass 1; date range via [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Antikythera_Mechanism/) (search summary)
- Tower of the Winds, Athens: c. 50 BC (Andronikos of Kyrrhos), water clock inside, sundials outside, Triton weathervane. — [Wikipedia: Tower of the Winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_the_Winds) (pass 1)

**Furniture and domestic objects (from the images, my visual reading)**
- Viewed images (not text sources): the Dionysios stele (Cyzicus, 2nd half 2nd c. BC) and the Menophanes relief (Berlin Ant Sk 834) each show diners reclining on a kline against bolsters, a low three-legged table loaded with loaves or food, a small servant in front, and seated women on chairs with backs. A second Berlin relief (Ant Sk 1462) shows a bearded man on a backed chair with a footstool and a Y-shaped object (tree or standard) above. Cleveland 2005.52 (Pamphylia, c. 50 BC): the deceased on an 'elaborate chair', servant with a fan, a hen on an upturned basket. — Commons file pages listed below
- Furniture text: klinai were rectangular on four legs, wood with bronze legs cast in animal styles; tripod tables with clawed legs; folding bronze tables; inlay of ivory, ebony, gold. — [search summary of Ancient Greek furniture pages, e.g. Walls with Stories](https://www.wallswithstories.com/ancient/ancient-greek-furniture.html) (low-authority; Roman-era examples mixed in)
- Klismos: appears mid 5th c. BC and lost favour in the Hellenistic period. — [Wikipedia: Klismos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klismos) (pass 1)
- Hadra hydriai: Hellenistic Alexandrian water jars used as cinerary urns (Met examples dated 227-213 BC). — Commons descriptions of Met objects (see images)

**Writing**
- Papyrus: made from the pith of Cyperus papyrus, sheets pasted side by side and rolled; 3rd c. BCE letter shown; the Derveni papyrus (Macedonia; Commons gives c. 340 BC, charred roll fragments) shows the roll format. — [Wikipedia: Papyrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus); Commons file page below
- Stylus scribes wax tablets; Met bronze stylus (Cypriot) 4th-1st c. BC. — [Wikipedia: Stylus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stylus); Commons Met file below
- Ostraka (potsherds) were cheap writing surfaces for receipts, notes and lessons; Greek ostraca voted in 5th c. BC Athens; the Oxyrhynchus example on Commons is 300-400 AD (late Roman, flag). — [Wikipedia: Ostracon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ostracon)

**Weights, scales, clocks**
- Steelyard in use among Greek craftsmen in the 5th and 4th c. BC; Roman and Chinese steelyards independently c. 200 BC; Met steelyards on Commons are Roman (2nd-6th c. AD). — [Wikipedia: Steelyard balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steelyard_balance)
- Athens Agora museum: official bronze weights c. 500 BC and commercial lead weights, 5th-2nd c. BC. — Commons description (below)
- Sundials: hollowed hemicycle, scaphe, hanging portable dials (Vitruvius); Hellenistic sundial dedicated to Dionysus at Claros (photo). — [Vitruvius 9.8]({V9})
