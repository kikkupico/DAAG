Taking Stock Without Stopping

How do you take an accurate snapshot of an entire distributed system while it keeps running, when no single machine can see the whole network at once? And what can such a snapshot reliably tell you?

To find out, we follow three ancient Greek trading houses that suspect bandits are robbing their carts on the coastal road—and must count every jar they own, including those in transit, without halting trade.

After K. Mani Chandy and Leslie Lamport, Distributed Snapshots: Determining Global States of Distributed Systems, 1985

First, in machine terms

Recording a system that is still running

In a distributed system, many computers pass messages to one another across a network. Answering basic questions about the whole system—like whether it has deadlocked, finished a job, or lost data—requires knowing its global state: the internal state of every machine plus all messages currently in flight across the network.

Because there is no shared clock, no machine can photograph the entire network at a single instant. If you simply inspect the machines one after another while the system is running, anything that moves between two inspections gets double-counted or missed entirely—as you can see in the interactive on the right. Freezing the whole system and waiting for the network pipes to drain is too disruptive, and even knowing when a pipe is empty already requires sending a special end-of-stream marker. In 1985, K. Mani Chandy and Leslie Lamport showed how to capture an accurate global snapshot without ever stopping the system:

  • The marker algorithm (Chandy and Lamport, 1985): When a machine records its own local state, it immediately sends a special marker message down every outgoing channel. Each machine then records the messages arriving on each incoming channel until that channel's marker arrives.
  • What the snapshot means: Because machines record at slightly different times, the combined snapshot might describe a global state the system never actually inhabited at any single wall-clock instant. Even so, it is guaranteed to be a state the system could have passed through—which is enough to reliably detect any stable property (a condition that, once true, stays true forever, like deadlock or data loss).

We explore how this works through the story of three trading houses on the island of Arche and their loaded carts along the coastal road.

The ring road of Arche

Three houses, carts on one cliff road

House 1, House 2, and House 3 sit along the coast of Arche, separated by a steep mountain. Carts of jars and messengers travel from house to house along the coastal cliff road—and bandits have been spotted on the island.

No overtakingNothing may pass another on a road track, so carts and messengers always reach the far gate in the exact order they set out.*
One track each wayEach house has a direct one-way track to each of the other two houses, making six directed road tracks in all.
Bandits take carts, not messengersBandits may ambush a loaded cart on the road. A messenger carrying no goods is not worth a bandit's trouble, so every messenger arrives safely.
The houses' question

How much do we hold between us?

During a busy winter of trade, rumors spread that bandits were seizing carts along the cliff road. Whenever a cart left one house and took a long time to reach the next, nobody could tell whether it had been stolen by bandits or was merely delayed behind a slow mule.

To find out whether they have actually been robbed, the three houses want to take a complete inventory of every jar of oil and grain they hold across the island, and compare that count against what their account scrolls say they ought to own. Any shortfall will prove what the bandits took.

The catch is that the houses' jars sit in two different kinds of places: some rest inside the three storehouses, while the rest are out on the road, strapped to carts and pack-mules in transit.*

The scribes' first idea is the obvious one: send an auditor to each of the three storehouses, count the jars inside each one, and add the three numbers together. The recorder of the customs rolls watched the carts moving along the cliff road and immediately saw why that plan fails:

Morning one

Counted twice

Suppose House 3's auditor counts at dawn and House 2's counts in the afternoon. In between, a cart carries 200 jars of oil from House 3 to House 2. House 3 counted those jars before they left, and House 2 counts them a second time after they arrive—making the total 200 jars too high!

Morning two

Counted by nobody

Suppose the auditors count at the same hours as before, but a cart of 200 jars leaves House 2 in the morning and hasn't reached House 3 by dawn. Those jars left House 2 before its auditor looked, and arrived at House 3 after its auditor finished—so 200 jars vanish from the count even though no bandit touched them!

The root cause

Wrong by whatever moved

Counting the storehouses one after another is always off by the net goods that travelled between an already-counted house and an uncounted one—and without a shared clock, nobody can tell how much moved in between.

In both mornings, the auditors counted the storehouses accurately, but missed or double-counted the goods moving along the road. The most obvious way to keep moving carts from spoiling the count is simply to halt all road traffic while the auditors work.

The first thought

Why not stop the road?

Suppose the houses agree to freeze all trade: stop sending carts, wait until every cart already on the road has rolled into a gate, count the three storehouses while the roads sit empty, and then resume business.

Thinking through how to actually carry out that freeze reveals two problems—and a surprising shortcut that eliminates the need to stop at all:

First problem

Halting trade is expensive

The three houses supply the island's port towns every day. Freezing all shipments until the longest stretch of road empties out brings the island's commerce to a standstill.

Second problem

The stop order travels by road

Without a shared clock, the order to stop must travel by messenger. Each house stops sending new carts when the messenger arrives, keeps receiving carts still on the road, and counts once its incoming tracks are empty.

The hidden catch

How do you know a road is empty?

Standing at a gate, a clerk cannot see around the sea cliffs to tell whether another cart is still lumbering along the road. To prove a track is empty, each house must send a final messenger the moment it stops—meaning "no more carts behind me."

Look closely at that third card. Even if the houses stopped all trade, they would still need to send a special messenger down every track to mark the end of the carts sent before the freeze! The recorder of the customs rolls realized what that meant: if a messenger can mark the boundary between pre-count carts and post-count carts on a narrow road where nobody overtakes, the houses don't need to stop sending carts behind that messenger at all.

House 1's accountant added a second insight. Freezing the road was an attempt to capture the island at a single simultaneous instant. In truth, the houses don't need a simultaneous instant; they only need a sound (consistent) inventory where no jar is counted twice and no jar slips through the cracks.

To build that sound inventory while the carts keep rolling, we first have to be crystal clear about all the places on Arche that can hold jars.

What is being counted

Nine places, not three

Every house naturally thinks of its inventory as the jars sitting under its own roof. But jars strapped to a cart out on the cliff road belong to the island's stock just as surely as jars in a cellar—and ignoring the road is precisely why the auditors' first attempt went wrong.

A complete snapshot of Arche therefore consists of nine numbers: the stock inside each of the three storehouses, plus the jars currently travelling along each of the six one-way road tracks connecting pairs of houses.* Click each of the nine places on the map below.

Counting a storehouse is easy—a clerk can tally the shelves in a few minutes. But how can a clerk standing at a house's gate count the jars on a winding cliff road they cannot see?

The recorder of the customs rolls saw the answer: you don't have to look at the road to count what is on it, because every cart on a road track eventually rolls through the receiving house's gate. A receiving house simply needs to record on a wax slate every cart that arrives along that track after the house counted its own storehouse, and stop recording the moment the last cart sent before the sender's count has arrived.

And to tell the receiving gate the exact moment that last pre-count cart has arrived, the houses use the messenger in the red sash.

Chandy and Lamport's marker algorithm

The red sash

The signal that divides pre-count carts from post-count carts is a messenger wearing a bright red sash (a marker). Because no one can overtake on the narrow road, when a red-sashed messenger walks through a gate, every cart sent down that track before the sash has already arrived.

The messenger carries no jars, no orders, and no timestamps. Their sole job is to mark the boundary in a road track's stream of traffic.

The entire snapshot procedure comes down to one rule for sending a red sash and two cases for receiving one:

The rule of sending

Count your storehouse, then send sashes

When a house counts its own storehouse, it immediately dispatches one messenger in a red sash down each of its two outgoing tracks—before sending any further carts.

Receiving · Case 1

First sash seen (not yet counted)

If a red sash arrives at a house that has not yet counted its own storehouse, the house immediately counts its storehouse, records that this incoming track held 0 jars, and sends red sashes down its outgoing tracks.

Receiving · Case 2

Later sash seen (already counted)

If a red sash arrives at a house that has already counted its storehouse, the house closes its wax slate for that incoming track: the jars that arrived on that track between the house's own count and this sash are that track's recorded holding.

Any house can initiate the count without coordinating beforehand, and no cart or mule ever has to stop. Scroll through the eleven steps below to watch a full count unfold while carts keep moving across the island.

A cart behind the sash

Right after sending its sashes, House 1 dispatches a cart of 20 jars to House 2. House 1's current stock drops to 80, but its recorded snapshot remains 100—and because nobody overtakes on the road, this cart travels behind the red sash.

5 · Receiving (Case 1)

House 2 receives its first sash

First the 30-jar cart from House 3 rolls into House 2, raising House 2's stock to 100. Moments later, the red sash from House 1 arrives. Since House 2 hasn't counted yet, it records its storehouse at 100 (including those 30 jars), records 0 for the track from House 1, opens a slate for the track from House 3, and sends out its own red sashes.

6 · Behind the sash

The 20-jar cart arrives

Now House 1's 20-jar cart reaches House 2. Because House 2 already closed the slate for the track from House 1 when the sash arrived, it does not add these 20 jars to the snapshot—avoiding a double-count, since House 1 already included them in its 100!

7 · On the open slate

A cart caught in transit

Next, the 15-jar cart from House 3 rolls into House 2. Because House 2 has already counted its storehouse and has not yet seen a red sash from House 3, it logs these 15 jars on its open slate for the track from House 3.

8 · Receiving (Case 1)

House 3 receives its first sash

House 1's other red sash reaches House 3. House 3 immediately records its storehouse at 45 (which excludes the 15 jars it sent earlier), records 0 for the track from House 1, opens a slate for the track from House 2, and sends out its own red sashes.

9 · On the open slate

Another cart caught in transit

The 10-jar cart from House 2 rolls into House 3. Because House 3 has already counted its storehouse and is still waiting for House 2's red sash, it logs these 10 jars on its slate for the track from House 2.

10 · Receiving (Case 2)

Every remaining sash arrives

The remaining red sashes reach their destinations, closing the open slates at all three houses. Every storehouse has been counted and every road slate is now closed.

11 · The total

Nine numbers, added together

Adding the three recorded storehouses and six road slates gives jars—matching the exact jars on the island when the morning began! Not a single jar was counted twice, and not a single jar in transit was missed.

Notice how the wax slates and red sashes work together. Once a house counts its storehouse, it starts logging every cart arriving on an incoming track until that track's red sash walks through the gate. Those logged carts are precisely the ones that left the neighboring house before the neighbor counted its storehouse, and arrived after this house counted its own—in other words, the exact carts that were in transit across the boundary of the count!

This guarantee relies on two physical facts about the ring road:* no overtaking on any track (FIFO delivery, so a sash cleanly separates pre-count carts from post-count carts), and every red sash eventually arrives. What if bandits hijack a loaded cart during the count? That does not break the algorithm: a stolen cart vanishes from the island's stock just like goods sold away, whereas a red-sashed messenger carries no loot and always gets through to close the slate.

Because each house records its storehouse whenever the first sash reaches it, the three houses take their counts at different hours. Why can we be certain that combining counts from different hours never reproduces the auditors' double-counting mistake?

Consistent global states · Cuts

Cuts that hold and cuts that do not

To see why the nine numbers always fit together, we can draw the morning on a circular timeline: each house is a line radiating outward as time passes, and each cart is a curved arrow from the moment it leaves one house to the moment it reaches the next.

Joining the three dots where the three houses recorded their storehouses forms a dashed loop called a cut. Everything inside the loop happened before the local count at that house; everything outside the loop happened after.

A cart that starts inside the loop and lands outside it left before its sender counted and arrived after its receiver counted—which is completely natural and is caught on the receiver's road slate. The only thing that can ruin a cut is an inward-crossing cart: a cart sent after the sender counted its storehouse that somehow arrives before the receiver counts its own! Such a cart would include an effect inside the cut while leaving its cause outside, double-counting those jars in both storehouses.

Each line tracks one house moving outward over time. The dashed loop (the cut) connects the moments when each house recorded its storehouse, and each numbered arc is a cart.

Why can no cart ever cross the cut inward when the houses use red sashes? Because the moment a house counts its storehouse, it sends a red sash down every outgoing track before sending any post-count cart. Since no cart can overtake the sash on the narrow road, the red sash always reaches the neighboring house first—forcing that neighbor to count its storehouse before the post-count cart can arrive!

So the nine numbers form a completely consistent cut with no double-counting and no missing carts. Yet because the three houses counted at different times, what moment of the morning does this snapshot actually describe?

Proof · no message ever crosses the snapshot cut inward

Marker-sending rule: a process records its local state and immediately sends a marker on every outgoing channel before sending any further messages. Marker-receiving rule: upon receiving a marker, a process that has not yet recorded its state does so immediately; a process that has already recorded closes that incoming channel's state. Channels are FIFO and every marker eventually arrives. Claim. No message sent after its sender recorded is ever received before its receiver recorded.

  1. Call an event at a house early (inside the cut) if it occurred before that house recorded its storehouse, and late (outside the cut) if it occurred after.
  2. Suppose for contradiction that some cart crosses the cut inward: it is dispatched late at House i and arrives early at House j.
  3. Because the cart was dispatched late, House i had already recorded its storehouse. By the marker-sending rule, House i sent a red sash down the track to House j before dispatching that cart.
  4. Because the track from House i to House j is FIFO, the red sash must arrive at House j before the cart does.
  5. By the marker-receiving rule, House j records its storehouse no later than the arrival of that red sash.
  6. Therefore, House j had already recorded its storehouse before the cart arrived—meaning the cart's arrival was late, not early. This contradiction proves that no cart can cross the cut inward.∎
What the snapshot represents

A morning nobody lived through

Remarkably, the nine numbers may describe a state of the island that never existed at any single moment in real time!

To see how, suppose House 1 records its storehouse first, and House 2 records a little while later. Right after House 1 records, it sells 40 jars of oil into its own port town. A few minutes later—still before House 2 has recorded—House 2 mills 80 measures of barley into flour. Neither local action touches a road, a cart, or a sash.

Look at what the combined snapshot records: it captures House 1 before the oil sale (still holding the 40 jars), and House 2 after the milling (with the barley already turned into flour). Yet in real time under the sun, the island was never in that combined state: while House 1 still held the 40 jars, the barley had not yet been milled; by the time the barley was milled, the 40 jars were already gone!

Why is the snapshot still trustworthy if it describes a state the island never actually passed through? To state the guarantee precisely, call the island's true state when the first sash went out the start (Sbegin), the nine-number snapshot the recorded island (S*), and the island's true state when the last slate closed the end (Send).

Chandy and Lamport proved two things: first, the recorded island can be reached from the start simply by running the pre-cut events in a causally valid order; second, the end can be reached from the recorded island by running the remaining post-cut events.

In other words, the recorded island lies on a completely valid execution path from the start of the count to the end! Because the oil sale at House 1 and the barley milling at House 2 occurred on opposite sides of the mountain with no messages between them, they were concurrent (unrelated)*—so swapping their order changes nothing about any house's final state or any chain of cause and effect.

Now that we know the snapshot captures a valid state the island could have passed through between the start and the end of the count, what questions can such a snapshot reliably answer?

Proof · the recorded snapshot lies on a valid execution path

Let Sbegin be the global state when the snapshot began, S* the recorded snapshot state, and Send the global state when the final channel record closed. Theorem. There exists a causally valid permutation of the actual events between Sbegin and Send that passes directly through S*: Sbegin →* S* →* Send.

  1. List the actual sequence of events that occurred on the island between Sbegin and Send: e₁, e₂, …, em. Classify each event as early (occurring at a house before that house recorded its state) or late (occurring after that house recorded its state).
  2. In the real-time execution, some late event at one house may occur before an early event at another house. Suppose a late event ei is immediately followed by an early event ei+1.
  3. Claim: ei and ei+1 can be swapped without changing any process or channel state. First, they cannot belong to the same house, because at any single house all early events precede the recording and all late events follow it. Second, ei cannot have caused ei+1, because that would require a message sent after recording (late) to arrive before recording (early), which our cut-consistency proof ruled out. Thus ei and ei+1 are independent events at different houses and commute.
  4. Repeatedly swap adjacent (late, early) pairs until all early events precede all late events.
  5. Executing all early events starting from Sbegin brings every house to the exact state it had when it recorded, and leaves on each channel the exact messages sent early and received late—which is precisely the recorded snapshot S*. Executing the remaining late events from S* then reaches Send.∎

Detecting stable properties

What such a total is good for

Because a snapshot describes a morning nobody actually lived through, it cannot answer questions about fleeting moments—such as whether House 2 held 300 jars at the exact instant a ship dropped anchor. Without synchronized clocks, no distributed count can speak for a single wall-clock instant.

Instead, the snapshot is built to answer questions about settled properties (stable properties): conditions that, once they become true, stay true forever. If we look only at the closed network of trade between the three houses (setting aside outside port-town sales), classic examples include:

  • Everything delivered (Termination): Every cart has arrived and all three houses have finished their work.
  • Standstill (Deadlock): Every house is waiting for a cart from another house before it can send one of its own.
  • Exhaustion of supply: A vintage has run out across the island, and no trade between the houses can create more.
  • Deficit (Data loss / theft): Jars have disappeared from the closed trading loop—as when bandits seize a cart on the road.

For any settled property, the recorded snapshot gives an ironclad answer about the real island: if the property holds in the snapshot, it definitely holds on the real island by the time the count finishes (since the end state is reachable from the snapshot, and once true, a settled property stays true). Conversely, if the property does not hold in the snapshot, it definitely did not hold when the count began (since the snapshot is reachable from the start state).

That directly solves the houses' bandit problem! By running a red-sash count on a day with no port-town buying or selling, normal shipments merely move jars from one of the nine places to another without changing the sum. The decision rule for stable properties, in the paper's terms

A global predicate y is stable if for every global state S where y(S) is true, y(S') remains true for all states S' reachable from S. Let Sbegin →* S* →* Send be as in the reachability theorem. Decision rule. Evaluate the stable predicate y on the recorded snapshot S*:

  1. If y(S*) is true: Since Send is reachable from S* and y is stable, y(Send) must be true. The property definitely holds in the real system when the snapshot completes.
  2. If y(S*) is false: Since S* is reachable from Sbegin, if y(Sbegin) had been true, stability would force y(S*) to be true as well. Thus y(Sbegin) must be false: the property definitely did not hold when the snapshot began.∎

Examples of stable properties include distributed termination, deadlock, loss of tokens or conserved quantities (deficit), and unreachability of objects (garbage collection).

A running distributed system can record a consistent global snapshot without ever pausing its work—capturing a state it may never have inhabited at one instant, but could have passed through.

Because that recorded snapshot lies on a valid causal path from the start of the count to the end, it reliably settles every question whose answer, once true, stays true.

The Chandy–Lamport snapshot algorithm remains the foundation for how distributed databases and stream processors—such as Apache Flink—take live checkpoints today.

Setting The Ring Road

The system, stated formally

The system consists of a finite set of processes (the houses) connected by directed channels (the six one-way tracks of the ring road). Each channel delivers messages in the exact order sent (FIFO), without corruption, after an arbitrary finite delay, and never loses a marker. There is no shared global clock, and no process can directly inspect another process's memory or a channel's in-flight messages. A global state consists of the local state of every process together with the sequence of messages currently in transit on every channel.

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. K. Mani Chandy and Leslie Lamport, “Distributed Snapshots: Determining Global States of Distributed Systems,” ACM Transactions on Computer Systems 3, 1 (February 1985), 63–75. Covers the formal definition of global state, the marker-sending and marker-receiving rules, the reachability theorem, and stable property detection.

The ideas. In a distributed system, no single observer can view the state of all processes and channels at the same instant. Freezing the system and waiting for all channels to drain is expensive—and knowing when a channel has drained already requires sending an end-of-stream marker. Chandy and Lamport showed how those same markers can capture a consistent global snapshot while normal computation continues without interruption.

Whenever a process records its local state, it immediately sends a marker down every outgoing channel before sending any further application messages. Because channels are FIFO, markers cleanly partition each channel's message stream into pre-snapshot and post-snapshot messages. A receiving process records the messages arriving on each incoming channel between its own state recording and the arrival of that channel's marker, guaranteeing that no message crosses the snapshot cut inward (sent after the sender recorded, yet received before the receiver recorded).

Although the recorded snapshot S* may never have occurred at any single instant of physical time, it is causally consistent: reachable from the state Sbegin when the snapshot started, and able to reach the state Send when the snapshot finished. That makes S* sufficient to detect any stable property—such as deadlock, termination, garbage collection, or token loss—whose truth is preserved under all future steps.
