The Limits of Agreement

Can a group of computers always reach an agreement when messages can take any amount of time and even one machine might silently stop?

To find out, we follow four mercenaries camped at the foot of a hill. Out of sight of one another, they must agree whether to attack or hold their ground—knowing that at any moment, one of them might already be dead.

After Michael J. Fischer, Nancy A. Lynch and Michael S. Paterson, Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process, 1985

First, in machine terms

Many computers, one decision

Imagine a group of computers across a network that must all agree on a single choice—say, commit or abort—based on the initial values each machine started with.

Two obstacles stand in their way. First, the network is asynchronous: every message eventually arrives, but there is no upper limit on how long it might take. Second, one machine might crash at any moment without warning. Because messages can be arbitrarily slow, a computer waiting for a reply faces an impossible dilemma: has the other machine crashed, or is its message simply delayed?

  • No guaranteed decision (Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson, 1985): No deterministic protocol—one that acts strictly on the messages it has received without randomness—can guarantee that the group will ever reach a decision.
  • Only the ending is lost. The machines are never forced to disagree with one another. What cannot be promised is that the waiting will ever end.

To see why this happens step by step, we tell the story through four mercenaries, their messenger ravens, and a hill they must either storm together or not at all.

The foot of Mount Phyle

Four tents, out of sight of one another

Four mercenaries are camped around the base of Mount Phyle, one to a tent, hidden from one another by trees and rocky gullies and answering to no commander.* Bandits hold the fortress at the summit, and before dawn the four have one question to settle together.

Only ravensThe four send word to one another only by messenger raven. Every raven eventually arrives, but there is no limit to how long a bird might take on the way.
Nobody liesEvery mercenary reports honestly and follows the plan faithfully, and every raven delivers exactly what was written.
Any man may dieAt most one of the four may be killed during the night. A dead man's tent simply falls silent: nobody takes his place, and no bird flies out to report his death.
One question, two answers

Attack, or hold

The four mercenaries must decide whether to attack the hill or to hold their ground—and they must all make the exact same choice. If they attack, all of them climb together to overpower the bandits. If they hold, each man blocks the path down his own gully to keep the bandits trapped on top. Splitting up is worse than either choice: if half climb while half hold, there are too few men to take the summit, and the gullies are left wide open.

At dusk, each mercenary scouted his own quarter of the slope and formed an initial opinion: attack or hold. A mercenary commits the moment he leaves his tent—either to climb with the others or to guard his gully—and once he leaves, he will not return before dawn. Whatever plan they follow must satisfy three demands:

First demand · Agreement

No two commit differently

Nobody attacks while another holds. Every man who leaves his tent makes the same choice.

Second demand · Validity

Both answers stay possible

Their choice must reflect what they saw at dusk: if all saw attack, they attack; if all saw hold, they hold.

Third demand · Termination

Somebody eventually commits

They cannot wait forever. Sooner or later, at least one mercenary leaves his tent.

Why are all three demands necessary? The second demand rules out the trivial plan of agreeing in advance to "always hold" no matter what anyone saw at dusk—which guarantees agreement, but makes scouting the hill pointless. The third demand rules out the plan where nobody ever leaves his tent, which satisfies the first two demands simply by doing nothing. One final rule binds the four: they may not flip coins or draw lots. Each man's actions must be completely deterministic, driven strictly by his own dusk sighting and the ravens that have arrived at his tent.

Waiting for every tent's report satisfies all three demands whenever all four men are alive to answer. Because nobody lies, every tent collects the exact same four reports and reaches the same decision. Its fatal flaw appears only when a tent stays silent—and the reason lies in the ravens.

The heart of the problem

A dead man, or a slow bird?

The ravens can be trusted in every way but one: every bird eventually arrives and delivers its note intact, but there is no limit to how long its flight may take.

A raven might fly straight across the gullies in minutes, or it might roost in a pine tree for hours. Because no flight delay is too long to be possible, there is no hour of the night after which a mercenary can look at an empty perch and know that nothing more is on the way.

Now combine slow birds with the danger that one of the four men may be killed, leaving his tent completely silent. A mercenary waiting on a tent that has not answered is caught between two possibilities that look identical from the outside. Wait at Tent 2's perch below, and see whether the passing hours ever tell them apart.

From Tent 2's point of view, both nights look like the exact same thing: an empty perch. Waiting another hour never distinguishes a dead comrade from a slow raven. A valid plan therefore cannot wait for all four reports—because whenever one man truly dies, the others would wait forever and violate the third demand. Any working plan must allow the mercenaries to commit after hearing from at most three tents.

That single requirement—that the camp must be able to settle the decision while ignoring one silent tent—is the lever that undoes every possible plan. To see why, we need a clear way to track how the night unfolds step by step.

Three key concepts

When is the matter settled?

Pinpointing the exact moment a night's outcome is locked in requires three simple terms to describe the state of the camp.

A position

A snapshot of the whole camp

A position (or configuration) is the complete state of the night at a given moment: what each of the four men currently holds in mind, plus every raven currently in flight.

An arrival

One man receives one raven

The night advances one step at a time through arrivals (or events): one man takes in a raven from his perch, updates his state, and perhaps sends out new ravens. (Checking an empty perch changes nothing.)

Open or closed

Does flight order still matter?

A position is open (bivalent) if the night can still end in either holding or attacking depending on which birds land first. It is closed (univalent) once only one outcome remains possible.

Notice that "open" does not describe what any single mercenary is thinking; it describes the whole camp from above. As long as a position is open, the order in which ravens land still holds the power to tip the outcome either way. Once a position is closed, the camp's fate is sealed—even if no mercenary has actually stepped out of his tent yet.

Our first building block is a simple rule about the order of arrivals. Below, Tents 1 and 2 have ravens to exchange on one side of the hill, while Tents 3 and 4 have ravens to exchange on the other. Two sequences of arrivals that involve completely separate mercenaries do not interfere with each other (they commute): run either sequence first, and the camp reaches the exact same position. That is because an arrival only changes the memory of the one man receiving the bird and the pool of birds in the air—nothing that touches the other tents.

With these terms in hand, we can ask our first sharp question. At dusk, every man has his initial sighting and no bird has flown yet. Could a clever plan settle the outcome right at dusk, so that the night begins already closed by the sightings alone?

Proof · Courses with no man in common commute (Lemma 1)

In the paper's terms, a position is a configuration, an arrival is an event, a course is a schedule, a fair course is an admissible run, and open and closed are bivalent and univalent. Lemma 1. Let courses σ₁ and σ₂ both be applicable at a position, and let the set A of men who act in σ₁ and the set B of men who act in σ₂ be disjoint. Then each course can be run after the other, and both orders reach the exact same position.

  1. A position consists of what is in each man's head together with the ravens aloft. An arrival changes only two parts of it: the single man who takes the raven in, and the air (one raven removed, and perhaps new ones added).
  2. Let σ₁ involve a set of men A and σ₂ a disjoint set B. Running σ₁ alters only the men in A, whose states σ₂ never reads; and vice versa.
  3. Neither course disables the other through the air: every raven is addressed to a specific tent, so the ravens consumed by σ₂ are addressed to men in B, whereas σ₁ removes only ravens addressed to men in A. Both orders are therefore valid.
  4. In either order, the men of A experience the exact same arrivals of σ₁ and the men of B experience the exact same arrivals of σ₂, each in its own order, and the air has had the identical ravens removed and added. Thus, the resulting positions are identical.∎
Step 1 · The night can begin open

The dusk that settles nothing

No matter what plan the mercenaries use, the sightings at dusk cannot always lock in the outcome beforehand. To see why, line up five possible dusk beginnings from all-hold (HHHH) on the left to all-attack (AAAA) on the right, flipping just one man's sighting at each step along the row.

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that every beginning in this row were already closed—predetermined by the sightings alone. The second demand (Validity) forces all-hold to end in holding and all-attack to end in attacking. The three middle beginnings are yours to assign. A row of beginnings click the middle three

No matter how you assign the middle three, two adjacent beginnings in the row must always split—one fated for Hold and the next fated for Attack—even though they differ in only a single mercenary's sighting. Now suppose that single mercenary falls silent (or his ravens are simply very slow), while the other three run the plan to a decision. Because the other three saw the exact same dusk sightings and hear the exact same messages in both beginnings, they cannot tell the two beginnings apart and must reach the same decision in both! That contradicts the claim that one beginning guaranteed Hold and the other guaranteed Attack. Therefore, under any plan, at least one combination of dusk sightings leaves the night open from the very start.

So the night can begin open. Even so, it still feels as though the night cannot stay open forever—surely some raven's arrival, sooner or later, must be the decisive step that tips the balance and closes it.

Proof · Some beginning is open (Lemma 2)

A beginning is an initial configuration: the four mercenaries' dusk sightings, with no raven yet aloft. Lemma 2. Whatever plan the four have arranged, at least one beginning is open (bivalent).

  1. Suppose, for contradiction, that every possible beginning were closed—each already fated for either Hold or Attack by the dusk sightings alone.
  2. Both fates must occur: Validity (the second demand) forces holding when all four saw hold, and attacking when all four saw attack.
  3. Call two beginnings neighbours when they differ in only one man's sighting. Stepping from all-hold to all-attack one sighting at a time, there must be two adjacent neighbours B₀ and B₁—differing only in the sighting of one man p—that are fated for opposite decisions (say, B₀ for hold and B₁ for attack).
  4. Take a fair course starting from B₀ in which man p never takes a step. This is permitted because the plan must still reach a decision when one man dies; thus, the remaining three eventually commit to holding.
  5. Now run that exact same course starting from B₁. Since p never acts and p's sighting is the only difference between B₀ and B₁, the other three men see the exact same sightings and receive the exact same arrivals in the same order, so they must commit to holding here too.
  6. But B₁ was supposedly fated to attack, and a closed beginning admits no course to the opposite answer. This contradiction proves that some beginning must be open.∎
Step 2 · No arrival has to close it

The raven that would settle it can always be held back

Suppose the night is currently open, and a raven is about to land that would close the night and lock in the decision. Remarkably, by holding that raven back for a while and letting other ravens land first, the camp can reach a position where that same raven finally lands and yet the night remains open.

The raven is never lost—because the birds are reliable, it must eventually land. We only ask whether its landing must settle the outcome. Scroll slowly through the seven steps below to follow the argument.

1 · An open night

One raven is about to land

The night currently stands open: it can still end in either Hold or Attack. One specific raven is in the air, ready to be taken in. Because the birds are reliable, it must eventually land. The question is whether we can time its landing so that the night stays open afterward.

2 · Hold it back

Other arrivals can come first

Imagine holding that one raven back in the air while other arrivals happen first. The top row shows the positions the night can reach while our raven waits. Directly below each top position, the bottom row shows the position reached the moment our waiting raven finally lands.

3 · Suppose

Suppose its landing always settled the night

Suppose, for contradiction, that whenever our raven lands, it always closes the night. Then every position in the bottom row would be closed—either locked in for Hold or locked in for Attack. And because the night started open, both Hold and Attack must appear somewhere in that bottom row.

4 · Neighbours

Somewhere, two neighbours split

Since the bottom row contains both outcomes, there must be two adjacent positions in the top row—just one arrival apart—where landing our raven at the first position locks in Hold, while landing it after that single arrival locks in Attack.

5 · Another tent's arrival

If that step is at another tent, order cannot matter

What is that single arrival between the two top neighbours? Case 1: Suppose it happens at a different tent than the one our waiting raven is flying to. By Lemma 1 (separate tents commute), landing our raven first and taking that step second reaches the exact same position as taking that step first and landing our raven second. That would mean a position already locked into Hold can step directly into a position locked into Attack—which is impossible!

6 · The same tent's arrival

If it is at the same tent, let that man go quiet

Case 2: What if both arrivals happen at the same tent? Then let that man go completely quiet—as if his birds are stuck in the pines—until the other three men finish the plan and commit on their own. Because the other three never hear from the quiet man, their steps commute with his. So even after the other three have committed, delivering the raven alone to the quiet man would force Hold, while delivering his other arrival first and then the raven would force Attack. A finished night would still be open—which is impossible too!

7 · So

The raven lands, and the night is open still

Both cases lead to an impossibility, so our supposition fails. Somewhere in the bottom row there must be a position that is still open after the raven lands. No single raven is ever forced to settle the night.

In short, whatever happens while the raven waits is either another tent's business (so the order of delivery cannot change the outcome) or the same tent's business (in which case that man can go quiet while the others finish, leaving them unable to tell which bird landed first). We now have two powerful facts: the night can begin open, and any waiting raven can be delivered without closing it. All that remains is to see what happens when we use both facts over and over.

Proof · An arrival can always be deferred and the night left open (Lemma 3)

Lemma 3. Let C be an open position and e an arrival applicable at C. Let 𝒞 be the set of positions reachable from C without allowing e, and 𝒟 the set of positions obtained by finally allowing e from each position in 𝒞. Then 𝒟 contains at least one open position.

  1. Let C be open and e an arrival ready to occur at C. Suppose, for contradiction, that no position in 𝒟 is open—meaning every position in 𝒟 is already closed for either Hold or Attack.
  2. Both outcomes must occur in 𝒟. Since C is open, some course from C ends in holding and another ends in attacking. If such a course never allows e, its positions lie in 𝒞 and allowing e at the end gives a member of 𝒟 closed for that same outcome; if the course does allow e, the position immediately after e occurs is already in 𝒟 and closed for that outcome.
  3. So 𝒞 contains neighbours that split in 𝒟. Because every position in 𝒞 is connected by single steps from C, walking from a position whose image in 𝒟 is closed for Hold to one whose image is closed for Attack must cross two neighbours C₀ and C₁ = f(C₀), one arrival f apart, whose images D₀ = e(C₀) and D₁ = e(C₁) are closed for opposite outcomes.
  4. Case 1: f is another man's arrival. By Lemma 1 (separate folds commute), e and f commute, so D₁ = f(D₀). But every position reachable from the Hold-closed position D₀ must also be closed for Hold, whereas D₁ is closed for Attack—a contradiction.
  5. Case 2: f is the same man's arrival, say p's. Take a fair course σ starting from C₀ in which p never takes a step and the remaining three men eventually commit; this is permitted because p could be the one silent man. Call the decided position reached by σ position A.
  6. Because σ has no man in common with e or f, Lemma 1 says σ can also be run from D₀ and from D₁, reaching e(A) and e(f(A)) respectively. Since these are reached from D₀ and D₁, e(A) is closed for Hold while e(f(A)) is closed for Attack. That means both outcomes are still reachable from A, so A is open—contradicting the fact that σ had already finished and decided at A!
  7. Since both cases lead to a contradiction, 𝒟 must contain an open position.∎
Step 3 · Two facts, used without rest

A fair night in which nothing is ever settled

Used one after another without rest, our two facts construct a night that obeys every rule of fairness and yet never reaches a decision. Start from a dusk sighting that leaves the night open (Lemma 2), place the four mercenaries in a rotating queue, and time the arrivals using Lemma 3 so that the night stays open after every round.

Specifically, the mercenary at the front of the queue looks for the raven that has been in the air the longest for his tent. Using Lemma 3, other ravens are allowed to land until delivering that oldest raven leaves the night still open (or if no raven is flying to his tent, he simply checks his empty perch), and then he moves to the back of the queue. Because every man's turn comes around repeatedly and the oldest waiting raven is always delivered next, no mercenary is ever starved of a turn and every raven eventually lands.

This simulation illustrates the structure of the proof: the rotating turns and raven deliveries are completely fair, and Lemmas 2 and 3 guarantee that against any deterministic plan the four could have chosen, the arrivals can be ordered so the night remains open forever.

Left to run forever, that night is completely fair—every bird lands and every man acts—yet at no stage is the decision ever settled. And the exact same endless night can be arranged against any deterministic plan the four mercenaries could ever invent, however clever.

No plan guarantees that anybody ever commits

Nobody actually dies in that endless night. The single death allowed by the rules was used only inside the proof's "what-if" reasoning, never on the hill itself. What paralyzes the four men is the mere possibility of a death, which looks identical to a slow bird.

Agreement is never violated. Nobody ever commits to the wrong answer—because nobody commits at all.

Even one man committing would have been enough. The third demand asked only that somebody eventually commit, and even that weaker promise cannot be kept.

The hill and the number four are not special. The exact same impossibility holds for any group of two or more mercenaries (or machines) trying to settle on any two choices.

Notice how the simulation ended in a decision once the birds were given a maximum flight time. That points directly to what the four mercenaries would need to break the impasse.

Three ways out

What would have to change

To guarantee a decision, the four mercenaries need some way to tell a dead comrade from a slow bird. Slow birds by themselves are no trouble, and neither are birds that arrive out of order. The trap springs from two things combined: no maximum flight time, and a dead man looking exactly like a slow bird. Changing the rules in any one of three ways is enough to escape:

One

A maximum flight time

If every raven has a longest possible flight time, then silence past that deadline proves a tent is dead, and the survivors can safely move on without it.

Two

An imperfect report of who has died

Give the camp a failure detector that eventually names every dead man and eventually stops falsely suspecting at least one living man. That is enough as long as a majority live.

Three

Every death before dusk

If no man can die once the night's exchanges have begun—only beforehand—then a living majority can always reach agreement.

None of these three is available at the foot of Mount Phyle (nor is flipping coins). The first, however, is available at the top of the hill, where the bandits' messenger rats have a known maximum running time.*

Back to machines

The camp was a group of machines

Now read the camp again with computers in place of the tents. Every tent is a process, every raven is a network message, and a tent that falls silent is a machine that has crashed. Hover over or tap any row below to see how the story maps to distributed computing.

In computing

When messages may take any amount of time and even one machine may crash, no deterministic protocol can guarantee that the group ever decides.

Agreement itself is never broken. What cannot be promised is an end to the waiting.

Real-world distributed systems live with this result by relaxing the rules slightly: assuming message delays eventually stay within bounds, using imperfect failure detectors, or flipping coins.

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Michael J. Fischer, Nancy A. Lynch and Michael S. Paterson, “Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process,” Journal of the ACM 32, 2 (April 1985), 374–382.

The ideas. No deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous network if even a single process is allowed to crash. The proof centers on bivalence (what our story calls an open position): a system configuration from which both possible decisions are still reachable. The authors prove two key lemmas: first, every protocol has at least one bivalent initial configuration (Lemma 2); second, from any bivalent configuration, any pending message delivery can be delayed behind other steps so that delivering it still leaves the system in a bivalent configuration (Lemma 3). Chaining these together lets an adversarial scheduler construct an infinite, fair run in which every process takes steps and every message is delivered, yet no decision is ever reached. Three subtleties make the theorem especially striking: the infinite run constructed by the proof contains no actual crash at all (the single permitted fault is used only to show that processes cannot wait for everyone); safety (agreement and validity) is never violated—only termination (liveness) is lost; and the impossibility holds even when we only require a single process to eventually decide. The root obstacle is not slow messages alone, but the inability to distinguish a crashed process from a slow one. Relaxing that constraint—by bounding message delays (even only eventually), adding an unreliable failure detector, allowing randomness, or restricting crashes to occur only before the protocol begins (as shown in §4 of the paper)—makes consensus solvable again.
