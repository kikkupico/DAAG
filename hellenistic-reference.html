1Overview and chronology

The Hellenistic period runs from the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC to 31 BC, the conventional end date. In that span the Greek world stopped being a patchwork of independent cities and became a set of cities living under, between and beside large kingdoms. The successors of Alexander built the Ptolemaic, Seleucid and Antigonid kingdoms, and Attalid Pergamon grew beside them. Rome moved into the Aegean after 200 BC and ended Macedon as a state in 146 BC, the year it sacked Corinth and dissolved the Achaean League (Wikipedia, Hellenistic Greece).

For an island polis, the practical effect was a protector king (or a league, or Rhodes) whose officials were the visible face of power, while the city kept its own council, assembly and magistrates. The Nesiotic League, founded about 314/313 BC under Antigonos Monophthalmos, grouped island cities such as Mykonos, Keos, Naxos, Andros, Amorgos and Paros, with Delos as its ceremonial centre; control passed to the Ptolemies and later to Rhodes (Wikipedia, Nesiotic League). Delos became an Athenian-run free port in 167/166 BC and the Mediterranean's largest slave market, until Mithridates' attacks of 88 and 69 BC ended its prosperity (Wikipedia, Delos).

For the artist, the period has a recognisable look. Cities are rebuilt in stone on regular plans. Ionic and Corinthian fashions spread, while Doric survives in long colonnades and courtyards. Pottery is mass-produced, kings put their own portraits on coins, and engineers produce artillery, water-lifting machines and geared instruments. Wealthy citizens fund public buildings and are repaid in inscribed honours. The sections below take each topic in turn; every section ends with what to draw, what to avoid, and what the sources could not settle.

2Buildings: stone orders, tiled roofs and civic ensembles

Hellenistic architects favoured Ionic and Corinthian over Doric for temples; one overview says the Doric temple was "virtually abandoned" (Wikipedia, Hellenistic architecture). Didyma's Ionic temple of Apollo, begun about 300 BC by Daphnis and Paionios and never finished, set a colossal scale, and the Corinthian order dominates at Alexandria's Serapeum and at the Olympieion in Athens, which Cossutius took up around 175 BC for Antiochus IV. Hermogenes of Priene, working in the late 3rd and early 2nd century, became Vitruvius's chief authority on Ionic (Wikipedia, Hermogenes). But Doric did not vanish: it survives on the outer fronts of stoas and in peristyle courts, and the Athena temple at Pergamon is Doric. Sourced for the examples, Inference for the generalisation that Doric moved from temples to colonnades and courtyards.

The rule that matters for the project is simple and well attested: Doric columns have no base and stand directly on the top step (stylobate); Ionic and Corinthian columns carry a moulded base (Wikipedia, Ancient Greek architecture). Doric shafts usually carry 18 to 20 flutes, Ionic and Corinthian typically 24 (Wikipedia, Ancient Greek temple). The project's unbased Doric canon is therefore correct. One tension to know about: because large Hellenistic temples are rarely Doric, a Doric temple reads as old-fashioned or conservative; Doric stoas, gymnasium courts and house peristyles are the safer Hellenistic uses.

Roofs, stone and colour

Roofs are low-pitched gables of timber under terracotta tiles in the pan-and-cover system: flat tegulae with raised edges, joints capped by half-round imbrices. The system replaced thatch around 700-650 BC for fire resistance, so by the Hellenistic period it is standard rather than new (Wikipedia, Roof tiles). A single tile could be large and heavy (up to about 90 by 70 cm and roughly 30 kg in one account), so walls had to be strong (Wikipedia, Ancient Greek architecture). The project's pan-and-cover canon is correct. Painted antefixes along the eaves are plausible Inference. The Tower of the Winds is the exception that proves the rule: its roof is 24 stone slabs carved to look like tiles (Wikipedia, Tower of the Winds).

Walls are ashlar on stepped platforms: dry-laid blocks, column drums centred by pins, load-bearing joints clamped with iron set in lead (Wikipedia, Ancient Greek architecture). Marble comes from Paros, Naxos and (for Athens) Mount Pentelikon; Pergamon used grey-veined marble from the Marmara region. Stone was not left bare. Temple colour followed a white, blue and red scheme, with blue triglyphs between red metopes, and white or stuccoed columns (Wikipedia, Ancient Greek temple). For the Pergamon Altar, paint is likely but no traces survive. Debated Specific Hellenistic colour schemes are mostly inferred. Inference

The civic ensemble

The public core of a city is built from a few repeating types. Stoas are long porches with shops or offices behind; the Stoa of Attalos (159-138 BC) had two storeys with 42 shops, 21 per floor, and was rebuilt in 1952-56 (Wikipedia, Stoa of Attalos). Theatres are cut into hillsides; the Athenian theatre of Dionysus gained a permanent stone stage front (proskenion) in the Hellenistic phase (Wikipedia, Theatre of Dionysus), and Pergamon's theatre is among the steepest known. Council halls (bouleuteria) are near-square roofed rooms with raked seating, as the Priene plan shows. Gymnasia are courts enclosed by stoas into a peristyle; a palaestra is recognised by its peristyle, exedra and washing basin (loutron), and a covered running track (xystos) belongs nearby (Wikipedia, Palaestra at Delphi). The Pergamon library had four rooms, with a gap behind the shelves for air circulation (Wikipedia, Library of Pergamum). The Great Altar of Eumenes II (c. 166-156 BC) stands on a high podium with an Ionic colonnade round an inner court and a Gigantomachy frieze outside (Wikipedia, Pergamon Altar). The Pharos of Alexandria (284-246 BC) rose in three tiers, square, octagonal and round, and its architect is disputed (Wikipedia, Lighthouse of Alexandria).

Houses

Private houses face inward on a courtyard. At Priene the standard house is a rectangular plot with a porch (prostas or pastas), a main room (oikos) and a dining room (andron) opening onto the court, and the first peristyle houses appear in the Hellenistic period; about a third had indoor toilets, with water from aqueducts and cisterns (Wikipedia, Priene). Delos has richer two-storey peristyle houses: the House of the Trident (c. 150-100 BC) has a Rhodian peristyle with Doric columns and a rainwater cistern under an impluvium (Perseus). Floors are pebble or tessera mosaic, and Delos mosaics (a dolphin signed by Asklepiades of Arados) are among the best-known examples. Walls were plastered and painted in imitation of masonry Inference, low roofs were tiled, and upper floors were reached by a timber stair Inference.

Walls and towers

Hellenistic walls are described as "relatively low, but powerful", of ashlar or mudbrick. Towers began as single-storey squares and became multi-storey structures with large upper chambers for artillery, increasingly standing apart from the curtain (Wikipedia, Hellenistic fortifications). Aigosthena is one of the best-preserved circuits, with eight artillery towers and masonry that mixes isodomic, pseudo-isodomic and near-polygonal courses (Wikipedia, Aigosthena).

What to draw

  • Doric: no base, 18-20 flutes, blue triglyphs and red metopes, white or stuccoed shafts. Ionic and Corinthian: moulded base, about 24 flutes. Sourced
  • Low-pitched gable roofs of pan-and-cover terracotta tiles, with eaves ornaments. Stone built in regular ashlar courses on a stepped base. Sourced
  • Long stoas with shop doors behind a colonnade; a theatre cut into the slope; a council hall with raked seats; gymnasium courts ringed by porticoes. Sourced
  • Inward-facing courtyard houses with a cistern, pebble or mosaic floors and a timber stair. Sourced and Inference
  • Towers of several storeys with large rectangular artillery windows above small arrow slits. Sourced for the idea, Inference for window shapes

Avoid

  • Bases under Doric columns; Roman arches, concrete vaults, domes and monumental aqueduct arcades.
  • Bare unpainted white marble everywhere. Colour on entablatures, antefixes and walls is the safer default.
  • Presenting the Stoa of Attalos or the Great Altar photographs as ancient fabric: both are reconstructions in their present form.

Known gaps

  • No verified proportions for Hellenistic Doric against Classical Doric, and no source on antefix forms or marble roof tiles. Inference only.
  • Fountain houses, tombs and stage-building details were not verified; Macedonian painted tombs and the Peirene fountain are background knowledge only.
  • The roofing of council halls, and furniture, doors and lamps of houses, were not sourced.

3Town planning: grids, terraces, harbours and countryside

New foundations and rebuilt cities were laid out on a grid, traditionally credited to Hippodamos of Miletus, a Classical figure (Wikipedia, Hippodamus). Priene, rebuilt in the 4th century and occupied through the Hellenistic period, is the model: a compass-aligned grid on a steep slope, with a few broad streets crossed by many narrow ones, about 80 blocks of about eight houses each and a population of 4,000 to 6,000. The agora is the commercial centre with the council hall and prytaneion beside it, while the theatre, gymnasium and sanctuary of Athena stand on higher terraces, and an aqueduct from mountain springs feeds public drains (Wikipedia, Priene). Where the slope is steep, the grid is kept and absorbed into terraces, and the narrow cross streets become stairs. Inference

Not every town is a grid. Rebuilt and newly founded cities (Priene, Kos, Rhodes, Alexandria, Miletus) are regular; organic old towns and Delos's Theatre Quarter wind along crooked streets, "ungoverned, if not confused" in one excavator's phrase, and rich and modest houses are mixed in every quarter (EFA, Delos). Pergamon, a royal capital on a mesa, has hairpin streets, an upper acropolis with altar, theatre, Athena sanctuary, library and palaces, and a lower city with a three-terrace gymnasium and several agoras (Wikipedia, Pergamon). Hellenistic plans "conformed to the natural setting" rather than levelling it (Wikipedia, Hellenistic art). A mixed island town, with a regular new quarter beside an older winding core, is therefore plausible. Inference

Public core, water and the waterfront

The agora is an open court framed by stoas, "decorated with fountains, trees, and statues", with merchants' stalls under the arcades (Wikipedia, Agora). Fountain houses, altars and honorific statues on bases crowd the edges. Inference Water came from springs by aqueduct, from cisterns, and from fountains; Pergamon's supply included a long pressurised pipeline across a valley (Wikipedia, Pergamon), and covered drains run under the northern streets of Delos. Harbours follow Vitruvius's advice, written in the Roman era about Greek practice: use natural headlands, build colonnades or shipsheds round the basin, and set towers on both sides of the mouth (Vitruvius V) Roman. At Delos the sacred port had a protective mole, and the commercial port lay to the south with shops and warehouses lined along the water (Ancient Ports Antiques, Delos). Rhodes had a commercial harbour south of the military one, protected by a fortified mole. At Alexandria the Heptastadion causeway divided two harbours, with the Pharos at its end (Wikipedia, Alexandria).

The countryside and the island edge

The countryside (chora) is a patchwork of terraces with isolated tower-farms made of a tower (pyrgos), courtyard (aule) and house (oikia), documented in Attica, Keos, Thasos and Melos. Towers on Thasos served variously as signals for sailors, farm strongholds and strongrooms (BSA, Island Towers), and terraces are documented at Delos and Keos (Agricultural Terraces). Boundary stones (horoi) marked estates, shrines of Pan, Nymphs and Hermes were common, and cemeteries lined the roads outside the gates. Inference Colonnaded main streets, aqueduct arcades and triumphal arches belong to the Roman city; avoid them.

What to draw

  • A regular grid on a slope, long blocks following the contour, narrow stair-streets between terraces. Sourced
  • An agora with stoas on at least two sides, an open court, trees, statues on bases, an altar, a fountain house at a corner. Sourced and Inference
  • Council hall and prytaneion beside the agora; theatre and sanctuary above; gymnasium and stadium on the lower edge of town. Sourced
  • A waterfront of low shops and warehouses with wide doors, a rubble mole, ships beached or anchored off. Sourced for Delos, Inference for the details
  • Terraced fields, a stone tower-farm, dry-stone walls, tracks to the shore. Sourced and Inference

Avoid

  • Roman colonnaded avenues, arches, aqueduct arcades; every town as a perfect grid; flat plains for hill cities.
  • Using measured street widths or block sizes as fixed rules; the project describes places by constraints, and the sources give only that main streets run about twice the width of cross streets. Sourced

Known gaps

  • Gate and tower typology, craft-quarter zoning and necropolis placement were not sourced; they are background knowledge only. Inference
  • No citable island fort or refuge examples were retrieved. Paving materials, rural sanctuary types and road layout are unsourced.
  • No Rhodes, Kos, Miletus or standalone Priene city-plan image was found with a public-domain licence.

4Tools and crafts: from quarry to kitchen

Hellenistic craftwork is conservative in tools and inventive in machines. Stone carving kept the old iron kit: the point chisel (the "oldest, simplest and most versatile" mason's tool), the flat chisel for finishing, the tooth or claw chisel between them, and picks and wedges in the quarry (Wootton, Russell and Rockwell). That essay concerns the Roman world, so its sizes are plausible rather than Hellenistic-certain. Roman Blocks were rough-hewn at the quarry and fitted precisely on site, with lifting bosses left on the stone for ropes and levers, and lewis irons set into cut sockets (Wikipedia, Lifting boss).

The standard building crane was a shear-legs derrick (three poles pinned at the head) with a compound pulley block and a windlass or capstan worked with push-bars, held by staked guy ropes (Vitruvius X) Roman (Roman text using Greek terms). The treadwheel crane is shown only on Roman reliefs, so it is an anachronism risk on a Greek site. Roman Tiles, bricks and stucco follow Vitruvius's Greek-derived types: bricks of several palm-based sizes, and stucco built from three sand-lime coats and three marble-dust coats, with colours applied on the damp surface (Vitruvius VII) Roman.

Farm, sea and hive

Aegean farming was ard-and-hoe cultivation of cereals, vine and olive. The ard is an all-wood scratch plough drawn by oxen or donkeys, with two-pronged mattocks and hoes, and curved sickles for both grain and pruning (OCD entry, via summary). Grain was ground on saddle querns and, increasingly, on the hopper-rubber mill of Olynthus; the large rotary mills of Pompeii are Roman (Wikipedia, Quern-stone). A Hellenistic oil installation at Argilos had a trapetum (two lens-shaped stones on wooden beams), grooved press beds and a winch-assisted beam press (Argilos excavation). Wine from Rhodes, Kos, Chios, Lesbos and Thasos demand peaked in Hellenistic and Roman times. Beekeeping used terracotta hives, and Hellenistic hives of a recognised type are known from Isthmia. Fishing used lines, nets, traps and tridents, with lookouts for tuna (AKMED, via summary). Ptolemaic water wheels belong to Egypt; for the Aegean the safer water-lifters are the well sweep and bucket, or the screw pump. Inference

Pottery, glass and cloth

Vase painting declined and pottery became an industry. The ware families are Megarian (mould-made relief bowls), West Slope (white and yellow sprays on dark slip), Gnathia and Tanagra terracotta figurines (Wikipedia, Hellenistic pottery). Rhodian amphorae are the standard trade jar: pointed, narrow-necked and two-handled, stamped on one handle with a seal naming the fabricant and the annual eponymous official, usually with a rose or Helios head (a Met example of about 180 BC names Xenophon and the month Sminthios). Lamps are olive-oil burners; mould-made lamps appear at the start of the 3rd century BC, made from two clay halves pressed in a two-part mould (Getty, Ancient Lamps). Glass is soda-lime, core-formed or cast, with mosaic bowls and, from the early 2nd century, monochrome hemispherical bowls (Wikipedia, Hellenistic glass). Cloth was woven on the warp-weighted loom, an upright frame with hanging weights, shown on vases with two weavers side by side (Wikipedia, Warp-weighted loom).

Machines, clocks and everyday objects

Ctesibius of Alexandria (active about 285-222 BC) devised the double-action force pump and improved water clocks; Archimedes' screw is first mentioned between 250 and 220 BC as a bilge pump on a ship of Hiero II; Philo of Byzantium (about 280-220 BC) described the earliest water mill and a chain pump (Wikipedia, Ancient Greek technology). Hero of Alexandria is Roman-era (1st century AD) and should not be used for Hellenistic scenes. The Antikythera mechanism (a geared bronze calendar and astronomical instrument in a wooden case, found in 1901) belongs to the 2nd or early 1st century BC Debated. The Tower of the Winds (c. 50 BC) held a water clock inside and sundials outside (Wikipedia, Tower of the Winds). Furniture is best read from banquet reliefs: a kline with bolsters, a low three-legged table, and chairs with backs for seated women. The klismos chair lost favour in the Hellenistic period, so it is a poor default. Writing used papyrus rolls, wax tablets with a stylus, ostraka and, for public records, stone and bronze; steelyards were already used by Greek craftsmen in the 5th and 4th centuries BC.

5Weapons and war: pike, oval shield, torsion engine and ram

The signature infantryman is the Macedonian phalangite, whose sarissa is a two-handed pike of ash or cornel, about 5 to 7 m long, with a metal head and butt-spike. Theophrastus gives 12 cubits (about 5.8 m), Polybius 14-16 cubits for later armies, and a modern 5.8 m reconstruction weighed about 4 kg. The shield is small and hung from a neck strap so that both hands can hold the pike (Wikipedia, Sarissa), (Macedonian phalanx). Some details are Debated: the head size is estimated anywhere from 13.5 to 51 cm, and the two-piece jointed shaft is "largely abandoned". Helmets are the Phrygian type (one-piece bronze with a tall forward-leaning apex and cheekpieces) and the open-faced Boeotian type, mostly for cavalry (Wikipedia, Phrygian helmet). Body armour is the linothorax, a layered linen cuirass with a fringe of strips (pteruges), whose construction is Debated; the muscle cuirass in bronze mostly marks kings and officers (Wikipedia, Muscle cuirass).

The medium infantryman of the 3rd to 1st centuries BC is the thureophoros, with a large oval thureos shield with a spine, a long thrusting spear, javelins and a sword. The shield was probably adapted from a Celtic type after the Gallic invasion of 279 BC; the Aetolian and Achaean Leagues and Boeotia adopted it in the 270s, and the Achaeans dropped it about 208-207 BC for the phalanx (Wikipedia, Thureophoros). Heavier versions in mail are thorakitai. Missile specialists were hired: Cretan archers with the composite bow, and Rhodian slingers whose almond-shaped lead bullets were sometimes inscribed (Wikipedia, Sling). Cavalry range from lance-armed prodromoi and Boeotian-helmeted riders to armoured cataphracts; elephants (500 Indian elephants for the Seleucids, turreted African ones at Raphia in 217 BC) stood in the line (Wikipedia, War elephant). Riders used no stirrups. The kopis is the forward-curving single-edged sword, the xiphos the straight double-edged one. Galatians are shown with torcs, as on the Dying Gaul.

Siege machines and fortification

Torsion artillery appears in Athenian arsenal inscriptions of 338-326 BC and at Tyre in 332 BC. A torsion machine is a rectangular wooden frame with two bundles of twisted sinew or hair skeins holding two arms, a bowstring, a slider and a rear winch, with bronze or iron washers capping each bundle (Wikipedia, Ballista), (Greek and Roman artillery). All parts were sized from one module, the spring-hole diameter (Antigone Journal). The meaning of euthytonon and palintonon is Debated; do not adopt the single-arm reading found in one article. Rhodes withstood Demetrios Poliorketes in 305-304 BC with counter-mines, ditches and fire; his giant siege tower (helepolis) was iron-plated, carried catapults on several floors, and the Rhodians flooded the ground before it with mud and sewage. Ancient and modern sources give conflicting dimensions for it, so draw it as a very tall, wheeled, multi-storey, plated tower rather than from a number (Wikipedia, Helepolis). Sources also disagree on how thick a wall must be to resist stone shot; this is unresolved, so draw massive walls, not a figure. Debated

Ships of war and local defence

Fleets ran from light lembi and hemioliai to quinqueremes, with showpiece giants such as the tessarakonteres of Ptolemy IV; the quinquereme's oar arrangement is Debated (Wikipedia, Polyreme). The lembus is small, low-sided and fast, used by Illyrian pirates and, in 100 examples, by Philip V about 216 BC (Wikipedia, Lembus). A warship's bronze ram is a single casting around timber (the Athlit ram, whose date is uncertain). The Lindos rock relief is a warship stern, not a prow, from about 180 BC.

For small cities, the sourced picture is thin. Rhodes defended itself in 305 BC with about 6,000 citizens, 1,000 resident foreigners and freed slaves (Wikipedia, Siege of Rhodes); leagues used thureophoroi; the ephebes of Athens were issued a spear and shield, and their equipment included the chlamys and petasos (Wikipedia, Ephebe) Classical (rules from Classical Athens). A night watchman or small garrison with spear, thureos or small shield, chlamys and petasos is therefore a reasonable reading. Inference Mixed kit in one band (Phrygian helmet, linothorax, thureos, javelins) fits the sources.

  • Medium troops: tall oval shield with a vertical spine, spear, javelins, helmet; some in mail. Sourced
  • Artillery: wooden frame, two skein bundles with bronze washers, arms, winch; walls with multi-storey towers and big upper windows. Sourced and Inference
  • Rams and tall wheeled towers with iron plates or hides; lembi with low sides; warships with a bronze ram. Sourced

Avoid

  • Roman scutum and segmented armour, gladius, Roman metal-frame ballistae, testudo formations (the soldiers' shield roof is Roman). Roman
  • Stirrups; Corinthian helmets in the front line; Chalcidian and Attic helmets as everyday kit (they are Classical and fading, or archaising for generals). Classical
  • Copying a Roman copy (Alexander mosaic, Dying Gaul) as if it were a Hellenistic original; use it to read kit only.
  • Any exact dimensions for the helepolis or for wall thickness.

  • Not found: greaves and muscle-cuirass specifics for Hellenistic use, Companion cavalry details after Alexander, Thracian rhomphaia in art, ephebes' and watchmen's kit on islands, bandit and pirate dress.
  • Philo's and Marsden's artillery formulas were not read, and euthytonon/palintonon geometry stays unresolved.
  • No public-domain image exists in the sources for the helepolis, a Marsden-type torsion engine, a Greek (non-Italic) Hellenistic helmet, a thureos find or ship sheds.

6Clothing: draped wool and linen, hats and hair

Greek dress is mostly rectangles of cloth, pinned and belted. Men wear a chiton, a linen or wool tunic fastened at the shoulders, shorter for work or travel, and a himation, a heavy rectangular wool wrap passed under one arm and over the opposite shoulder (Wikipedia, Ancient Greek clothing). A new Hellenistic habit is the himation worn with the arm bound inside it, as on the portrait of Aeschines, an early example of the modest pose that spread among citizens and philosophers. For travel and military use the chlamys is a seamless wool rectangle pinned at the right shoulder. The exomis is a short belted tunic fastened on one shoulder, leaving the other arm free, worn in art by artisans, labourers, slaves and fishermen. Sourced Hats are the broad-brimmed petasos (Thessalian in origin, with a chin cord, worn by farmers, travellers and hunters), the close pilos and the flat Macedonian kausia, the best single Hellenistic marker, though it belongs to Macedonians and Macedonian-linked figures rather than mainland civilians (Wikipedia, Petasos), (Kausia).

The project's choices fit this evidence. Working figures in petasos and exomis with a satchel (pera) are well supported by the artisan and traveller type; the pera is attested for travellers, Cynics and shepherds in background scholarship that was not verified in the sources reviewed Inference. A bare-headed messenger is a deliberate project choice that departs from the usual petasos-and-herald (Hermes) iconography; the sources support the petasos for travellers, so the bare head is a choice, not an error.

Women, hair and ornament

The Tanagra terracottas, which run from the later 4th century BC through the Hellenistic period, show women in a chiton under a thin enveloping himation, often drawn over the head, with wreaths, fans and large sun hats; they were moulded, coated in white slip and painted in pink, blue and red, sometimes gilded (Wikipedia, Tanagra figurine). Hair is confined by headbands and nets, in styles named in sources: the lampadion topknot, the grooved "melon" coiffure and the Knidian bun, with the Heracles knot as the most elaborate Hellenistic form. Hellenistic gold is coloured with garnet and other stones and often carries a Heracles knot, with snake bracelets and diadems (Met 250990). Men's beards are the key change: after Alexander, a clean-shaven face became the elite fashion, leaving the beard to philosophers, older men and rustics (The Collector).

Footwear, cloth and colour

Sandals are standard; krepides are laced, more enclosed shoes; endromides are high boots for travel and hunting, shown mostly on gods and heroes in art, and Greeks often went barefoot indoors (x-legio, a re-enactor site of moderate reliability). Wool and linen dominate; silk and fine Amorgos cloth are luxury goods; Tyrian purple signals wealth. Mourners wear grey or black. A realism rule: ordinary people wear undyed, brown, grey or ochre wool, while bright dyes and purple mark the rich, and a colourless white-marble look is a modern error (Wikipedia, Ancient Greek clothing). Inference for the rule, Sourced for the dyes. Hellenistic genre sculpture, such as the Old Fisherman and the Old Market Woman (both Roman copies), shows the poor and old in short tunics, with an emphasis on bodily decay.

7Transport: round ships, mule tracks and signal fires

The best-evidenced Hellenistic merchantman is the Kyrenia ship: a pine, shell-first, mortise-and-tenon hull of about 15 m, launched in the late 4th century BC and sunk off Cyprus in the early 3rd after a long working life with many repairs (Wikipedia, Kyrenia ship). Its final repair added lead sheathing. It carried one square sail gathered by cords through lead rings (a brail), one anchor with a lead-filled stock, and a crew of about four, inferred from the cabin pottery. The cargo was mainly Rhodian wine amphorae with other jars, almonds, millstones and iron billets. Pomey defines a "Hellenistic type" hull: wine-glass section, keel and rabbeted stem, planking joined by mortise-and-tenon, and floor timbers alternating with half-frames (Pomey, Naval Architecture).

Sources disagree on details, and the disagreement should stay visible. The launch date is given as about 325-315 BC in one account and about 315 BC in another, with a radiocarbon summary as early as 389 BC (plus or minus 44); the sinking is dated 295-285 BC or about 294 BC. The cargo count is 381 amphorae (340 Rhodian) in one source, while another describes a main cargo of 220 large Rhodian amphorae as replicated for loading trials. The count of brail rings is "about 100" in one source and "over 150" in another. Debated For artwork, draw a loaded, small, round-hulled coaster with a brailed square sail, two stern steering oars and one anchor. Painted eyes, hull colours and a forward artemon sail are not evidenced for the Hellenistic period in the sources reviewed (the artemon is attested in Roman-period images) and are left out of the schematic. Bigger ships existed: the Syracusia of Hiero II is said to have carried 1,600 to 1,800 tons of cargo by one account and 2,000 to 4,000 by another, with eight towers and a garden Debated (Wikipedia, Syracusia); the Madrague de Giens wreck (c. 75-60 BC, about 400 tons, double planking with lead) is a later Roman-period ship of Hellenistic type. Do not paint every merchantman as a Syracusia.

Harbours, seasons and trade

Sailing was concentrated between late spring and early autumn; Hesiod's calendar closes the winter sea after the Pleiades set (Tammuz 2005), so a high-summer scene shows the most ships. Sourced Greek harbours were mole-protected basins with shipsheds for warships (the Zea harbour at Piraeus had 196 sheds by 330 BC, but that is Classical evidence) and, at the great ports, lighthouses. Grain flowed in from Egypt, the Black Sea and Sicily; wine, oil and pottery went out (Wikipedia, Economy of ancient Greece). Merchants borrowed against cargo and ship, typically for short periods and often at about 12 percent, though rates reached 100 percent (Classical Athenian evidence continued). The Kyrenia cargo shows a coasting circuit through Rhodes, Kos, Knidos, Samos, Cyprus, the Levant and Egypt. A Rhodian terracotta donkey with vessels survives, but its date is not stated on the file.

Roads, animals and messages

Roads were rudimentary: dirt tracks, with some cut with wheel ruts, and built roads only between major cities and sanctuaries; there were no state way-stations or milestones as in Rome (Wikipedia, Travel in classical antiquity). Mules and donkeys carried most goods (a mule up to about 90 kg), ox carts only heavy loads, and riders used no stirrups, the earliest hard foot support being a late-2nd-century-BC Indian toe loop (Wikipedia, Stirrup). Ordinary overland pace was about 24-40 km a day, an ox cart much slower (Encyclopedia.com), a mixed-period summary. News travelled by hemerodromoi (day-runners), messenger ships, and relay beacon towers (phryctoriae) about 30 km apart, with torch-letter signalling described by Polybius (Wikipedia, Phryctoria). For a rural island scene: a narrow track between dry-stone terrace walls, a donkey with paired vessels on a pack saddle, and perhaps one two-wheeled cart near the port. Inference

What to draw

  • A Kyrenia-size coaster: round hull, one broad square sail with brail cords, two steering oars at the stern, one anchor, amphorae in layers. Sourced
  • Pack animals on narrow terrace tracks; rare ox carts; riders on a pad or blanket without stirrups. Sourced and Inference
  • A hilltop beacon tower or signal fire for the Aegean; messengers on foot. Sourced
  • Busy beaches in summer; hulls hauled out in winter. Inference

Avoid

  • Roman stock-and-fluke anchors, artemon foresails and double-planked giant grain ships on small-island scenes. Roman
  • Paved Roman roads with milestones, litters and imperial post stations; stirrups; horseshoes. Roman
  • Treating the Syracusia print or the 1572 Colossus engraving as evidence. Roman / later imagination
  • Using the Piraeus ship sheds or a Classical trireme as a Hellenistic merchant ship. Classical

Known gaps

  • Not found: painted eyes and hull colours of Hellenistic merchantmen, fishing boats and caiques, harbour cranes, ship-sheds at Hellenistic ports, amphora dunnage, litters, bridges, inns, royal posts, and the Lindos stern image.
  • No farm-to-port data for islands. No public-domain Kyrenia photograph exists in the sources (all are CC BY or CC BY-SA), which is why the ship is drawn as a schematic here.

8Administration and economy: decrees, honours and coin

Hellenistic poleis kept the Classical machinery of council and assembly, but ran on benefactors. The Athenian assembly (ekklesia) met three or four times a month, voted by hands or counting stones, and had its agenda set by the council (boule) (Wikipedia, Ecclesia). Presiding committees (prytaneis) ran councils at Athens, and also at Rhodes, Alexandria and west-coast Asia Minor cities (Wikipedia, Prytaneis). Compulsory liturgies such as the trierarchy turned into voluntary prestige spending, called euergetism: benefactors paid for theatres, gymnasia, fountains and markets, and the city repaid them in crowns, statues, front seats and decrees (Wikipedia, Euergetism). Officers included the agoranomos (market, weights and measures), astynomoi (city order) and sitonai (grain). The gymnasiarch ran the ephebes, whose service shrank to a year and became "a sort of university" (Wikipedia, Ephebi).

The decree is the artefact to understand. The standard enactment formula is "it was resolved by the council and the people"; the text names a proposer, the honorand and the reason, the award, and where the stele is to stand, usually a temple (Wikipedia, Proxeny). A worked example is the Theangela decree for Semos of Delos (c. 230-220 BC): Semos bought women of Theangela out of pirates' booty, the assembly voted 620 in favour, honours included proxeny, citizenship and a place on the benefactors' stele, and two envoys carried the decree to Delos (Attalus, IG XI 4 1054). A stele at Delos in the 3rd century cost about 135 drachmas in all, mostly the engraving at one drachma per 300 letters Unverified (primary text unverified), which is on the order of 70 to 130 days of a labourer's wage if that wage was one to two drachmas a day. Inference

Kings, leagues and law

Kings governed cities by letter and reciprocity rather than direct administration, except in Egypt. The documents are royal letters and civic decrees, inscribed on stone by the recipient city, and the king is honoured with altars (BMCR review of Ma). Ptolemaic Egypt was a bureaucratic estate with 30-plus nomes, each under a Greek strategos, and tax rights auctioned to farmers who paid into royal banks (Britannica). In the islands the visible officials were the nesiarch of the Nesiotic League, its treasurer, and garrison commanders; a Ptolemaic commander on Thera governed the island from 267/6 BC (Wikipedia, Phrourarch). Rhodes ran on an assembly, a council that sat six months at a time, prytaneis, strategoi and nauarchs. The Achaean League elected one strategos a year and met four times a year; the Aetolian League's assembly met at Thermos. Disputes between cities went to foreign judges, most often supplied by Messene, Kos and Priene, and cities secured sanctuaries as inviolable (asylia) and merged by sympoliteia (Attalus). At Delphi, about 1,300 manumission inscriptions record slaves "sold" to Apollo, over 60 percent of them women (Wikipedia, Delphic manumissions).

Money, banks and piracy

The Attic standard is the working frame: a talent is 60 minas, a mina 100 drachmas, a drachma 6 obols, with weights of about 25.86 kg, 431 g, 4.31 g and 0.72 g (Wikipedia, units). Hellenistic novelties include portraits of living kings on coins. Rhodian coinage, with Helios and a rose, dominated eastern trade after the siege of 305-304 BC (Wikipedia, Rhodian coinage). Sanctuaries were the banks: Apollo's treasury on Delos lent at 10 percent a year against mortgaged houses, and Delos leases of the god's land ran ten years, required two guarantors, and posted defaulters on stone with their fathers' names (Attalus, inscription 223). Polybius records that Rhodian harbour dues, leased for 1,000,000 drachmas in earlier times, fetched only 150,000 after Delos became a free port (Polybius 30). Piracy made ransom a routine civic business, with endemic zones in Cilicia and Illyria, and Delos selling up to 10,000 slaves in a day on Strabo's account (Wikipedia, Piracy). Writing used ostraka, wax tablets, lead, linen and papyrus; the Dodona oracle's lead tablets (over 4,000, to the mid-2nd century BC) show private questions on marriage, travel and slaves.

What to draw

  • A herald and magistrates at an assembly in a theatre or agora; a council rotating through its presiding committee. Sourced for the structure, Inference for the staging
  • A stone mason cutting a decree stele; the stele set in a sanctuary beside statues; crowns and honorary seats as rewards. Sourced
  • Silver tetradrachms with kings or Herakles, bronze for local use, a banker at a table, a sanctuary treasury with locked chests. Sourced and Inference
  • King's officials as a garrison commander or league officer; pirates' captives awaiting ransom. Sourced

Avoid

  • Roman offices and courts (consuls, praetors, Roman-style tribunals) in a Greek polis scene. Roman
  • Giving a small city a coin type from a source that did not name one; the civic coin types of Tenos, Paros, Naxos and Amorgos were not found.
  • Stating the military pay figures of 50, 20 and 10 drachmas a day that appear in one summary; they are implausible and excluded from this reference.

Known gaps

  • No primary decree text for a small island polis, no ballot types or voting procedure for one, and no archive, notary or oath procedures were found.
  • Attalid and Antigonid administration, Rhodian maritime law, maritime-loan rates, the value of the bronze chalkous and Athenian New Style coinage were not sourced.
  • No public-domain image of a Hellenistic decree stele, ballot or court clepsydra was found.

9Art-use checklist and anachronisms to avoid

This section collects the guardrails in one place. The list of project decisions comes first, because those are choices rather than facts. Then come the cross-cutting do and avoid items, then a table of the clearest Roman and Classical intrusions.

Project canon, and how the evidence treats it

  • Unbased Doric columns. Fully supported: Doric has no base. Sourced Doric temples are rare in the period, so Doric is best used on stoas, courts and houses. Inference
  • Pan-and-cover tile roofs. Fully supported: the standard terracotta roof. Sourced The Tower of the Winds' carved stone slabs are the exception.
  • Petasos, exomis and pera for working figures. Petasos and exomis are attested; the pera is attested in background scholarship that was not verified. Sourced and Inference
  • Bare-headed messengers. A deliberate departure from the petasos-and-herald (Hermes) iconography, not an error. Keep it consistent across all images.
  • Places described by constraints, not measurements. Followed throughout: where a source gave a dimension it is used for scale only.

Do

  • Show colour: painted entablatures, dyed (but muted) wool, gilt and blue on figurines.
  • Use Tanagra figurines for dress, Delos mosaics for floors, Priene and Pergamon plans for towns.
  • Mix kit: Phrygian helmet, linen armour, oval shield and javelins can occur together.
  • Keep dates in view: Pergamon Altar after 166 BC, Delos free port after 167/166, Stoa of Attalos after 159 BC.

Avoid

  • Roman architecture and costume (arches, concrete vaults, toga, tunica with stripes, caligae, Roman scutum).
  • Classical-only items as everyday Hellenistic kit (klismos, Chalcidian helmet, the hoplite aspis as the default for royal field armies (it suits citizen levies)).
  • Modern reconstructions as ancient evidence (Stoa of Attalos in Athens, museum models and photographs of the Pergamon Altar in Berlin).
  • Later imaginations: Heemskerck's Colossus, Witsen's Syracusia.
