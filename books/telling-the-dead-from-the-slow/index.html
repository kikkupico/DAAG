Telling the Dead from the Slow

A machine that has crashed looks exactly like one that is merely slow, and that alone makes agreement impossible. How well must we tell the dead from the slow before agreement becomes possible again?

We find out by exploring how the same Greek mercenaries, each keeping a slate of the tents he thought dead, agreed whether to attack or hold though the slates were often wrong.

After Tushar Deepak Chandra and Sam Toueg, Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems, 1996

First, in machine terms

A guess about who has crashed

A machine that has crashed looks exactly like one that is merely slow, and that alone makes agreement impossible.

  • A failure detector is a module on each machine that lists the machines it suspects have crashed. The list may be wrong, often.
  • Two properties are asked of it: crashed machines are eventually suspected, and live ones are not.
  • Chandra and Toueg (1996): with one correct machine never suspected, agreement survives any number of crashes. If that holds only eventually, it survives while more than half the machines live.

This edition tells it with four mercenaries, their ravens, and a slate at each tent.

The foot of Mount Phyle

Four tents, out of sight of one another

Four mercenaries are camped at the foot of the hill, one to a tent, under nobody's command.* Bandits hold the top.

Three demands, one change

A slate at every tent

The four must all attack or all hold. Each formed an opinion at dusk, a sighting. Committing to either takes a man from the tent and its birds for the night.

First demand

No two commit differently

Nobody attacks while another holds.

Second demand

Only a sighted answer

A man commits only to an answer someone sighted.

Third demand

Every living man commits

Every man who is not killed eventually commits.

Can they be sure of meeting all three? On such a night no arrangement guarantees that anyone commits. A man waiting on a silent tent cannot tell whether the man there is dead or the bird is still in the pines.*

If the man waits, the wait may be for ever, on a dead man. If the man stops waiting, a live man whose bird was about to arrive may be left out.

What if each man had some help in telling the dead from the slow? That is the one change in this book. Each tent has a slate, with a row for every other tent. A man chalks a tent thought dead, and rubs the mark out when thinking again. How a man decides is not the question here.

Is the slate right? Often not. A mark may stand against a live man whose bird is merely slow, and a dead man's row may stay clean for a while. How good must a slate be for the four to agree, and what does it mean for a slate to be good at all?

Two ways to go wrong

How a slate may be wrong

A slate can fail to chalk the dead, or it can chalk the living. So two things are asked of it. Try a slate and see.

The dead

Chalking the dead

Every man who is killed is, in the end, chalked for good. Either by every living man, or at least by some living man.

The living

Sparing the living

Four strengths, from strongest to weakest. The next spread lists them.

A warning

Chalking the dead is worth nothing alone

A slate that chalks every tent all night chalks every dead man, and tells nobody anything. It is the sparing of the living that makes a slate useful.

Four strengths of sparing

Eight kinds of slate

Two ways of chalking the dead and four of sparing the living make eight kinds of slate.

  • Never chalked. No man is chalked while living.
  • One never chalked. Some man who lives through the night is never chalked by anyone.
  • In the end, never chalked. There comes a time after which no living man is chalked.
  • In the end, one never chalked. There comes a time after which some man who lives through the night is chalked by no one.

All that is asked is that, from some moment nobody can see, one man who lives is left alone. Does any man know when that moment has come, or who that man is? No.

The two rows are one

Copying marks

If only some living man chalks the dead, can the rest be brought to chalk them too? Yes: the men make every living man do so by copying one another's marks.

Every man keeps a second slate, a copy. Again and again the man sends every tent the marks on the first slate. When a bird comes from a tent, the man adds its marks to the copy, and rubs out the mark against the tent the bird came from.

Rounds of birds tent 4 is dead

If some living man in the end chalks every dead man for good, then with copying every living man does. And the copy spares the living exactly as well as the slate it was copied from. So only the top row matters: perfect, strong, in the end perfect, and in the end strong. Of these, two are needed, strong and in the end strong, because whatever works with the weaker works with the stronger.

Start with the stronger of the two. If one living man is never chalked by anyone, and nobody knows which, is that enough to agree?

  1. The dead. A dead man sends no more birds, so after his last bird arrives nobody rubs him out. The living man who in the end chalks him for good goes on sending his marks, and every living man adds them to his copy. So in the end every living man's copy chalks him for good.
  2. Never chalked, or one never chalked. A man that nobody's slate has chalked is on nobody's list of marks, so nobody copies a mark against him.
  3. In the end. Suppose from some time no living man's slate chalks a certain living man. The dead stop sending, so in the end no bird carries a mark against him. He goes on sending, and each bird from him rubs him out of every copy. So in the end no copy chalks him.∎
However many die

One man never chalked

Suppose the slates are strong: every living man in the end chalks the dead, and one man who lives, say the man at tent 2, is never chalked by anyone. Nobody knows it is tent 2. Can the four agree? They can, however many of them die, even three.

Each man keeps a tally of sightings: a row for each tent, holding that tent's sighting if the man has learned it, and blank if not. At first it holds only the man's own.

Spreading, three rounds

Send what is new

In each round a man sends every tent the sightings learned for the first time in the round before (in the first round, the man's own). The man waits for that round's bird from every tent except a tent chalked, and writes any new sightings into the tally.

Comparing

Send the whole tally

The man sends every tent the whole tally, and waits in the same way. Any row that is blank in any tally received is blanked in the man's own.

Committing

First filled row

The man commits to the answer in the first row of the tally that is not blank, taking the rows in number order. Scroll to watch a night run.

1 · At dusk

Each tally holds one row

Each man's tally holds the man's own sighting, A for attack or H for hold. The man at tent 2 will never be chalked, though nobody knows it. Tent 4 is about to be killed.

2 · Round one

A bird reaches only one tent

Tent 4's bird reaches only tent 3 before tent 4 is killed. Tent 1 chalks tent 3 and does not wait for its bird, and tent 3 chalks tent 1. Tent 3's tally now holds tent 4's sighting.

3 · Round two

Chalk hides a sighting

Tents 1 and 2 chalk tent 3 and do not wait for its bird. So tent 4's sighting, which only tent 3 holds, goes no further. Tent 3 waits for everyone, and learns tent 1's sighting.

4 · Round three

Nobody is chalked

Every bird is taken in, and nothing more is new. Tents 1 and 2 hold rows 1 to 3. Tent 3 alone holds a fourth row, which tent 2 never heard.

5 · Comparing

One row is blanked

Each man sends the whole tally. Tent 3 receives tent 2's tally with no row 4, and blanks row 4 in its own. Everyone waited for tent 2, so every tally now matches tent 2's.

6 · Committing

All three commit alike

Each man commits to the first row that is not blank: row 1, attack. All three agree, on an answer someone sighted, though a man was killed and the slates chalked living men.

Why does it work? The whole thing turns on the man nobody chalks. Everybody waits for every one of that man's birds, so whatever the man has learned by a round, everyone who finishes the next round has learned too. After comparing, everyone who reaches the end holds exactly that man's tally, which is not empty, since it holds the man's own sighting.

Could anyone wait for ever? No: a dead man is chalked in the end, and a live man's bird comes in the end. Who is in charge? Nobody. Everybody sends everything to everybody, and the man who holds it together is one nobody can name.

Another night choose how many are killed

Each press deals new sightings, new deaths and new mistaken marks, and runs the arrangement by its rules.

But a slate that never, from dusk on, chalks one living man is a great deal to ask. What if the slates are right about someone only in the end?

Proof · that with a strong slate everyone who finishes holds the same tally

Theorem 2. With a failure detector of class S, consensus is solvable whatever the number of crashes. Here n = 4; Vp is the tally of p (a blank is ⊥); the algorithm is that of Fig. 5, §6.1, with n − 1 = 3 rounds of Phase 1, then Phase 2, then Phase 3.

  1. Every row of every tally is either blank or that tent's true sighting, since sightings are only ever copied.
  2. Let c be the man never chalked. Everyone who finishes a round received c's bird for that round, since he waited for it.
  3. After spreading, every finished tally holds all of c's. Take a sighting c first learned in round r. If r is before the last round, c sends it in round r + 1 and everyone who finishes that round has it. If r is the last round, three rounds among four men means the sighting reached c through every other man, each of whom wrote it into his tally before passing it on.
  4. After comparing, every finished tally equals c's. A row filled in c's tally is filled in every tally sent in comparing, by the last step, so nobody blanks it. A row blank in c's is blanked by everyone, since everyone waits for c's tally.
  5. c's own row is filled in c's tally and so in everyone's, so every finished tally has a first filled row, the same in all. Everyone who commits, commits to that tent's sighting.
  6. Nobody waits for ever, since a dead man is in the end chalked by every living man and a live man's birds all arrive. So every living man commits.∎
Slates right only in the end

In the end, one man spared

Suppose less: the slates are only in the end strong. For as long as the slates like, every man may be chalked by everyone. Only from some moment nobody sees is one living man left alone. The four can still agree, provided more than half of them live. With four men, one may die.

They take turns. Each round belongs to one tent, in number order: 1, 2, 3, 4, then 1 again. The round number decides whose turn it is, not anyone's command, as on the windy nights.* Each man carries an estimate, an answer, which starts as the sighting, and beside it the round in which the man last took it up.

  • (1) Every man sends the round's owner the estimate and the round it was taken up.
  • (2) The owner waits for three of these, its own included, and takes the estimate taken up most recently. The owner sends it to every tent.
  • (3) Every man waits for the owner's estimate, or until the man chalks the owner. If the estimate comes, the man takes it up, notes this round, and sends the owner agreed. If the owner is chalked first, the man sends not agreed.
  • (4) The owner waits for three replies. If all three say agreed, the owner sends every tent a parting bird naming the estimate.
  • Committing. A man who takes in a parting bird for the first time sends it on to every tent, then commits to its answer.

Rounds by turns choose a night

Choose a night.

Why do they never commit to two answers? Take the first round in which three men sent agreed, say on hold. Those three took up hold in that round. Any later owner waits for three estimates, and any three out of four share a man with those three, so the owner sees at least one hold taken up that recently or later. Take the estimate taken up most recently, and by the same argument round after round, it is hold. So every later owner sends hold, and every parting bird names hold.

Why do they commit? Nobody waits for ever: a dead owner is in the end chalked, and three men live to reply. From some moment one living man is never chalked. When that man's turn next comes, the man gathers three estimates, everyone waits for the estimate, nobody chalks the owner, all say agreed, and the parting bird goes out.

While the slates are still chalking the living, rounds fail: a man chalks a live owner whose bird is slow, says not agreed, and the round comes to nothing. Failing is always safe. The chalk can cost time; it can never cost agreement.

A strong slate bore any number of deaths; this one needs more than half alive. Is that a weakness of the arrangement, or would every arrangement need it?

Proof · that the turns never commit anyone to two answers, and in the end commit everyone to one

Theorem 3. With a failure detector of class ◇S, consensus is solvable if fewer than n/2 processes crash. Here n = 4, so three is ⌈(n + 1)/2⌉; the algorithm is the rotating coordinator of Fig. 6, §6.2, and the parting bird is Reliable Broadcast of the decision (Fig. 4). Here e is an estimate, r a round, and q a process.

  1. No two answers. Let r be the first round in which three men sent agreed to its owner, on estimate e. Each of them took up e in round r. Show by induction that every owner of a round k ≥ r who sends an estimate sends e. For k = r it is so. For later k, the owner heard from three men; one of them is among the three of round r, and reported an estimate taken up in round r or later. So the most recent round t among the three satisfies r ≤ t < k, and the estimate taken up in round t was sent by that round's owner, which by induction was e.
  2. Every parting bird was first sent by an owner who had three agreed in a round no earlier than r, so every parting bird names e, and everyone who commits, commits to e. Every estimate is someone's sighting, so e was sighted.
  3. Committing. If anyone living takes in a parting bird, he sends it on before committing, so every living man takes it in and commits. Suppose instead nobody living ever commits. No living man is stuck for ever in any round: three men live to send estimates and replies, and a man waiting on an owner either gets his estimate or, if the owner is dead, in the end chalks him.
  4. From some time, one living man q is chalked by nobody, and the dead are all dead. Take a later round whose owner is q. He gathers three estimates and sends one; everyone waits for it, since nobody chalks him; three reply agreed; he sends a parting bird. That contradicts the supposition.∎
Would every arrangement need it?

Why more than half, then

Every arrangement would. The need is real, and it holds even for slates in the end perfect, which in the end chalk the dead and nobody else. Suppose some arrangement carried the four past two deaths, and run it on three nights.

Three nights pairs: tents 1 and 2, tents 3 and 4

Each line is one tent, and each ring further out is a later moment. A hollow dot is a man who has not committed; a filled dot is one who has, blue for hold and red for attack. A cross is a dead man.

If two of the four may die, no arrangement meets the three demands, even with slates in the end perfect. What is the difference from a strong slate? When the slate is right. A strong slate never chalks one living man, from dusk, and that man holds the four together from the start. A slate that is right only in the end may be wrong about everyone for as long as the arrangement needs it to be right. While it is, the four are two pairs, each able to believe the other dead.

So a slate in the end strong, with more than half alive, is enough. But where does such a slate come from, and what has it left unsaid?

Proof · that slates in the end perfect cannot carry the four past two deaths

Theorem 5. Consensus cannot be solved using a failure detector of class ◇P if f ≥ n/2, where f is the number of processes that may crash. Here n = 4 and f = 2; the three nights are the runs R₀, R₁ and RA, and A and B are times defined in the steps.

  1. Suppose some arrangement does. Divide the four into pairs: Numbers 1 and 2, Numbers 3 and 4.
  2. The first night. All sighted hold. Numbers 3 and 4 are dead from dusk, and the other two chalk them all night. That slate is in the end perfect. Numbers 1 and 2 commit, within some time A, and to hold, since only hold was sighted.
  3. The second night. The same with pairs and answers swapped: Numbers 3 and 4 commit to attack within some time B.
  4. The third night. Numbers 1 and 2 sighted hold, Numbers 3 and 4 attack. Nobody dies. Every bird between the pairs sits in the pines until after A and B, and until then each pair chalks the other; after that, all birds arrive and all chalk is rubbed out. That slate is in the end perfect too.
  5. Until then each pair sees exactly what it saw on its own night, and commits: one pair to hold, the other to attack. That contradicts the first demand.∎
Granted, not earned

What the slate does not say

The slate's promises are granted, not earned. On a night when no bird has a longest flight, and nothing more is known, no man can keep a slate that is even in the end weak. If one could, the four could agree on that night, and they cannot.*

The slates of this book are an assumption about the hill, stated as a promise about chalk.

Can a slate ever be kept? On a windy night, yes. Where a wind blows for a while and then drops, and in still air every bird comes within a glass,* a man can keep a slate in the end perfect. Every man sends a bird to every tent, again and again, to say the sender is alive. A man chalks a tent when a glass has run out with no bird from it. When a bird from it comes after all, the man rubs out the mark and waits longer for that tent next time.

A slate kept on a windy night one watched man

Each cell is one moment, later to the right. Blue cells are the windy part of the night, when a bird may take any time. A dot is a bird taken in, a cross is a mark standing against the watched man, and a loop is a mark rubbed out.

The delays are drawn at random for the picture; the rule the watcher follows is the one in the text.

Once the wind has dropped, a live man's birds come in time once the waiting is long enough, so in the end nobody living is chalked. A dead man's birds stop, so the dead man is chalked for good. The slate is a way of saying what the wind gives, without saying anything about wind.

Must it be right to be safe?

No

If the slates break every promise, chalking the living for ever and never chalking the dead, the two arrangements may never commit anyone. They never commit two men to different answers. Only committing is lost.

The weakest slate that will do

In the end weak

In the end weak, with more than half alive, is enough. It is also necessary: any slate with which the four could agree can be made into one in the end weak. That was proved separately, and is not proved here.

Many things to decide

The same slates

If the four had to decide one after another, and take them in the same order, the same slates would do, and nothing weaker: agreeing once and agreeing on an order can each be turned into the other.

What follows from committing? Nothing. The four attack together or hold together. What happens then is not the subject of this book.

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Tushar Deepak Chandra and Sam Toueg, Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems. Journal of the ACM 43, 2 (March 1996), 225–267. The eight classes, the reduction from weak to strong completeness, consensus with S and with ◇S, the majority lower bound, and the equivalence with atomic broadcast.

The ideas, plainly. Consensus is impossible in an asynchronous system with even one crash, because a crashed process cannot be told from a slow one. Chandra and Toueg add an unreliable failure detector: a module at each process that lists the processes it suspects, and may be wrong. They classify detectors by completeness (crashed processes are eventually suspected, by every or by some correct process) and accuracy (correct processes are not suspected: never, or eventually; all of them, or at least one).

Weak completeness can be turned into strong by gossiping suspicions, so four classes matter. With S, where some correct process is never suspected, consensus tolerates any number of crashes, by relaying proposals for n − 1 rounds. With ◇S, where some correct process is eventually never suspected, consensus needs a majority of correct processes and uses a rotating coordinator; safety holds however badly the detector behaves, and progress returns once it behaves. The majority is necessary even with ◇P, by a partition argument.

A failure detector packages timing assumptions as a statement about suspicions. In partially synchronous systems, growing timeouts implement ◇P; the detector lets algorithms be written without mentioning time at all. ◇W is the weakest detector for consensus, and consensus is equivalent to atomic broadcast.
