First, in machine terms

Replacing a leader that may lie

A group of computers keeps one log in the same order, led by one machine at a time, and some of them may lie.

  • Replacing the leader is costly. When a leader stalls, the group must change it, and in earlier protocols a change costs far more than an ordinary step.
  • Every machine tells every machine. The messages in one step grow with every pair of machines.
  • HotStuff (Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta and Abraham, 2019): votes go only to the leader, and one more phase lets a new leader take over as cheaply as an old one.

This edition tells it with four bandits on a hilltop and their trained rats.

The arrangement so far

What a change of job cost

Each entry in the loot scroll is numbered by whoever holds the job, echoed twice, and written in the same order at every honest post. When the holder stalls, the job passes.*

What does that arrangement cost? Two things.

First cost

Every man writes to every man

Each echo goes from each man to all the others. With four men that is a dozen rats a stage. With forty it would be over fifteen hundred.

Second cost

A change of job is heavy

The new holder must gather requests from three men, each carrying sealed proof of every entry readied at their post, and send all of it on, so that each man can check the new numbering.* A change costs far more than an ordinary entry, and the more often the job passes, the more that matters.

The crown, the scroll, the seals, the wind and the four men all stay. Only the procedure changes.

Could the job pass at every entry, whether or not anyone suspects its holder, with a change of holder costing no more than an entry? Start with the rats: must every man write to every man?

One stage, two ways

Votes to one man, bundles to all

No. Instead of every man echoing to every man, each man sends their sealed vote to the holder alone.

When the holder has three matching votes, they tie them into a bundle and send the bundle to everyone. Why trust a bundle? Three seals prove that three men voted, and so, with one liar, that at least two honest men did.

A stage costs rats in step with the number of men, not with every pair of them. On the crown a bundle is three seals tied together.

Fewer rats, then. But the heavy part was the change of holder. How can a new holder pick up where the last left off without gathering everyone's proofs?

The new holder asks for one thing

Three stages and a lock

By having every man send the new holder their latest bundle, and nothing more. For that to be enough, entries now form a chain: each entry names the one before it, so an entry stands only with everything behind it. Two entries conflict if neither lies on the other's chain.

Each man keeps two bundles: the latest first bundle they have seen, and their lock, the latest second bundle they have voted on. A term goes like this. Scroll slowly.

1 · Starting

Every man sends the holder their latest first bundle

The holder waits for three, takes the latest of them, and proposes a new entry on top of the entry it names, sending the proposal and that bundle to all.

2 · First vote

A man votes if the proposal is safe for them

They vote for the proposal if it lies on the chain of the entry they are locked on, or if the bundle it carries is from a later term than their lock.

3 · Second vote

The first bundle

The holder ties three first votes into a first bundle and sends it. Every man keeps it and votes again.

4 · Third vote

The second bundle, and the lock

The holder ties three second votes into a second bundle and sends it. Every man locks on it and votes again.

5 · Standing

The third bundle

The holder ties three third votes into a third bundle and sends it. Every man writes the entry and everything behind it into their copy.

6 · Moving on

A sandglass runs out

If a man's sandglass runs out before the term ends, they move to the next term and send its holder their latest first bundle.

Where the term is in the paper

The steps above are the paper's Basic HotStuff: a first, second and third vote are the prepare, pre-commit and commit votes, and a first, second and third bundle are prepareQC, precommitQC and commitQC.

The rule for the first vote

Two halves, two jobs

Why does the rule for the first vote have two halves? Each has its own job.

First half

Lies on my locked entry's chain

This keeps the scroll safe: a locked man will not help a conflicting entry forward.

Second half

Carries a later bundle than my lock

This keeps the scroll moving: a man locked on something the rest have gone past can be shown that they have, and let go.

The first promise

Never two entries

Letting a lock go can never let a conflicting entry stand.

Within a term, can two bundles of the same stage vouch for conflicting entries? No: each needs three seals out of four, the two threes share an honest man, and an honest man votes once a stage a term.* And across terms? There the lock does the work.

Proof — that the locks keep conflicting entries from both standing

Theorem 2. Conflicting nodes cannot both be committed by correct replicas. Here W and B are conflicting entries (nodes); a, b and s are terms (views); and r is an honest man (a correct replica). A third bundle is a commitQC, a first bundle a prepareQC.

  1. Suppose W stands on a third bundle from term a, and a conflicting B on one from term b. They are not the same term, so say a is earlier.
  2. B's standing needed a first bundle for B, or for something on B's chain, in term b. Among all first bundles from terms after a, up to b, for entries that conflict with W, take the one from the earliest term, s.
  3. W's third bundle in term a and that first bundle in term s each carry three seals, so they share an honest man, r. A man casts his third vote only after locking, so in term a, r locked on W's second bundle. Nothing between a and s can have moved his lock off W's chain, since that would need a first bundle for a conflicting entry earlier than s.
  4. So in term s, r was locked on W, and the proposal conflicted with W. It carried a bundle from no later than term a, again because s is the earliest. Neither half of the rule lets r vote for it. But he did vote, since his seal is in the term-s bundle. Contradiction.∎

Safe with three stages. But two echoes were enough before. Why add a third?

What two stages would do

The hidden lock

Try two stages, and see what goes wrong. A man locks as soon as they see a first bundle, and an entry stands on the next.

In some term an entry reaches its first bundle, and only one man, say bandit 3, sees it before the others' sandglasses run out; they lock on it. The next holder hears from three men, and bandit 3 is not one of them, since the holder need only wait for three. None of them has that bundle, so the holder proposes an entry that conflicts with bandit 3's lock.

The two other honest men vote for it. Bandit 3 refuses: it conflicts with their lock and carries no later bundle. That is two votes, not three. The sandglasses run out, and just then a liar adds a vote, so the holder forms a first bundle that one man sees and locks on. The next term begins the same way. Could it go on for ever? It could.

The new holder listens two stages

The holder waits for the first three answers. Pick the man whose answer is slowest, so that the holder does not wait for it.

Pick the slowest man.

Is there a remedy with two stages? Make each new holder wait out a full sandglass before proposing, long enough, once the wind drops, to be sure of hearing every man's latest bundle. That works, but it ties every change of holder to the length of a sandglass, not to the run of a rat.

What does the third stage change? With three stages the lock cannot hide. A man locks only on a second bundle, and a second bundle exists only because three men voted on a first bundle and kept it. So a lock at any honest post means at least two honest men hold the matching first bundle. A new holder who hears from any three men hears from at least one of them. They learn the latest bundle without waiting out anything.

The second promise

Going at the rats' pace

Going at the Rats' Pace

Once the wind has dropped, a term whose holder is honest, with every honest man in it long enough, ends with an entry standing, and it needs to wait only for the first three answers at each stage, never for a full sandglass.

Proof — that an honest holder after the wind drops gets their entry through

Lemma 3, Theorem 4 (optimistic responsiveness). After GST, in a view with a correct leader and every correct replica in it long enough, the leader obtains a highQC at least as high as every correct replica's lockedQC, every correct replica votes for its proposal, and each phase completes on the first n − f answers, so the proposal commits. A second bundle is a precommitQC, and a lock is lockedQC.

  1. Take the latest lock held by any honest man: a second bundle. It exists because three men voted on its first bundle, at least two of them honest, and each keeps it.
  2. The holder hears the latest first bundles of three men, which include at least one of those two. So he takes a bundle at least as late as every honest lock.
  3. Every honest man then votes for the proposal. A man whose lock is from the same term as the bundle the holder took is locked on that very entry, since one term has only one second bundle, and the proposal lies on its chain. Every other honest man's lock is earlier, and the proposal carries a later bundle. In still air all votes and bundles arrive, and each stage completes on three answers. So the entry stands.∎

How do the honest men come to be in the same term for long enough once the wind drops? The holders pass in a fixed order by term number, and each man doubles their sandglass whenever a term ends with nothing standing, as before.* In the end the sandglasses overlap for long enough under an honest holder. The paper keeps this part, which it calls the pacemaker, apart from the rest: it affects only whether entries stand, never which.

Each entry now waits three stages, where two echoes did before. Must the scroll then take in entries more slowly?

The stages run on top of one another

A new holder every entry

No. Each entry still waits three stages, but the three stages all look alike: the holder sends something, the men vote, the holder bundles the votes. So they can be run on top of one another.

Each term the job passes to the next man, who proposes a new entry carrying the latest first bundle. A vote for it is also a second vote for the entry before it, and a third vote for the one before that.

Terms in a row no term yet

Each line is one man, and each ring further out is a later moment. The numbered dot is a term's proposal. The arc into it is the bundle it carries for the entry directly before.

How does a man tell what stands? They read the chain behind each new proposal. If it carries a bundle for its direct parent, the parent has its first bundle. If that parent carries one for its own direct parent, the grandparent is ready to lock on. If the grandparent carries one for its own direct parent too, that entry, the one behind all three, stands. Where a holder failed and a term left nothing, the chain has a gap, and counting starts again.

The job now passes at every entry, suspected or not. Is that costly? No, for the same reason a change of holder is cheap: a new holder only proposes on the latest bundle they hear of, like any other.

A new holder every entry, at the rats' pace. What has not changed?

Five questions

What it does not say

  • Does anything stand before the wind drops? Nothing. Order holds in any wind. Entries stand only once it has dropped, as for any arrangement.
  • Is the bundle always small? Only with a threshold seal, where each man holds a piece and any three pieces combine into one seal that anyone can check. Then the bundle is the size of a single seal however many men there are, and each stage costs a number of seals that grows only in step with the number of men. The crown's rings are ordinary seals and make a bundle of three, so the saving here is in rats, not in the size of what they carry.
  • Is an entry slower? It takes one more stage than before to stand. Chained, the stages overlap, so a new entry still stands at every term.
  • Does anyone learn who lies? No, and a liar may still file nonsense that stands in order.
  • What follows from any entry? Nothing.

In the machine room

A leader costs only linear traffic per decision, and a new leader can take over as cheaply as an old one.

HotStuff gets there by having replicas send votes only to the leader, who aggregates them into a quorum certificate, and by chaining three phases of such certificates so that leader replacement is just another round. Once the network behaves, it runs as fast as the network allows.

The paper behind this book

Maofan Yin, Dahlia Malkhi, Michael K. Reiter, Guy Golan Gueta and Ittai Abraham, HotStuff: BFT Consensus with Linearity and Responsiveness. Proceedings of the 38th ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), 2019. — Basic and Chained HotStuff, the safety and liveness arguments, the two-phase liveness failure, and the comparison with PBFT, Tendermint and Casper.

The ideas. HotStuff is a Byzantine state machine replication protocol for n = 3f + 1 replicas under partial synchrony, like PBFT, with two properties together: a new leader costs no more than an ordinary step (linear view change), and after the network stabilizes a correct leader proceeds at the speed of actual messages rather than a timeout (optimistic responsiveness).

Votes go only to the leader, who combines n − f of them into a quorum certificate, one threshold signature, so each phase is linear. There are three phases where PBFT had two. Replicas lock on the second certificate, and vote for a proposal if it extends their lock or carries a certificate from a later view. Because a lock implies that f + 1 correct replicas hold the matching first certificate, a new leader hearing from any n − f replicas learns it without waiting; with two phases a lock can hide, and progress needs a wait for the maximum delay.

Because the phases are identical, they pipeline: every view proposes a new block, the leader rotates every view, and a block commits when three consecutive views extend it.
