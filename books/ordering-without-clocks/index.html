Ordering Without Clocks

If no two computers agree on the exact time, how can they agree on what happened first? And when two events happen far apart, how can anyone tell whether one caused the other, or if they happened completely independently?

To find out, we travel to an ancient Greek island where three trading houses must keep their trades in order—even when heavy clouds make their sundials useless.

To find out, we follow the algorithms step by step. Three processes, each with its own unsynchronized clock, must put their events in an order that never places an effect before its cause.

Comparing two columns row by row settles the clerk's doubt completely. One entry came before another if and only if every number in its column is less than or equal to the corresponding row in the other's column (and the two columns aren't identical). Two entries are unrelated if and only if one column is larger in one row while the other column is larger in another. Where a single tally could not tell coincidence from cause and effect, the column proves which is which.Comparing two vectors entry by entry settles the doubt completely. One event happened before another if and only if every entry of its vector is less than or equal to the corresponding entry of the other’s (and the two vectors aren’t identical). Two events are concurrent if and only if one vector is larger in one entry while the other is larger in another. Where a single timestamp could not tell coincidence from cause and effect, the vector proves which is which.

In principle, such synchronized physical clocks can be built: each house keeps a steady clock that barely drifts, every slip carries the sender's clock reading, and any house that falls behind nudges its clock forward. In computing

Even without a shared clock, networked machines can agree on a single order of events that never puts an effect before its cause.

A single counter per machine cannot tell related events from unrelated ones—but keeping one counter for every machine can.

Lamport's logical clocks, together with the vector clocks of Fidge and Mattern, remain the foundation for how distributed systems put events in order today.
