# Hellenistic Greek tools, crafts, technology and everyday objects

Scope note: only Wikipedia pages were retrievable in this pass (about 18 fetches; several URLs 404'd). Sourced findings are therefore thin. Items marked "background knowledge" are NOT verified against a fetched source and are kept in Inferences/Gaps only. Nothing below was checked in Oleson, Landels, Vitruvius or museum databases.

## Building tools and machines (chisels, cranes, polyspastos, clamps, quarrying, roofing)

### Takeaway
Greek masonry used dry-laid ashlar and column drums held by bronze pins and lead-sealed iron clamps/dowels. Cranes date to c. 515 BC, long before the Hellenistic period, and the polyspastos/treadwheel evidence found is Roman. Hellenistic-specific building-tool detail was not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Column drums rest on each other without mortar, sometimes centred with a bronze pin. Load-bearing blocks were fixed with iron clamps, dowels and rods of wood, bronze or iron set in lead to minimise corrosion. — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture)
- Fine ashlar was laid in regular courses with large blocks to minimise joints. Blocks were rough-hewn at the quarry, then cut and bedded precisely. Marble from Paros and Naxos, and limestone, were the main stones. — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture)
- Fired ceramic roof tiles replaced thatch c. 700-650 BC. Tiles were up to 90 x 70 cm, 3-4 cm thick, about 30 kg each, so walls had to be strong. (Earlier than the Hellenistic period but still the standard pan-and-cover system.) — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture)
- Greek crane dated c. 515 BC (archaic; flagged as earlier than the Hellenistic period). It let small teams work efficiently, and winches were added later for heavier loads. — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_technology)
- Roman polyspaston crane (Roman-era, flagged): four men could lift 3,000 kg, and a treadwheel doubled the load to 6,000 kg with half the crew. — [Wikipedia: Treadwheel crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treadwheel_crane)
- Treadwheel is a large wooden wheel on a central shaft with a treadway wide enough for two men walking side by side (medieval description; also usable as a visual for the Roman type). Cranes lift vertically and rarely had brakes. — [Wikipedia: Treadwheel crane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treadwheel_crane)

### Inferences
- For Hellenistic scenes, a crane with a windlass/capstan and multi-sheave block (polyspastos) is plausible. The treadwheel is attested only for Roman times in the retrieved source, so mark it as a later anachronism risk (uncertain).
- Background knowledge, unverified here: lifting bosses and lewis holes on blocks, lead poured into clamp channels, point/claw/flat chisels, mallets, plumb bobs, and the set square/compass/level (chorobates in Vitruvius). Check Vitruvius 10 and Oleson before use.

### Gaps
- No source retrieved for scaffolding, mortar use, specific chisel types, levels, or quarry methods. Vitruvius book 10 (Perseus) and Oleson/Landels were not accessed.

## Agriculture (plough, presses, mills, irrigation)

### Takeaway
Archimedes' screw and the Philo-described water wheel are the key Hellenistic hydraulic devices. Beyond that, agricultural detail was not retrievable.

### Cited Findings
- Archimedes' screw is first mentioned between 250 and 220 BC (Moschion, bilge pump on a Syracusan ship). Its origin is debated, including whether Archimedes met it in Egypt. It is a helical screw inside a hollow pipe. — [Wikipedia: Archimedes' screw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_screw)
- Diodorus describes screw irrigation in the Nile Delta. Vitruvius (c. 27-22 BC, Roman-era) documents an eight-bladed wooden screw. — [Wikipedia: Archimedes' screw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_screw)
- Water wheel is first described by Philo of Byzantium (c. 280-220 BC), and the earliest mention of a water mill (c. 250 BC) is also in Philo's Pneumatics. — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_technology)
- The saddle quern is the oldest type, worked by pushing and pulling a handstone. Rotary beehive/disc querns and animal-powered "Pompeian" mills are later or Roman. — [Wikipedia: Quern-stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotary_quern)
- Wikipedia's olive-press article shows a reconstruction of an ancient Greek olive extractor from Dion and a lever-and-weights press (4th-8th c. AD, so Late Antique, flagged), but gives no Hellenistic dating. — [Wikipedia: Olive press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_press)

### Inferences
- Background knowledge, unverified: beam press with stone weights, screw press (attributed to Hero/Hellenistic period), the ard plough, hoes, mattocks, sickles, shadoof, hopper-rotary mill (Olynthus, Greek Mediterranean, 4th-3rd c. BC), crops (wheat, barley, olive, vine, fig) and livestock (sheep, goats, oxen, donkeys). All need confirming.

### Gaps
- No sourced detail on the ard plough, hoes, sickles, wine presses, threshing, or shadoof. No Hellenistic dating for beam or screw presses.

## Crafts (pottery, metalwork, glass, textiles, wood, leather, shipbuilding)

### Takeaway
Hellenistic pottery was industrialised and standardised, led by mould-made relief wares and West Slope decoration. The warp-weighted loom with paired weavers is the textile base. Firing was a three-phase process.

### Cited Findings
- In the Hellenistic period vase-painting lost importance, and pottery became an industry with standardisation and some lowering of quality. Metalwork and luxury arts overshadowed it. — [Wikipedia: Hellenistic pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_pottery)
- Hellenistic ware categories listed include Megarian ware, West Slope ware, Gnathia, Canosa and Centuripe wares, plus Tanagra terracotta figurines. — [Wikipedia: Hellenistic pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_pottery)
- Greek potters threw pots on the wheel and could add hand-formed or mould-made elements. — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_pottery)
- Kiln firing had three stages: oxidation (about 920-950 C, vents open), reduction (vents closed, green wood added, turning the slip black), then reoxidation (about 800-850 C). — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_pottery)
- Warp-weighted loom: warp hangs from a bar on upright poles, weighted; vase paintings show two women weaving side by side; Bronze Age loom weights found at Miletos. — [Wikipedia: Warp-weighted loom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warp-weighted_loom)
- Philo of Byzantium documented gimbal ink pots and escapements in automata. — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_technology)

### Inferences
- Background knowledge, unverified: mould-made "Megarian" bowls were made by pressing clay into a stamped mould (bowl turned on a wheel), c. 2nd c. BC onward; West Slope ware has white/yellow ivy and garland painted over a dark slip; glass was core-formed or cast/mosaic; bronze was cast by lost-wax; ships used mortise-and-tenon planking. These need real sources.

### Gaps
- Megarian bowl and West Slope Wikipedia pages 404'd. No sourced detail on glass, metalwork, leather, woodworking tools or shipbuilding.

## Everyday objects (amphorae, lamps, furniture, writing, weights, clocks, mechanisms)

### Takeaway
The best-sourced items are the Antikythera mechanism and the Tower of the Winds. Furniture facts are limited to the klismos, which declined in the Hellenistic period. Rhodian stamps, lamps, writing and scales were not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Antikythera mechanism: 2nd century BC (the clock-tower page puts it c. 100-70 BC in another article). Case 34 x 18 x 9 cm, wooden case, hand-cut bronze gears with triangular teeth, about 30-35 gears, largest about 13 cm with 223 teeth. Five rear dials (Metonic, Saros, Games, Callippic, Exeligmos) and a half-black, half-white moon-phase ball. Found 1901, now in Athens, in 82 fragments. — [Wikipedia: Antikythera mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antikythera_mechanism); date range at [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_technology)
- Ctesibius (fl. 285-222 BC) made a double-action piston pump and water clocks with alarms. Heron (1st c. AD, Roman-era, flagged) made the fire hose, holy-water vending machine and steam temple doors. — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_technology)
- Tower of the Winds, Athens: octagonal marble, about 12 m tall and 8 m wide, by Andronikos of Kyrrhos, c. 50 BC (late Hellenistic), with a water clock inside, sundials outside, and a bronze Triton weathervane. — [Wikipedia: Tower of the Winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_the_Winds)
- Klismos: curved backrest and splayed, tapering legs, seat of staves morticed into the legs with cording or leather strips under cushions. It appears from the mid 5th c. BC and lost favour in the Hellenistic period (so a poor choice as the default Hellenistic chair). — [Wikipedia: Klismos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klismos)
- Amphorae have a pointed base, two handles and a narrow neck, and were stored upright in sand. A general article only; the Roman capacity of about 39 L is Roman, not Rhodian. — [Wikipedia: Amphora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphora)

### Inferences
- Background knowledge, unverified: Rhodian amphorae have a tall narrow neck with two high handles, one stamped with a magistrate/fabricant name and rose or Helios-head emblem; wine amphora were pitch-lined and stoppered with clay or cork; lamps are wheel-made or mould-made with a nozzle; klinai are timber with bronze/ivory fittings; writing used papyrus rolls, wax tablets, styluses and reed pens with carbon ink; steelyard and balance scales; Ctesibius' clepsydra with a float and pointer. All need checking.

### Gaps
- No source retrieved for Rhodian stamp conventions, lamps, tables, baskets, scales/weights, papyrus, wax tablets or ink.
