Changing Leaders Among Liars

When some machines in a replicated system may lie, replacing a stalled leader has traditionally been complex and expensive. Can a new leader take over as cheaply as any ordinary step, and move at the speed of the network rather than waiting out a timer?

To find out, we follow four bandits on a hilltop who rotate the job of keeping their loot scroll at every single entry, with each leader gathering the others' wax seals into a compact bundle instead of everyone writing to everyone else.

To find out, we follow the protocol view by view. Four replicas, one of which may be faulty, rotate the leader on every single block, with each leader aggregating the others’ signatures into one compact certificate instead of everyone messaging everyone else.

After Maofan Yin, Dahlia Malkhi, Michael K. Reiter, Guy Golan Gueta and Ittai Abraham, HotStuff: BFT Consensus with Linearity and Responsiveness, 2019

The rule for the first vote

Two halves, two jobs

The voting rule for Step 2 (the paper's safeNode predicate) has two halves because it must balance two competing duties: protecting entries that have already stood (safety) and freeing posts whose locks are out of date (liveness). A bandit votes for a proposal if either half holds:The voting rule for step 2 (the paper’s safeNode predicate) has two halves because it must balance two competing duties: protecting blocks that have already committed (safety) and freeing replicas whose locks are out of date (liveness). Second half · Liveness branch

Within a single term, two bundles of the same stage can never vouch for conflicting entries. Every bundle requires three seals out of four, any two sets of three out of four share at least two bandits (at least one of whom is honest), and an honest bandit votes only once per stage in any given term.*Within a single view, two certificates of the same phase can never vouch for conflicting blocks. Across different terms, the locks prevent a split. Suppose an entry stands in some term. Because it reached a third bundle in that term, three bandits cast third votes for it, and a bandit casts a third vote only after setting their lock on its second bundle in that term. Thus at least two honest bandits are locked on that entry at the end of the term. For any conflicting entry to ever get a first bundle in a later term, three bandits would have to cast first votes for it, which requires the vote of at least one of those two honest bandits locked on it. A leader uses only linear message traffic per decision, and a new leader can take over as cheaply as an old one.

HotStuff achieves this by having replicas send votes only to the leader, who aggregates them into a quorum certificate, and by chaining three uniform phases of certificates so that leader replacement is just another round. The ideas. HotStuff is a Byzantine fault-tolerant state-machine replication protocol for n = 3f + 1 replicas under partial synchrony that achieves two properties simultaneously: linear view change (a new leader takes over using only O(n) messages and authenticators, no more than an ordinary round) and optimistic responsiveness (once the network stabilizes, a correct leader drives consensus at actual network speed rather than waiting out a worst-case timeout Δ).

Instead of all-to-all broadcasts, replicas send signed votes solely to the leader, who combines n − f = 2f + 1 votes into a single quorum certificate (QC, implemented with threshold signatures) and broadcasts it out. Where PBFT and Tendermint use two voting phases (prepare and commit), HotStuff uses three (prepare, pre-commit, and commit). Replicas update their lock (lockedQC) only upon receiving a second-phase precommitQC, and vote for a new proposal if it extends their locked node or carries a prepareQC from a higher view than their lock (safeNode). Because any replica's lock on a precommitQC implies that at least f + 1 correct replicas hold the matching prepareQC, a new leader hearing from any n − f replicas is guaranteed to learn of a prepareQC at least as high as every correct replica's lock without waiting for a timeout. In two-phase protocols, by contrast, a single replica can lock on a first-phase QC that no other replica has seen, creating a "hidden lock" that forces either a liveness deadlock or a mandatory Δ timeout wait on every leader change.

Because all three phases of Basic HotStuff have identical structure, Chained HotStuff pipelines them across views: every view proposes a new block carrying a single QC, the leader can rotate on every view at no extra cost, and a block commits as soon as it is followed by a contiguous Three-Chain of blocks.
