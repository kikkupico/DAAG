Pilot · Events · Arche · The Ring Road

Ordering Without Clocks

If no two machines agree on the time, how can they agree on what happened first? And when two things happen far apart, how can anyone tell whether one led to the other or neither knew of the other?

One grey day on the ring road, told in three threads that take turns: House 3's grain and the orders for it, a clerk at her book, and a goatherd on the mountain. Each chapter breaks off where its thread gets hard, and the thread picks up again two chapters later.

Chapter I · The Orders

Two Messengers

Three trading houses, House 1, House 2 and House 3, stand round the coast of Arche with Mount Phyle between them. Word goes between them only on written slips carried by messengers along the ring road, where no messenger can pass another, so slips from one house to another arrive in the order they were sent, after a time nobody can bound. In the trading season cloud silences the sundials, and the water-clocks of the three counting rooms keep no common hour*.

On a grey morning House 3 has grain in its storehouse, enough for one order, and both other houses need it. At House 2 the clerk writes an order for the grain, and a messenger takes it down the cutting. At House 1, which has not heard of House 2's order and could not have, Lamportios, who keeps the book, writes an order of his own, and another messenger sets off the other way round.

The houses have agreed to fill orders first come, first served. At House 3's gate the keeper waits with the storehouse tally-stick in his hand. Which order is first? The one that reaches him first? Then the house with the quicker messenger wins. The one written first? Then he would need a clock the three houses share, and the water-clocks of the counting rooms were never set together.

Late in the morning a messenger comes round the last headland at a run, and the keeper holds out his hand for the slip.

Chapter II · The Clerk's Doubt

The Clerk's Doubt

That evening at House 2, the clerk who keeps the book sits over it by lamplight. Two entries a few lines apart trouble her: an order House 2 placed for oil, and above it a slip that came in from House 1 about the price of jars. Was the order placed because of the slip? Or would House 2 have placed it anyway?

It matters. If the order followed from the slip, whatever House 1 does next with its jars may bear on it. But her book records only what happened at House 2, one line under another. It does not record what House 2 knew when it acted, still less what House 1 knew when it wrote.

She turns back a page, then another, looking for anything that would say. There is nothing. Then she looks again at House 1's slip, at the number written small in its corner, beside the number in the margin of her own order.

Chapter III · The Orders

What Came Before

The slip the keeper took at House 3's gate was House 1's. House 2's messenger came in a little after, having been held up behind a mule on the lower cutting. The keeper had the two orders in his hand and grain for one. Should House 1 have it because its messenger was quicker?

Lamportios had seen this coming, and had changed the question. The houses had been asking which order came first in absolute time, which no house can see. What their business needs is narrower: an order must never be served before one that could have led to it. And whether one thing could have led to another the houses can see, because the only way news moves on Arche is by slip. One event came before another (written a → b) when:

The three rules of coming-before (1) They happened in the same house, and the book of that house has the first written above the second.
(2) The first is a house dispatching a slip, and the second is another house receiving that same slip.
(3) Or there is some third event that the first came before, and which came before the second.

If none of the rules joins two events they are unrelated (a ∥ b): neither could have known of the other. The two orders in the keeper's hand are unrelated. The sun may have seen one before the other; nothing the houses hold can tell them which.

To make this something clerks could calculate with, every house keeps a tally, written on every slip and raised by two rules.

The two rules of the tally (1) A house advances its tally by at least one between any two entries in its own book.
(2) When dispatching a slip, a house writes its current tally on it. When receiving a slip, a house sets its own tally higher than the number on the slip, and never lowers it, before entering the arrival in its book.
The Clock Condition

Whenever event a came before event b, the tally of a is strictly smaller than the tally of b:
a → b ⟹ C(a) < C(b)

The converse is false. A smaller tally does not mean an earlier event: two houses that have heard nothing from each other may give tallies 2 and 3 to unrelated events. The keeper knows this. He turns both slips over to read the numbers in their corners. House 1's order is marked 4. House 2's is marked 4 as well.

Chapter IV · The Mountain

Over the Mountain

At House 3 that same morning, a trader concludes a sale in the counting room, whose busy tally has reached 9 by the time the sale is entered. He wants House 1 to know at once, and the road is slow, so he pays a goatherd to take the news over Mount Phyle by the smugglers' tracks. It is not a way anybody uses by choice: a man who takes it may take two hours or two days, or never come down*.

This time the goatherd is lucky. By midday he is at House 1, and House 1, on the strength of what he says, issues an order at once. It has had a quiet morning; its tally stands at 3, and the order goes into the book under a smaller number than the sale that caused it.

By the tally's own rules nothing is wrong, since its rules touch only slips and the goatherd carried none. Down on the coast, House 3's messenger with the official letter about the sale is still on the road, somewhere behind a slow cart, and nobody at House 1 has any idea he is coming.

Chapter V · The Orders

Breaking Ties

Two orders, grain for one, and the same tally on both. A partial order cannot decide who gets the grain, and “these two orders are unrelated” does not tell the keeper whose order to fill. He needs a sequence that decides between every pair, that every house works out the same way, and that never contradicts a chain of slips.

The houses had settled it beforehand, by the plainest means available: compare tallies, and where they are equal, compare the houses' numbers, House 1 before House 2 before House 3.

The rule of precedence An event a at house i is earlier than event b at house j (written a ⇒ b) if:
(1) C(a) < C(b);
(2) or C(a) = C(b) and i is less than j.
Proof — that precedence is a total order extending coming-before
  1. Total comparability. For any two distinct events a and b: if their tallies differ, clause (1) decides. If their tallies are equal and they occurred at the same house, rule (1) of Chapter III already gave the later one a higher tally, so they must belong to different houses, whose numbers differ, and clause (2) decides.
  2. Antisymmetry and transitivity. Compare by the pair (C(e), house). This is the standard lexicographical ordering of pairs drawn from two total orders, which is strictly total and transitive.
  3. Consistency with causality. If a → b, then by the Clock Condition C(a) < C(b) strictly. Precedence places a earlier by clause (1) alone; house numbers are never consulted. Hence a → b ⟹ a ⇒ b.∎

So the keeper fills House 1's order. Did House 1 really ask first? Neither the keeper nor anyone else can say. The rule replaces the question with an answer every house reads the same way, and it is fair in the one sense the houses can check: where one order led to another they are filled in that sequence, and no house wins by owning a quicker messenger.

Before the day is out there is a second contest. The island keeps one common storehouse, cut into the rock beside the road, and House 3 wants to load from it, and so does House 2; and House 2's request is already on the road, in a messenger's pouch, when House 3's clerk reaches for the storehouse key.

Chapter VI · The Clerk's Doubt

The Column

The clerk at House 2 has the two numbers side by side: the tally in the corner of House 1's slip, and the tally in the margin of her order. The order's is larger. Does that mean the order followed from the slip? She knows it does not. The tally says only that her order was not made before the slip could have reached it. Joined or unconnected, the numbers would look the same.

Why can't a single number say more? Because it mixes together everything a house has heard. When House 2 raised its tally past House 1's slip, it forgot whose number it was raised past. To remember, it would need one number for each house.

Fidgeos of House 2 and Matternos of House 3 had worked this out at the same time, and she takes up their method. Each house keeps a column of three rows, one for each house; row i is the latest count of house i's own entries that this house has heard of.

The rules of the column (1) When entering any event of its own, the dispatch of a slip included, house i increments its own row: Vi[i] := Vi[i] + 1.
(2) When sending a slip, house i stamps its full column Vi on the slip, as it stands after that increment.
(3) On receiving slip with column U, house j merges each row by taking the maximum, then increments its own row: Vj[k] := max(Vj[k], U[k]), and Vj[j] := Vj[j] + 1.

House 1's slips have begun to carry columns, and so do House 2's own entries. She copies the column from the slip onto a wax tablet, and the column of her order beneath it, and lays a ruler across the two.

Chapter VII · The Orders

The Shared Storehouse

House 3's clerk has the storehouse key in his hand. Could he simply go in, since his is the only request he knows of? That would be the trouble of the morning again: House 2's request, sent earlier, is still on the road. A keeper who let in whoever asked first would let in whoever had the shorter road.

The houses share the storehouse by five rules. Each keeps a private queue of requests, ordered by precedence.

The five rules of the storehouse (1) To request: House i writes a request under its current tally, enters it in its queue, and sends a messenger carrying it to every other house.
(2) On receiving a request: House j enters the request in its queue, advances its tally, and dispatches an acknowledgement slip back to i.
(3) To release: When done, house i removes its request from its queue and dispatches a release slip to every other house.
(4) On receiving a release: House j removes that house's request from its queue.
(5) To enter: House i enters the storehouse when its own request stands first in its queue, and it has received from every other house a slip carrying a tally greater than its request.

So House 3's clerk puts the key down. His request is first in his own queue, but he has not heard from House 2 with a tally larger than his request's, and on a road where no messenger passes another that is exactly the evidence that nothing older is trailing behind. When House 2's messenger comes in, the slip carries House 2's request, marked earlier than House 3's. House 2 loads first.

Why is the fifth rule enough? The proof is short.

Proof — that two houses never enter the storehouse at once
  1. Suppose for contradiction that House 1 and House 3 both hold the storehouse at once, on requests R₁ and R₃. Precedence is total, so one is earlier; assume R₁ ⇒ R₃.
  2. By Rule 5, when House 3 entered, it had received from House 1 some message carrying a tally T > C(R₃).
  3. House 1 dispatched R₁ to House 3 under tally C(R₁) before dispatching any message with tally T. Because the coastal road is single-file, slips from House 1 to House 3 arrive in FIFO order.
  4. Therefore, House 3 received R₁ before receiving the message with tally T. So R₁ was already in House 3's queue when House 3 entered.
  5. Since R₁ ⇒ R₃, R₃ did not stand first in House 3's queue. Rule 5 was violated, and House 3 could not have entered.∎

That evening a merchant at House 3 insists, to anyone who will listen, that by the sun House 3 asked for the storehouse before House 2 ever did. He was in the counting room when House 3's clerk wrote the request, he says, and a goatherd down from the mountain told him House 2 wrote its own only later.

Chapter VIII · The Clerk's Doubt

What the Column Sees

The clerk at House 2 reads the two columns row by row along the ruler. The slip's row for House 1 is no greater than her order's, and neither is any other row. Her order came after the slip, by a chain of slips and book lines; it could have been placed because of it. Had any row of the slip been larger, she would know the opposite: House 2 had not heard of that entry when it ordered, and the two were made in ignorance of each other.

Is that luck, or a law? It is a law: comparing columns settles causality completely.

Vector Equivalence

For any two events a and b:
a → b ⟺ V(a) < V(b)
And two events are unrelated if and only if neither column dominates:
a ∥ b ⟺ ¬(V(a) ≤ V(b)) ∧ ¬(V(b) ≤ V(a))

Proof — that vector timestamps detect causality and concurrency exactly
  1. If a occurs at house i with timestamp V(a) and a → b, there is a causal chain of local events and messages from a to b. By Rules 1–3, every step non-strictly increases all components and strictly increases the component of the executing process. Thus V(a)[i] ≤ V(b)[i]. Since at least one step strictly increments a component, V(a) < V(b).
  2. Conversely, suppose a occurs at house i and does not precede b (a ↛ b). Then no slip carrying information about a has reached b. By Rule 3, the value of row i at b reflects only events at i that causally precede b. Since a is not among them, V(b)[i] < V(a)[i]. Thus V(a) ≰ V(b).
  3. Therefore, a → b if and only if V(a) < V(b). Concurrency a ∥ b holds if and only if neither column dominates the other.∎

Where the tally could only fail to rule out a connection, the column finds it or rules it out. What is left that it cannot see? Only what never went by slip. As she blows out the lamp, the clerk thinks of the goatherds on the mountain, who carry news and no slips at all.

Chapter IX · The Mountain

Clocks That Run With the World

Late in the afternoon House 3's messenger reaches House 1 with the official letter about the sale. It is hours too late. House 1 acted on the goatherd's news long ago, and its book shows the order under tally 3, beneath the letter now entered under a larger number. Anyone reading the book would think the order came first.

Could any clock have caught the goatherd? It would have to respect every path in the world, not just slips: if one event precedes another by any path at all, its number must be smaller. No rule about slips can promise that. Only clocks that run with the world can.

Lamportios described such clocks. Each house keeps a physical clock that runs true to within a small fraction; every slip carries the sender's reading; a house that receives it sets its own clock forward, if it is behind, to that reading plus the least time the road could have taken. If messengers go down every stretch at regular hours and the road has a longest crossing, the clocks stay within some small bound of one another, and the goatherd is caught, provided that bound is smaller than his fastest crossing.

The houses cannot keep such clocks. The remedy needs a longest crossing and messengers at regular hours, and the trading season gives neither. Arche's clocks are the water-clocks of the counting rooms, and the tallies are all the order the houses have.

Nor can they answer the merchant who swore House 3 asked first. Perhaps it did, by the sun. The sequence the houses keep is their own: it respects every slip, decides the rest by a rule every house reads alike, and can neither prove him wrong nor prove him right.
