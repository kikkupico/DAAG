One Leader at a Time

Many replicated systems put a single leader in charge at a time so that operations can be ordered without dueling ballots. When that leader crashes or falls silent, how does a new leader take over without losing anything the previous leader promised?

To find out, we visit Schedia — a sister city on the eastern arm of Paxos with laws of its own — where five legislators maintain the city's law from a central podium held by one person at a time, and see how a new holder takes the podium whenever the previous one goes silent.

After Brian M. Oki and Barbara H. Liskov, Viewstamped Replication: A New Primary Copy Method to Support Highly-Available Distributed Systems, 1988

First, in machine terms

One primary, and what happens when it goes

Suppose a service is replicated across several machines so that it survives individual crashes. Instead of letting every machine propose commands, one machine acts as the leader — the primary — executing operations in order and streaming the resulting log to the other machines, the backups.

Normal operation is fast and simple, because backups only copy what the primary sends. The real difficulty comes when the primary crashes or gets cut off by the network. Worse still, in **Viewstamped Replication** — published by Brian Oki and Barbara Liskov in 1988, a year before Paxos first circulated — machines keep their logs in fast volatile memory rather than on disk, so a machine that crashes and reboots comes back having forgotten its log! Even so, two rules guarantee that no committed operation is ever lost:

  • An operation is committed as soon as a majority of the machines hold it in their logs.
  • When changing views to replace a silent primary, the new primary is chosen from a majority of replicas as the one holding the most up-to-date log (the highest viewstamp), provided enough replicas still have their logs intact.

Because any two majorities overlap, a majority of intact logs is guaranteed to contain at least one machine that holds every committed operation. We tell the story in the Tholos of Schedia, where five legislators keep one law from a central podium.

The Tholos of Schedia

Five legislators, one law

Schedia, the independent city on the eastern arm of Paxos, makes its own laws in the Tholos — a round stone hall looking across the bay toward the Paxon Chamber. Just as in the Chamber, the acoustics are so poor that no voice carries across the floor, so every word goes by messenger.*

Slow messengersMessengers across the floor can be delayed, reordered, or lost altogether.
Stepping out, going homeA legislator may step out onto the porch for a while (a network partition) or go home for the night and return to smooth wax tablets in the morning (a crash and recovery).
Nobody liesEvery legislator and messenger is honest: whatever they write or report is truthful, and no one ever forges a note.
What is kept

The council and its law

Schedia's council has five legislators — Okios, Liskovia, Kleon, Melissa, and Theron. Each sits at their own stone bench along the wall and keeps a complete copy of the city's law on wax tablets:

The law grows line by line: harbour dues are two obols a hull; the cove is closed to nets in spring; the dues above are halved for boats of Schedia. Every bench's copy must record the exact same lines in the exact same order, because later lines depend on earlier ones — halving the harbour dues means nothing unless the dues have already been set.

Even as legislators step out or go home and messengers dawdle across the floor, the council owes the citizens of Schedia two strict guarantees:

First guarantee · Consistency

One law

All five copies act as if they were a single law scroll kept in a single place. No citizen ever sees two different laws occupying the same line.

Second guarantee · Durability

Passed stays passed

Once a law has been declared passed (committed), it stands permanently in its place, no matter who holds the podium afterward.

With five copies of the law and legislators constantly coming and going, how does the council decide what goes into the law next without conflicting ballots?

In the paper · the group and its failures

A module group of cohorts behaves as a single logical entity; nodes may crash (fail-stop) and recover; the network may lose, delay, duplicate, and reorder messages and may partition; failures are non-Byzantine. Events known to a majority of cohorts survive view changes. (§§1–2, §4.)

Who decides what goes in

The podium

Unlike the Chamber across the bay — where any legislator can start a ballot at any time — the Tholos has a podium at its center. Only one legislator holds the podium at a time: the holder (the paper's primary). Only the holder may write new lines into the law.

The podium is an office, not a speaker's pulpit, since no voice carries from it either. The holder sits at the podium's desk, assigns each new line its number, and sends it out by runner; the other four legislators sit passively at their benches (as backups) and simply copy the lines the podium's runners bring them. Three rules govern the podium's work:

First · Writing

Only the holder writes

The holder receives each citizen's petition, writes the new line of law on their own tablet, gives it the next line number, and hands it to the podium's runners.

Second · Running

Runners never skip a line

Each bench has a dedicated runner (the paper's communication buffer) who delivers lines strictly in order without ever skipping a number. A runner may be delayed, but any bench that holds Line 12 is guaranteed to hold Lines 1 through 11 as well.

Third · Petitions

Citizens petition the podium

Citizens at the door send their petitions (client calls) to the podium. If a petition accidentally goes to a bench instead, that bench simply sends it back with the current holder's name.

Once the holder writes a new line and dispatches the runners to the benches, the citizen who petitioned is waiting at the door for an answer. How many runners must return before the holder can safely declare that the new line has passed into law?

In the paper · primary, backups and the communication buffer

The primary executes client calls and generates event records; backups are passive and apply event records received from the primary. The primary's communication buffer delivers event records reliably and in timestamp order to every backup in the current view; if delivery fails for too long, a view change is triggered. Any cohort that is not the active primary rejects client calls, returning the current viewid and primary identity. (§2, §3.3.)

When the holder may say “it is law”

When a law passes

A new line becomes law as soon as it is held by a majority of the council — which out of five legislators means at least three: the holder at the podium plus at least two benches (a sub-majority of backups).

Because three out of five is enough, the holder never has to wait for everyone. As each runner returns from a bench, they bring the bench's confirmation: "I hold everything up to Line 12." The moment two benches have confirmed Line 12, three legislators in all (the holder plus those two benches) hold it: Line 12 passes, and the holder tells the waiting petitioner that it is law. Passed means three hold it

A law has passed when its line — and every line before it — is held by the holder and at least two other legislators

Waiting for three out of five solves two problems at once. First, up to two legislators can step out of reach without stalling the city's business. Second, if the holder steps out tomorrow and a new holder takes the podium after polling a majority of three legislators, any two majorities of five must overlap in at least one legislator — the exact same quorum-intersection principle used in the Chamber across the bay.*

Any line that the holder has written at the desk but that has not yet reached two benches is merely a draft. If the holder steps out before a draft reaches a majority, a change of holder may discard that draft — which is completely harmless, because no citizen was ever told it had passed.

There is a catch, however: if every holder numbers their lines starting from 1, or continues numbering while another holder takes over, how do we tell "Line 12 written by Okios" apart from "Line 12 written by Liskovia"?

In the paper · waiting for a sub-majority

The operation force-to(v) waits until a sub-majority of backups (one fewer than a majority of the group configuration) acknowledge receiving every event of the current view up to timestamp v.ts; together with the primary itself, a full majority then holds the event. (§3. In Oki and Liskov's full distributed-transaction system, force-to is invoked at transaction prepare and commit; in our single-group telling, passing a law stands for committing an operation.)

Numbers that survive a change of holder

Board and line

Because a line number makes sense only under a single holder's term at the podium, every spell at the podium is given its own unique identifier: a board (a view, identified by a viewid), which hangs on a post beside the podium's desk.

Each board is stamped with a rising counter and the name of the legislator who called it — such as Board 7, Liskovia. Boards are ordered first by their counter, and if two legislators happen to call a board with the same counter at the same time, ties are broken alphabetically by the legislator's name. Because each legislator only ever puts their own name on a board they call, no two boards are ever identical.

Whenever a new board is raised at the podium, line numbers start fresh from 1. Thus every line of law is uniquely identified by its board and line (a viewstamp = ⟨viewid, ts⟩). When comparing two marks, the one from the later board always wins; within the same board, the higher line number wins. Each legislator also keeps a compact note (their history) in the margin of their tablets recording the last line they hold for each board they have served under. The later line knows more

A copy with a later board-and-line mark holds every passed law that an earlier mark holds

Why does a later mark always know at least as much passed law as an earlier one? Within a single board, it is true because runners never skip a line: anyone who holds Line 12 of Board 6 necessarily holds Lines 1 through 11 of Board 6 as well. Across different boards, it is true because every new board opens with its new holder sending their entire law copy out as the new board's opening line — so anyone who holds even Line 1 of Board 7 already holds everything Board 7 started with! Notice that a raw line number means nothing without its board: Board 6, Line 40 may have a bigger line number than Board 7, Line 1, yet Board 7, Line 1 is the later mark.

Now, what happens when the holder at the podium steps out or falls silent? Someone at the benches has to notice — and call for a new board.

In the paper · viewids and viewstamps

A view identifier is a pair viewid = ⟨cnt, mid⟩, totally ordered lexicographically and unique to its creator mid; a timestamp ts is unique and ordered within a view; a viewstamp = ⟨viewid, ts⟩ pairs the two. Each cohort's history is a sequence of viewstamps (the largest timestamp it holds for each view it has participated in), and because event records arrive in order without gaps, its state reflects every event up to each viewstamp in its history. (§2, Figures 1 and 4.)

Who notices, and what they do

An empty podium

The council keeps track of who is reachable through a periodic heartbeat called the roll: every so often, each legislator sends a brief note — "I am here" (I'm alive) — to the others.

Whenever a legislator stops hearing from the podium (or from another member), hears again from someone who was away, or has just walked back in from home, they call for a new board and become its caller (the view manager). Of course, the caller cannot tell whether a silent holder actually went home, stepped out onto the porch, or merely had slow messengers — and the caller does not need to know, because calling a new board is safe in every case.

To call a new board, the caller picks a board counter one higher than any board they have ever seen, attaches their own name, and sends an invitation to every legislator. Any legislator who receives an invitation for a board higher than their current one immediately accepts it, stops working under the old board, and replies in one of two ways depending on whether they still have their wax tablets:

First kind of reply · Normal acceptance

With tablets intact

"I accept, and my tablets hold up to Board 6, Line 40" — along with whether they were the holder of Board 6.

Second kind of reply · Crash acceptance

Without tablets (after going home)

"I accept, but I went home and my tablets were smoothed; the stone on my bench says I last served under Board 6."

What if two legislators notice the silent podium at the same time and both send out invitations? Because no two boards ever have the same mark, the higher board simply wins: the caller of the lower board receives the invitation to the higher board, abandons their own call, and replies to the higher board like everyone else.

Once invitations go out and replies come back to the caller, how does the caller decide whether the new board is allowed to form — and who gets to take the podium?

In the paper · the view manager and its invitations

Periodic “I'm alive” messages detect changes in reachability; the cohort that initiates a view change acts as the view manager and sends invitations carrying a higher viewid. A cohort with intact volatile state replies with a normal-accept carrying its latest viewstamp and whether it was the primary; a cohort recovering from a crash replies with a crash-accept carrying only the cur-viewid recorded on stable storage. (§4.)

Who takes the podium

A new board can form as soon as a majority of the council has accepted the invitation, provided we can be sure that at least one of those respondents holds every law that has ever passed. When nobody has gone home and lost their wax tablets, any majority of três out of five automatically satisfies that requirement! In the walkthrough above, every legislator who answered the call still had their wax tablets intact. What happens when someone has gone home for the night, had their wax tablets smoothed clean, and answers the call without tablets?

Sketch · that what passed survives, while everyone keeps their tablets

Let b and c be boards (views), ordered by their board marks. A line has passed under b when the holder of b and at least two other legislators hold that line and every line before it. Claim. Any line that passed under board b is held by every member of every board that forms after b.

  1. Oki and Liskov state this argument in §§2 and 4; here we lay it out step by step across consecutive boards.
  2. Suppose a law's line passed under board b: at least three of the five legislators held that line (and every line before it) under board b.
  3. Let c (> b) be the very next board that successfully forms. Board c forms on replies from at least three of the five legislators. By majority intersection, those three respondents share at least one legislator with the three who held the passed line; assuming no tablets were lost, that shared legislator replies with board b and a line number at or past the passed line.
  4. The new holder of board c is chosen as the respondent with the largest board-and-line mark (viewstamp). By The Later Line Knows More, the new holder's tablets contain everything the shared legislator's tablets contain, including the passed line.
  5. Board c opens with the new holder sending their complete law state to every member of c, so every member of c holds the passed line, and any new lines in c are ordered after it.
  6. By induction from board c to every subsequent board that forms, the passed line survives in every later board.∎
A majority of answers is not always enough

When tablets are lost

When a legislator goes home and returns to smooth wax, a simple majority of three replies is no longer enough on its own! To see why, look at Board 6 again: suppose Line 12 passed because Okios, Liskovia, and Kleon held it, while Melissa and Theron held only up to Line 10. Now suppose Okios goes home (losing his tablets) and comes back, while Liskovia and Kleon step out. When a call for Board 7 goes out, Okios (without tablets), Melissa (at Line 10), and Theron (at Line 10) answer — three out of five!

If Board 7 were allowed to form from those three replies, the best intact tablet among them would stop at Line 10, and passed Line 12 would be lost forever! Why did quorum intersection fail? Because the one person in the overlap between {Okios, Liskovia, Kleon} and {Okios, Melissa, Theron} was Okios — and Okios lost his tablets! To prevent this, Oki and Liskov require that a new board may form only when a majority of the council has answered and at least one of three safety conditions holds:

Condition 1

A majority still has its tablets

At least three of the five legislators replied with intact tablets (normal acceptances). Then the three intact replies alone form a majority and overlap the three who passed Line 12.

Condition 2

The lost tablets are from an older board

Every reply without tablets names an older board on its bench stone than the latest board shown by a reply with tablets. Since that later board already formed safely, everything from the older board was already carried forward into it!

Condition 3

That board's holder still has their tablets

A reply without tablets names the same board as the latest reply with tablets, and that board's holder replied with intact tablets. Because every line under a board is written at the podium first, the holder's intact tablets hold every line of that board, covering for any bench whose tablets were smoothed!

    In the paper · when a view may form

    A new view may form when the view manager has received acceptances from a majority of cohorts in the group configuration, and either: (1) a majority of the group sent normal-accepts, or (2) every crash-accept has crash-viewid < normal-viewid (the highest viewid among normal-accepts), or (3) the highest crash-viewid equals normal-viewid and the primary of that view sent a normal-accept. The new primary is chosen as the respondent with the largest viewstamp (preferring the old primary on a tie). (§4.)

    What going home costs

    Wax, not ink

    Schedia's legislators write the law on wax tablets (volatile memory) that remain on their benches and are smoothed clean whenever their owner goes home for the night (a crash).

    Only two facts are chiselled into the stone of the bench itself (stable storage): the legislator's name, and the mark of the last board they joined, which is recut only when a new board forms. Stepping out onto the porch leaves a legislator's wax tablets untouched, whereas going home wipes them clean. When a legislator returns from home to smooth wax, they know only their name and the last board carved into their bench: they immediately call for a new board, answer without tablets, and recover the law from the new holder's opening line.

    Writing in wax is fast — because nobody has to chisel every single line of law into stone before answering a runner! Instead, Schedia relies on the assumption that a majority of the council will not all go home on the same night.       Stopped, never wrong

      If too many tablets are smoothed at once, the council stops forever — it never starts a new board with a passed law missing

      Oki and Liskov note two ways to make this catastrophe arbitrarily unlikely in practice: have the primary alone log to disk, or equip each machine with an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) so it can flush its volatile memory to disk when power fails.

      Across the bay, the Paxon Parliament makes the opposite trade-off: its legislators write every promise and vote in indelible ink on parchment scrolls, and its Chamber has no podium. Let us set the two halls side by side.

      In the paper · what survives a crash

      Almost all of a cohort's state is kept in volatile memory. Only mymid, configuration, and mygroupid (written when the cohort is created) and cur-viewid (written once at the end of each view change) reside on stable storage. When a cohort recovers from a crash, it sets up-to-date = false and initiates a view change. A simultaneous crash of a majority (a “catastrophe”) “does not cause a group to enter a new view missing some needed information. Rather, it causes the algorithm to never again form a new view.” (§4, §4.2.)

      The other hall

      Across the bay

      Across the water on the western arm of the island stands the Paxon Chamber — another round stone hall where speeches cannot be heard, legislators wander in and out, and messengers take as long as they take.*

      Because each hall legislates only for its own city, their two law scrolls never conflict.* Comparing the two halls side by side shows how closely related Viewstamped Replication and Paxos really are — and where each makes its own design choice:

      What the Chamber's ink buys

      Immunity to everyone going home at once

      Because a Paxon legislator writes every promise, vote, and decree in indelible ink on their scroll (stable storage on every step), the entire Parliament can go home for the weekend and resume on Monday morning with nothing lost.* Schedia's wax tablets avoid disk writes during normal operation, at the cost of requiring that a majority not lose their wax at once.

      What Schedia's podium buys

      Strict primary-to-backup streaming

      In the Chamber, any legislator can initiate a ballot for any decree slot, and a newly elected president runs Phase 1 across open slots.* In Schedia's Tholos, only the podium holder ever writes lines, benches receive them strictly in order without gaps, and switching holders is an explicit view-change step that transfers the most up-to-date log.

      What the two halls share

      Overlapping majorities

      Both protocols rest on the exact same mathematical foundation: any two majorities of a group share at least one member, ensuring that whatever a majority committed in the past is discovered and preserved in the future.

      Remarkably, the two protocols were invented independently at almost the same time: Oki and Liskov published Viewstamped Replication in 1988, while Lamport's Paxos manuscript began circulating in 1989 and was published in 1998. That is why Schedia has a city and a Tholos of its own on the eastern arm of the island.

      The limits

      What it does not say

      The Tholos guarantees that every passed law survives any change of holder. Five questions mark the exact boundaries of what Oki and Liskov's 1988 paper promises:

      Does the council always form a new board right away?

      Not if messages keep getting delayed or lost. If a caller stops waiting too early and forms a board without a reachable colleague, that colleague may soon call a still higher board, causing repeated view changes. The paper notes this explicitly and recommends generous timeouts and message retransmissions: progress requires the network to settle long enough for a view to complete.

      What if a petitioner gets no answer before the holder changes?

      A citizen waiting at the door cannot tell whether their petition was passed just before the old holder stepped out or lost as an unconfirmed draft. In Oki and Liskov's transaction system, rather than risk running a non-idempotent operation twice, an unanswered call causes the enclosing transaction to abort cleanly.

      Was the paper written for a single council alone?

      In the 1988 paper, each council (module group) is one shard of a larger distributed transaction system coordinated by two-phase commit (`prepare` and `commit`). In 2012, Liskov and Cowling's Viewstamped Replication Revisited presented VR in the standalone state-machine replication form told here.

      Could a legislator lie?

      Not in the Tholos. Here legislators may step out, go home, or miss messages, but everything they write and report is honest (crash-recovery faults). Tolerating lying legislators requires the $3f + 1$ quorums and two-echo cross-checks of Castro and Liskov's PBFT.*

      How does a citizen find out who holds the podium?

      Clients cache the current holder's identity; if they send a petition to a bench by mistake, the bench redirects them to the current holder, or they consult a location directory service.

      Schedia and the Tholos, in detail All the papers

      Setting Schedia and the Tholos

      The paper behind this book

      Brian M. Oki and Barbara H. Liskov, Viewstamped Replication: A New Primary Copy Method to Support Highly-Available Distributed Systems. Proceedings of the Seventh Annual ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), Toronto, August 1988, 8–17. — Introduces the primary-copy replication method, viewids and viewstamps, forcing event records to a sub-majority of backups, the view-change algorithm and its crash-safe formation conditions, and integration with two-phase commit.

      The ideas, plainly.

      Oki and Liskov replicate a service across a group of cohorts that may crash, recover, and suffer network partitions or lost messages, but never lie. In each view, a single cohort acts as the primary, executing client calls and streaming event records in timestamp order to the passive backups. An event is committed once a majority of the group holds it (the primary plus a sub-majority of backups via force-to).

      Each period under a single primary is a view identified by a unique, totally ordered viewid = ⟨cnt, mid⟩, and each event is tagged with a viewstamp = ⟨viewid, ts⟩. When the primary appears unreachable or membership changes, any cohort can act as a view manager and invite the group into a higher view. It collects responses from a majority of cohorts, chooses the respondent with the latest viewstamp as the new primary, and starts the new view by broadcasting that primary's full state. Because any two majorities intersect and event records arrive in order without gaps, the respondent with the largest viewstamp in a majority is guaranteed to hold every committed event.

      Because cohorts keep their logs and state in volatile memory rather than writing every event to disk, a cohort that crashes and recovers loses its log and remembers only the cur-viewid stored on disk. Three view-formation conditions prevent a new view from forming if too many cohorts have lost their volatile state to guarantee that all committed events are present: if a majority crash at once, the group halts safely rather than starting a view with missing history. Meanwhile, a deposed primary in a minority partition can do no harm because its old viewid is rejected by the majority in the new view.
