Distributed Algorithms of Ancient Greece · Paxos · The Scholars' Coast

Spreading by Word of Mouth

Based on “Epidemic Algorithms for Replicated Database Maintenance” by Alan Demers, Mark Gealy, Dan Greene, Carl Hauser, Wes Irish, John Larson, Sue Manning, Scott Shenker, Howard Sturgis, Dan Swinehart, Doug Terry and Don Woods (1987)

Can news reach every library on a coast just by each scholar passing it on to a few others, with no one organising the spread? And what catches the libraries the gossip misses?

On the scholars' coast of Paxos, a correction written at one library has to reach all the others, and nothing orders the others to listen. Letters go astray, so scholars tell each other in the stoa until they tire of it, and once a year pairs of copyists compare whole collections at the festival ground. The telling is quick and leaves some who never hear; the festival is slow and leaves no one out.

Setting The Scholars' Coast

In a Nutshell

Demers et al.'s epidemic algorithms ask how an update made at one replica of a database can reach all the others cheaply and reliably. Direct mail is fast but loses messages, anti-entropy (pairs of replicas comparing whole copies) reaches everyone but slowly, and rumour mongering (replicas passing news to random peers until it goes stale) is quick and cheap but leaves a small fraction never told. The paper measures that fraction, shows it can be made small but never empty, and shows how anti-entropy catches the rest. Deleting needs death certificates, because an absence cannot be passed on, and the spatial distribution of partners decides how much traffic each link carries.

The book tells it with the libraries of the scholars' coast. A new edition of a work appears at one library of the scholars' coast, and every other library should end up holding it. Letters to everyone are quick but miss libraries; pairs of copyists comparing whole collections at a yearly festival miss no one but take many festivals; scholars passing the news on in the stoa are quick and cheap but some libraries are never told. A work is withdrawn by leaving a certificate that it is dead.

Chapter I

One Scrap, Many Shelves

The scholars' coast of Paxos is a string of small libraries, each in its own olive grove, with a stoa where scholars gather and a festival ground beside it. Nothing here waits for a vote, and a library answers its readers from whatever it holds*. Many libraries keep copies of the same works, and each library's copy of a work is an edition that carries a tally. A tally is the day and hour by the sun at which the edition was written, with the number of the library that wrote it to break a tie, so that of any two editions of one work, one is newer. Whoever holds two editions of a work keeps the one with the larger tally, and has no other way to choose.

What travels here is short: a work of a single learning, set down under a title on a scrap of papyrus. Say Demerios, at his own library, corrects the work The Order of the Heavens to say that the Earth goes round the Sun. He writes a new edition of it, and its tally is that day and hour by the sun, later than any other tally on that work. That is the one thing a scholar may do: write a new edition at his own library. Nothing else changes a library's shelves except receiving someone else's edition.

What should happen next? Every library should in the end hold Demerios's edition, or one newer than it, and so all libraries hold the same edition of the entry. Does that mean all of them hold it at once? No. While news is still travelling, some libraries hold the old edition and others the new, and a reader asks whichever library is near. The libraries can agree completely only when no one is writing any more and the news has caught up; until then most of what a library holds is current, and some is not. Nothing here tries to prevent that, only to bring it to an end.

Is it enough to say send it to everyone? Whoever writes it does not know what every library holds, and no one is in charge of seeing that it arrives. So the question is a narrow one: with no one ordering the others, by what plain habits does a new edition reach every shelf, how quickly, at what cost in walking and carrying, and what is left over?

The simplest habit is to write to everyone. Why is that not enough?

Chapter II

Letters to Everyone

A scholar who has written a new edition can copy it onto a slip for every other library and send a courier to each. Each library that receives the slip compares its tally with the one it holds, and keeps the larger. Nothing could be plainer, and while the coast was small it was enough.

What goes wrong? Three things. A courier can be lost, or delayed past use, or his slip can be dropped in a ditch, and the library he was bound for never hears. The writer may not know every library: a new one has opened since his list was made, or he has simply forgotten one, and a library he does not know of gets no slip. And all the work falls on one library, which must write and send a slip for every other, however many there are.

What does it cost? One slip for every library, each carried the whole way from the writer, so the carrying grows with the number of libraries and with how far apart they are on average. Who notices when one slip goes astray? No one. A library that never receives a slip does not know there was one, and the writer does not know it was not received. No one chases such misses by hand, and on a coast of hundreds no one could.

If no one can tell which libraries were missed, what could catch them without anyone knowing where to look?

Chapter III

The Festival

Once a year the copyists of the coast come to the festival ground, below the stoa, where long stone tables stand in rows. Each copyist draws by lot the name of one library from all the libraries of the coast, goes to its copyist, and the two sit down opposite each other with their whole collections of scrolls; some are drawn by two or three, some by none. They go through them together, work by work, and wherever the two hold different editions of a work, the one with the smaller tally takes the other's.

There are three ways to do it. The copyist whose edition is newer may give it to the other and take nothing back; or the copyist whose edition is older may ask for the newer one; or the two may do both, each giving and each asking, so that after the table both hold the newer edition whichever of them had it. Which is better? That is what the next paragraphs find out.

First, does it ever fail? No. Every copyist has some chance of being paired with every other, so a library that holds a new edition will in time be paired with any library that does not, and the older edition will give way. This does not wait on couriers or on any list of libraries: the lot needs only the libraries that come. The festival is the habit that misses no one. How long does it take? Not long, in the sense that matters: the number of festivals the news needs grows only with the logarithm of the number of libraries, so doubling the coast adds a fixed handful of festivals to the wait. For the giving way, the paper quotes a result of Pittel's for the exact amount, about the logarithm of the number of libraries to base two plus its natural logarithm; the book takes that on the paper's word.

Proof — that anti-entropy with every pair possible reaches every site with probability 1
  1. This is the paper's argument of §3.2, set out. Let S be the set of sites, and suppose that for every pair s, s′ in S the probability that s chooses s′ as partner in a cycle is at least some q > 0.
  2. Let s₀ be a site holding the entry with the largest timestamp t, and let s be any site holding an older value. In any cycle, the probability that s chooses s₀ is at least q.
  3. If s chooses s₀, then whichever of push, pull or push-pull is in use, ResolveDifference leaves s with a value of timestamp at least t, since the value with the smaller timestamp is replaced by the one with the larger, and none is larger than t. (With push alone the same holds in the cycle in which s₀ chooses s.)
  4. The probability that s has not chosen s₀ in c cycles is at most (1 − q)c, which tends to 0 as c grows. Since there are finitely many sites, the probability that some site still holds an older value tends to 0.∎

Now suppose the news has already reached nearly everyone, say by some other means, and only a few libraries still lack it. Giving or asking? Here the two part company sharply. When a library that lacks the edition is paired with a library that has it, asking brings it the edition at once, but giving brings it only if the one who has it happens to draw its name, which a library holding the edition is unlikely to do for any one of the few who lack it. When only a few still lack the news, asking makes the fraction of libraries without it fall as the square of itself: one in ten becomes one in a hundred, then one in ten thousand, then one in a hundred million. Giving shrinks that fraction only by about the same factor each festival, a little over a third, so it takes many festivals more to finish. Doing both behaves like asking.

Proof — that pull squares the susceptible fraction and push only scales it
  1. This is the paper's deterministic model of §1.3. Let pi be the probability that a site is still susceptible after the ith cycle, n the number of sites, and partners be chosen uniformly.
  2. Pull. A site is susceptible after cycle i + 1 exactly when it was susceptible after cycle i and the partner it contacted was also susceptible. The partner is susceptible with probability pi, independently, so pi+1 = pi².
  3. Push. A site is susceptible after cycle i + 1 exactly when it was susceptible after cycle i and none of the infective sites chose it. There are n(1 − pi) infective sites, each choosing it with probability 1/n, so pi+1 = pi(1 − 1/n)n(1−pi).
  4. For large n the last factor is close to e−(1−pi), and for small pi close to e−1. Push therefore multiplies a small pi by about 1/e each cycle, a geometric decrease, while pull squares it, which falls faster than any geometric decrease once pi is small.
  5. Push-pull makes a site informed if either holds, so it does at least as well as pull; the paper reports that it behaves essentially like pull.∎

So the festival is a sure habit, and for the last few libraries asking is the right way to run it. What is wrong with it? Look at what the pair actually do. They go through their whole collections, work by work, and nearly all of it is the same on both sides, since in an ordinary year almost every entry agrees. Almost all the effort is spent finding that nothing needs doing.

If nearly everything agrees, must the pair read everything to find the few lines that do not?

Chapter IV

Comparing Marks First

Each library keeps, beside its catalogue scroll, a catalogue mark: a short mark worked out from the whole catalogue, so that two catalogues that agree on every work and edition have the same mark, and two that differ anywhere almost certainly have different ones. The librarian renews it a little at a time as the catalogue changes. A pair at the festival can now begin by comparing marks, which is a glance across the table, and sit down to the long reading only if the marks differ.

Does that settle it? Not quite, and the reason is worth seeing. A recent edition is almost always known to some libraries and not yet to others, and any library that holds it has a different mark from any that does not. So unless news crosses the coast much faster than new editions are written, the marks of two libraries chosen by lot will usually differ because of something recent, and the long reading is back.

The remedy is to treat the recent editions separately. Each library keeps a recent list: the editions whose tallies are newer than a chosen span. The pair first swap recent lists and apply them, each taking whatever is newer, which also brings each catalogue mark up to date. Only then do they compare marks, and the mark no longer differs because of anything recent, so it usually agrees and the reading is not needed. The expected cost of a meeting is then about the length of the recent lists, which is only as long as the editions written in the span.

What span should be chosen? It has to be longer than the news needs to cross the whole coast. If it is, anything recent is on the list long enough to be swapped before it drops off. If the coast grows until the news takes longer than the span, the marks start to fail, and the work returns to something slightly worse than comparing everything. The span has to be chosen with the coast's size in mind, and the paper warns that it is critical.

Is there a way that needs no span? Yes: the pair swap editions newest first, comparing marks after each, and stop the moment the marks agree. For that, a library must keep its catalogue entries in a second scroll ordered by tally, which costs a little to keep in step; the paper calls it peel back.

Even a cheap festival comes only once a year. Can news move between festivals, quicker than the calendar, without anyone sending it?

Chapter V

Word of Mouth

The stoa is the long colonnade beside the festival ground, where scholars from every library of the coast pass in turn and stop to talk. A scholar who has just written, or just heard, a new edition carries a scrap of it, and each day he tells it to whoever the road puts in his way. Whom he meets is a matter of chance, as at the festival: no one arranges who talks to whom, and no one keeps a record of who has been told.

With respect to one scrap, every scholar is in one of three states. He has not heard it. Or he is still telling it: it is on his hot tablet, a wax tablet of the scraps he is currently passing on. Or he has stopped: the scrap is on his shelf and he holds it as an edition, but he no longer tells it. Does a scholar who stops tell it differently? He no longer tells it at all, and still answers any reader from the scrap he holds.

Why would a scholar ever stop? Because telling a scrap to someone who already has it is wasted breath, and a scholar who goes on telling after most of the coast knows it is only wearying his hearers. So the rule is losing interest. A scholar who tells a scrap to someone who already had it may, with some chance, give up; the chance is one in some chosen number, his allowance. Whoever he tells who has not heard begins to tell it too. So at first the telling grows, because most of those met have not heard it, and later it dies out, because most of those met have.

The same idea comes in several versions, and the paper separates the choices. Does the scholar learn, when he tells, whether the other already knew? In the attentive version he does, and he loses interest only after an idle telling. In the blind version he does not: he simply gives up on a toss, whoever he tells. Does he lose interest by chance, or by counting? With a toss he gives up at the chance of one in his allowance; with a count he gives up after as many idle tellings as his allowance and not before. Both pairs can be combined, so that a scholar may count blindly.

Is there anything here that is a plan? No. Nobody decides who tells whom, nobody keeps track of who has heard, and a scholar does not know whether the news has reached everyone; he knows only the last few people he told. If one scholar has the news, a few days later a few have it, and soon many, and then the telling fades. That is quick and cheap. But is every library certain to hear it?

When the last scholar stops telling, who is left who never heard?

Chapter VI

Who Never Hears

The telling runs until no one is still telling, and then it stops for good: a scholar who has stopped does not start again of his own accord. Whoever has not heard by then never will, by this habit. Those libraries are the residue. How large is it? The paper measures three things, and they pull against each other: the residue, the tellings per library, which is the traffic, and the delay, both the average days until a library hears and the days until the last one that will hear does.

Take the plainest version, where a scholar gives up on a toss after an idle telling, at the chance of one in his allowance, and the coast is so large that one library hardly matters. The paper models the telling with two equations for the shares of the coast that have not heard and that are still telling, and solves them. The residue comes out as the share that satisfies a single relation, and it falls exponentially as the allowance grows. With an allowance of one, about a fifth of the coast never hears; with an allowance of two, about a sixteenth. A larger allowance shrinks it further, and no allowance makes it nothing.

Proof — that the fraction who never hear satisfies s = e−(k+1)(1−s)
  1. This is the paper's model of §1.4. Let s, i, r be the fractions of the population susceptible, infective and removed, so that s + i + r = 1. A susceptible site becomes infective in proportion to si; an infective site becomes removed at rate (1/k)(1 − s) per unit of i, since it loses interest with probability 1/k when it calls a site that is not susceptible, which happens with probability 1 − s. Hence
    ds/dt = −si,   di/dt = si − (1/k)(1 − s)i.
  2. Dividing the second by the first removes t: di/ds = −1 + (1 − s)/(ks) = −(k + 1)/k + 1/(ks).
  3. Integrating, i(s) = −((k + 1)/k)s + (1/k) ln s + c.
  4. At the start one site in n is infective, so i(1 − ε) = ε with ε = 1/n. For large n, ε → 0, so i(1) = 0 and c = (k + 1)/k. Hence i(s) = ((k + 1)/k)(1 − s) + (1/k) ln s.
  5. The epidemic ends when i = 0, that is when ln s = −(k + 1)(1 − s), so the residue satisfies s = e−(k+1)(1−s). For k = 1 the solution is about 0.20 and for k = 2 about 0.06, as the paper states.∎

The paper then runs the variants on a coast of a thousand libraries and sets the results side by side. A scholar who is blind and gives up on a toss leaves far more of the coast without the news than one who is attentive and counts idle tellings, for the same allowance, but tells far less; the paper adds that counting matters more to the residue than being attentive does. The picture is the same in every version: the residue falls steeply, and each fall costs more tellings.

The rows under the figure are the paper's own measures: with counting, an allowance of one leaves about 18 libraries in a hundred without the news at 1.7 tellings per library, an allowance of three about 1 in a hundred at 4.5, and an allowance of five about 1 in a thousand at 6.7. Delay changes little. On average a library hears in some eleven to thirteen days with counting and in fourteen to nineteen with tosses, and the last library to hear does so in about seventeen days with counting and over thirty with tosses.

Is that a coincidence, that every version costs the same for the same residue? It is not. In every version, the residue and the number of tellings per library go together in one way, the residue is about one over the exponential of the tellings per library: 1.7 tellings leave a fifth, 3.3 leave about a twenty-seventh, 6.7 about a thousandth. Why? Because the scrap is told to a library about as often as chance picks it, and a library is missed only if chance never picks it in all the tellings there are. So the delay is the only thing that tells the versions apart; the residue is bought with tellings, at the same price in every version.

Argument — that s = e−m in every version of rumour mongering
  1. The paper gives this argument in §1.4 and says it is not fully proved. Let m be the average number of updates sent from a site, so the epidemic sends nm updates in all.
  2. Each update is addressed to a site chosen uniformly, so a given site is not chosen by any one with probability 1 − 1/n.
  3. If the updates were independent, the probability that a given site is chosen by none of them is (1 − 1/n)nm, which tends to e−m as n grows. This probability is the residue s.
  4. The step that is not proved is that m may be treated as a constant: it varies from site to site, and the relation holds only if its spread about the mean goes to zero as n → ∞. The authors observe this empirically and have not proved it.
  5. Table 1 agrees with the relation: m = 1.7 gives e−1.7 ≈ 0.18 and m = 3.3 gives ≈ 0.037.∎

So the residue can be made as small as one likes, at a price, and is never nothing. A scholar can be told he must tell, or asked to tell, in more than one way. Is there a way of running the telling that does better than the price every version here pays?

Does it matter whether a scholar tells or asks, as it did at the festival?

Chapter VII

Telling, Asking and Crowded Days

At the festival, asking beat giving for the last few libraries. In the stoa the same two ways exist. A scholar who is telling goes to the one he meets and gives him his scraps. A scholar who is asking goes to the one he meets and says what is new? Whichever of the two meets the other, a scrap changes hands. Does asking do better here too? Yes, and by a good deal: the share who never hear falls much faster than the one-over-exponential price that telling pays. With an attentive scholar who counts, the paper's trials leave about 3 libraries in a hundred without the news at an allowance of one, at 2.7 tellings per library; about 6 in ten thousand at an allowance of two; about 4 in a million at three.

Why is telling used at all? Because it depends on how many things there are to tell. If many scraps are going round, a scholar who asks is nearly sure to meet someone with something to say, and asking works. If the coast is quiet and there is nothing new, a telling scholar has nothing to say and says nothing, which costs no breath; an asking scholar goes on asking, and every day's asking is wasted. So asking suits a busy coast and telling a quiet one. A scholar can also do both, and meet one who does both: if both already knew the scrap, only the one who has told it fewer times is counted as having wasted a telling, and if both have told it equally, both are. That gives the smallest residue the paper reports, and it costs a scholar telling his count as well as his scrap.

What if a scholar can speak with only so many people in a day? That changes the two ways in opposite directions. When only one scrap is going round and a scholar can be spoken to by only one at a time, two scholars who both pick the same listener are not both heard: one is turned away. The listener still gets the scrap, with one fewer telling, so telling gets better, since the turned-away telling would have been a wasted one. Asking gets worse, because its strength was that every scholar lacking the news asks every day and is answered, and a limit means some askings are refused, so that a library refused day after day can miss the news. The chance is small with a modest limit, since the chance of a scholar being asked by as many as some given number in a day falls away very fast with that number (the paper gives it as one over e times the number's factorial). What a turned-away scholar can do is to hunt: choose another listener. Hunting is cheap and helps in every limited case, and with a limit of one speaker a day and hunting without end, every scholar ends up paired with one other, as at the festival, so telling and asking become the same and the residue is very small.

However well the telling is run, some libraries may be left. What does the coast do about them?

Chapter VIII

The Festival Behind the Gossip

The two habits cover for each other. The telling is quick and cheap and may leave a few libraries; the festival is slow and dear and leaves none. So the coast does both: the scrap is told in the stoa, and then, at the festival, whichever pairs find a difference put it right. Does the telling ever get so far wrong that the festival cannot catch up? It can fail outright: the last teller can stop while some libraries have not heard, and the scrap is then held by some libraries and not by others for ever, unless something else carries it. The festival is that something else. Run rarely, since it costs so much, it brings every library the scrap with certainty in the end, so that every update reaches every library or is replaced by a newer one.

When two copyists at the festival find that one holds a scrap the other lacks, what should they do besides put it right? The cautious reply is nothing more: the festival will in time carry it to everyone. The bolder reply is to treat the scrap as news again, and have the pair tell it afresh to others, which is wanted if the telling had failed altogether. If they redistribute by letters, the cost can be very high. In the worst case, news that has reached about half the coast makes every one of those libraries write to every library at the next festival, which is the square of the number of libraries in letters. If they redistribute by word of mouth, the cost is small. Why? Because a scrap already known to nearly everyone is soon dropped by a scholar who tells it, as everyone he meets knew it; and when the scrap is known to half the coast, a great many scholars start telling it at once, which costs less than starting it from one scholar.

The paper adds a combination that cannot fail. Each library keeps its recent scraps on a list ordered by how lately each was useful, not by tally; a scrap that proves new to the one told moves to the head of the list, and one that proves old slips down. At a meeting the pair swap scraps from the head of their lists in batches until their catalogue marks agree. This keeps the scrap going while it is still news, needs no second scroll of tallies, and since it goes on until the marks agree, has no chance of leaving a library out; and it deals sensibly with a coast that is cut in two for a while and joined again.

All of this spreads an edition. How can anyone spread the news that a work is gone?

Chapter IX

Withdrawing a Work

Suppose Demerios judges a work wrong and withdraws it at his own library, where he has the right to. Can he simply remove it from his shelf and let the others follow? No, and the reason is the whole of what follows. At the next meeting, the pair compare: his library holds nothing of the work, the other holds an edition of it. The rule is that a library takes the other's edition when it has none or an older one, so the meeting gives the work back to him. Every habit so far spreads what is present; none spreads an absence. Left alone, the old edition spreads over the coast again from any library that did not strike it in time.

The remedy is to make the absence something that can be carried. In place of a struck work, the library writes a notice of withdrawal: a slip that names the work and carries a tally, like an edition. It is spread by the same habits as an edition, and whenever an old edition meets a notice with a newer tally, the old edition is struck and the notice stays. So a withdrawal is spread as any news is, and cancels every older copy it meets.

What does that cost? Notices pile up. A library cannot keep every notice for ever, or they will fill its shelves. When can a notice be thrown away? The first answer is: when every library has received it. That is exactly the kind of knowledge nobody on the coast has, since it asks everyone to be heard from; a method for it exists, but it needs every library to answer, and on a coast of hundreds there is nearly always some library closed or cut off for days. The second answer is the plain one: throw a notice away after a fixed span, a month say. But then an old edition older than the span that turns up after the notice is gone is let back in. There is a plain trade: a longer span lessens the risk and uses more shelf.

Can the span be made far longer without filling the shelves? Yes, by dormant notices. When a notice is written, it also names a few keepers, libraries drawn by lot from the coast, and it spreads in the ordinary way. After a first span, most libraries throw the notice away, but the keepers it names keep a copy, asleep: a sleeping notice is not carried to others at meetings. After a second span the keepers discard it as well. If an old edition older than the notice now turns up at a library that holds a sleeping copy, the notice wakes and is spread afresh to every library, and cancels the old edition. The paper compares it to an immune reaction, with the woken notices as antibodies. The gain is large: for the same shelf space the history that can be kept is longer by a factor that grows with the number of libraries, from a month to years.

When a notice wakes, what tally does it carry? The obvious answer is a fresh one, but that is wrong. Somewhere on the coast there may be a legitimate later edition, written after the notice to bring the work back, and a notice with a fresh tally would strike that edition too. So a notice carries two tallies. The first never changes: it says when the work was withdrawn, and an edition is cancelled only if its tally is lower than this. The second is the waking tally: it says when the notice was last awake, and it alone decides whether the notice is asleep and whether it is carried. Waking a notice sets the second tally to now and leaves the first alone, so the notice is spread afresh without cancelling anything written after the work was withdrawn.

Proof — that reactivating by the activation timestamp cancels no later update
  1. This is the argument of §2.2, set out. A death certificate carries an ordinary timestamp t and an activation timestamp a, equal when created.
  2. The rule of cancellation uses only t: a certificate cancels an item with timestamp u exactly when t > u. The rule of propagation uses only a: the certificate is dormant, and so not propagated by anti-entropy, once a is older than τ₁, and is deleted once a is older than τ₁ + τ₂.
  3. Suppose a site has a legitimate item with timestamp u > t, an update that reinstated the item after its deletion. The certificate has t < u, so it does not cancel it.
  4. Reactivating a certificate sets a to the current time and leaves t unchanged. The cancellation test is unchanged, so the reactivated certificate still fails to cancel any item with u > t, while the new a lets it propagate.
  5. By contrast, had reactivation set t to the current time, a certificate with t′ > u would cancel the reinstating update, which is incorrect.∎

Does the scheme scale for ever? It does not. The time a new notice takes to reach the coast grows with its size, and the first span does not. While the first span is longer than that time, a notice is almost never needed again once it has been put to sleep, and wakings are rare. When the coast grows until the time exceeds the span, wakings become common, each one adds traffic that slows the next notice further, and the habit breaks down altogether. To avoid it, the first span, and so the shelf space each library gives to notices, has to grow as the logarithm of the size of the coast.

All these meetings are by lot, every library as likely as any other. Does that suit a coast that is not an even plain?

Chapter X

Near Partners and the Ferry

The coast is not an even plain. Most libraries lie along the coast road within walking distance of their neighbours, but an inlet cuts off a few of them from the rest, and the only crossing is a ferry. The few libraries across the ferry draw nearly all their partners from the other side, and each of the many on this side has a small chance of drawing one of them, so that added up the ferry carries far more meetings than any road. What would be better? The paper's answer is to prefer nearer partners.

How much nearer? The paper starts with libraries strung along one road. If every scholar picks only his nearest neighbour, the roads carry little, but the news takes as many days as there are libraries to creep along the road. If he picks anyone by lot, the news is quick, but the traffic through any stretch of road grows with the number of libraries. It tried a chance of picking a library that falls with its distance as some power of the distance, and found a clear pattern: the heavier the preference for the near, the lighter the traffic per stretch of road, and the slower the news. Its analysis, which it calls informal for the time the news takes, suggests the best balance on a line is a chance falling with the square of the distance: the traffic on a stretch of road then grows only with the logarithm of the number of libraries.

A coast is not a line, so how is "near" measured? Each library makes a list of the others in order of distance and draws partners from the list, with chance falling with the square of a library's place in the list, not of its distance, so that where the coast is thickly settled the near ones get most of the chance and where it is thin the chance spreads further. Libraries at the same distance are given the same chance. This works on lines, on plains and on odd shapes, because "the number of libraries nearer" measures how crowded a place is wherever it is.

What did it gain, on the real network of the paper? In the paper's trials on a coast with one such crossing, the crossing carried about 76 meetings at each festival when partners were picked by lot, against about 6 on an average road; with the preference for near partners it carried 2.4. The ferry got more than thirty times less traffic, and the traffic on an average road fell about four times, at the cost of news taking less than twice as many festivals to reach the last library. The paper notes that one could arguably afford to hold the meetings twice as often for the same load. The authors had the improved habit installed in the system they studied, and the load and the disagreement between copies both fell.

Is the preference safe for the festival and for the gossip alike? For the festival, yes: so long as every pair has some chance of being paired, every library will in time meet every other. For word of mouth, no, because the telling runs out. The paper gives two small coasts that show it. In the first, two libraries lie close together and a little further from many others that are all equally far from both. If the allowance is smaller than the number of those others, there is a good chance that the two will pick each other as many times in a row as the allowance, and if the news is born at either of them, it stops there. In the second, one library lies beyond a long stretch from the root of a great branching tree of others. In the tree, few will ever pick the lone library in the time a scrap stays warm, and the news may never reach it before it stops being told. The remedy is to raise the allowance as the preference grows. That works well for telling and asking together; telling alone is far more sensitive, and the paper's trial of it needed an allowance of thirty-six. The plain safeguard is the festival behind the gossip.

What, in the end, does all this promise, and what does it only hope for?

Chapter XI

What It Does Not Say

Does the coast promise that news reaches every library within a number of days? No. Because every choice is by lot, nothing can be said such as the news will arrive in so many days. The most that can be said is that, if no one writes any more, the chance that the news has still not reached everyone falls exponentially as time passes. The authors take this as a fair price for habits that are simple to carry out correctly.

Does telling alone reach everyone? No: it leaves a residue that can be made small and never empty, and a set of small cases where it fails badly. Only the festival behind it guarantees every library in the end, and the guarantee is that every edition arrives or is replaced by a newer one, with probability one, not by a day.

Does the coast ever agree completely? Only when no one writes any more. While scholars keep writing, there are always libraries behind, and a reader of any one of them may be handed an out-of-date edition. How likely, and how far behind, is a question of its own.*

What does it do when two scholars write different editions of one work? It keeps the one with the larger tally and throws the other away. It does not combine them, and nothing in it considers whether the loser had something the winner lacked. All that the habits spread is the edition whose tally is larger. The habits compare tallies and nothing else. A tally is a reading of the sun, so it is only as good as the library's reading of it. The paper warns that its method wants tallies close to the true time of writing: if they stray, the habits work as written and the coast still agrees in the end, but a newer edition may lose to an older one.

And what is left unsolved? Awkward shapes of the coast, which can ruin the gossip and which the authors say they do not yet understand; whether a fixed span for notices can be avoided; and whether some libraries should be set above others, so that those on high talk to each other across the long distances and those below to their neighbours. The authors leave that open, since the trouble with it is keeping the order in place.

The ideas, plainly

Demers and his co-authors keep a database copied at many sites and ask how an update made at one site can reach all the others with very little help from the network and no one in charge. Each entry carries a timestamp and the larger timestamp wins, so any two sites that meet can settle which entry is newer. Mailing the update to everyone is quick but unreliable, and the sender may not know every site. Anti-entropy, in which each site regularly picks another at random and the two reconcile, always reaches every site, but slowly and at the cost of comparing whole databases; checksums and lists of recent updates make it cheap.

Rumor mongering passes an update on while it is still news: a site that has it tells others and loses interest after meeting enough who already knew. That is quick and cheap, but it can leave a fraction of sites who never hear, a fraction that shrinks exponentially with the patience allowed and is tied to the traffic by one relation in every version. Pull does much better than push when few sites are left. Anti-entropy run rarely behind it makes certain that nothing is missed.

Deletions have to be spread too, or an old copy brings the item back, so a deletion is a certificate with a timestamp; certificates cannot be kept for ever, so a few sites keep them asleep and wake them if an old copy turns up, with a second timestamp so that waking does not cancel newer updates. Choosing partners nearer first, with a chance that falls with the number of closer sites, spares the few critical links, at a small cost in delay; but it can defeat rumor mongering in awkward networks, which is another reason for the anti-entropy behind it.
