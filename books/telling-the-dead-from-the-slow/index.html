Telling the Dead from the Slow

Based on “Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems” by Tushar Deepak Chandra and Sam Toueg (1996)

A machine that has crashed looks exactly like one that is merely slow, and that alone makes agreement impossible. How well must we tell the dead from the slow before agreement becomes possible again?

The four mercenaries at the foot of the hill must all attack or all hold, and any of them may fall silent while no bird has a longest flight. Give each tent a slate on which a man chalks the tents he thinks are dead. The slate may be wrong, often. It turns out to need very little: if one living man is never chalked, the four can agree however many die; if that holds only in the end, they can agree while more than half live.

Setting The Foot of Mount Phyle

In a Nutshell

Chandra and Toueg's unreliable failure detectors take the obstacle in the Fischer–Lynch–Paterson result, that a crashed process cannot be told from a slow one, and give each process a module that guesses which others have crashed. The guesses may be wrong, often. The paper classifies detectors by how complete (crashed processes are eventually suspected) and how accurate (live ones are not suspected) they must be, and finds how little consensus needs: with one correct process never suspected, consensus survives any number of crashes, and if that holds only eventually, it survives as long as a majority is alive.

The book tells it with four mercenaries and their tents. Each tent gets a slate on which a man chalks the tents he thinks are dead. The slate may be wrong, yet it needs very little to work: if one living man is never chalked, the four can agree however many die, and if that holds only in the end, they can agree while more than half live.

Chapter I

The Slate

At the foot of Mount Phyle four mercenaries, Number 1 to Number 4, keep four tents out of sight of one another and send word only by raven*. They must all attack or all hold, and committing to either takes a man from his tent and its birds for the night. Each formed an opinion at dusk, his sighting. A bird always arrives, but there is no longest flight. Nobody lies. Any man may be killed, and a dead man's tent simply falls silent.

The three demands First. No two of them commit to different answers.
Second. A man commits only to an answer someone sighted.
Third. Every man who is not killed eventually commits.

Can they be sure of meeting all three? On such a night no arrangement guarantees that anyone commits, because a man waiting on a silent tent cannot tell whether the man there is dead or his bird is still in the pines*. If he waits, he may wait for ever on a dead man. If he stops waiting, he may leave out a live man whose bird was about to arrive.

What if each man had some help in telling the dead from the slow? That is the one change in this book: each tent has a slate, with a row for every other tent. A man chalks a tent he thinks is dead and rubs the mark out when he thinks again. How he decides is not the question here. Is the slate right? Often not: a mark may stand against a live man whose bird is merely slow, and a dead man's row may stay clean for a while.

A slate that may be wrong, then. How good must it be for the four to agree, and what does it mean for a slate to be good at all?

Chapter II

How a Slate May Be Wrong

In how many ways can a slate go wrong? Two. It can fail to chalk the dead, or it can chalk the living. So two things are asked of it.

Chalking the dead. Every man who is killed is, in the end, chalked for good. Either by every living man, or at least by some living man.

Sparing the living. Four strengths, from strongest to weakest:

Four ways of sparing the living Never chalked: no man is chalked while he lives.
One never chalked: some man who lives through the night is never chalked by anyone.
In the end, never chalked: there comes a time after which no living man is chalked.
In the end, one never chalked: there comes a time after which some man who lives through the night is chalked by no one.
Completeness, strong and weak; accuracy, strong, weak, eventual strong and eventual weak, §2.3.

Two ways of chalking the dead and four of sparing the living make eight kinds of slate.

What does the last column promise? The least a slate can promise and still promise anything. Every living man may be chalked at one time or another, over and over, by everyone. All that is asked is that, from some moment nobody can see, one man who lives is left alone. Does any man know when that moment has come, or who that man is? No.

Is chalking the dead worth anything on its own? Nothing. A slate that chalks every tent all night chalks every dead man, and tells nobody anything. It is the sparing of the living that makes a slate useful.

Eight kinds of slate. Must the four study all eight, or do some of them come to the same thing?

Chapter III

Copying Marks

The two rows of the table are really one. If only some living man chalks the dead, can the rest be brought to chalk them too? Yes: the men make every living man do so by copying one another's marks.

Copying marks Every man keeps a second slate, his copy. Again and again he sends every tent the marks on his own slate. When a bird comes from a tent, he adds its marks to his copy, and rubs out the mark against the tent the bird came from.
The reduction TD→D′, Fig. 3, §3.
Copying Marks

If some living man in the end chalks every dead man for good, then with copying every living man does. And the copy spares the living exactly as well as the slate it was copied from.

Proof — that copying makes every living man chalk the dead, and spares the living as before
  1. The dead. A dead man sends no more birds, so after his last bird arrives nobody rubs him out. The living man who in the end chalks him for good goes on sending his marks, and every living man adds them to his copy. So in the end every living man's copy chalks him for good.
  2. Never chalked, or one never chalked. A man that nobody's slate has chalked is on nobody's list of marks, so nobody copies a mark against him.
  3. In the end. Suppose from some time no living man's slate chalks a certain living man. The dead stop sending, so in the end no bird carries a mark against him. He goes on sending, and each bird from him rubs him out of every copy. So in the end no copy chalks him.∎

So only the top row matters: perfect, strong, in the end perfect, and in the end strong. Which of these are needed? The two middle ones, strong and in the end strong. A perfect slate is also strong, and one in the end perfect is also in the end strong, so whatever works with the weaker works with the stronger.

Start with the stronger of the two. If one living man is never chalked by anyone, and nobody knows which, is that enough to agree?

Chapter IV

One Man Never Chalked

Suppose the slates are strong: every living man in the end chalks the dead, and one man who lives, say Number 2, is never chalked by anyone. Nobody knows it is Number 2. Can the four agree? They can, however many of them die, even three.

Each man keeps a tally of sightings: a row for each tent, holding that tent's sighting if he has learned it, and blank if not. At first it holds only his own.

The arrangement for a strong slate Spreading, three rounds. In each round, a man sends every tent the sightings he learned for the first time in the round before (in the first round, his own). He waits for that round's bird from every tent, except a tent he has chalked; if he chalks a tent while waiting, he stops waiting for it. He writes any new sightings into his tally.
Comparing. He sends every tent his whole tally, and waits in the same way. Any row that is blank in any tally he receives, he blanks in his own.
Committing. He commits to the answer in the first row of his tally that is not blank, taking the rows in number order.
The algorithm of Fig. 5, §6.1: n − 1 = 3 asynchronous rounds of Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3.

Why does it work? The whole thing turns on Number 2. Nobody ever chalks him, so everybody waits for every one of his birds. Whatever he has learned by a round, everyone who finishes the next round has learned too. By the end of spreading, every tally that finishes holds everything Number 2's holds. In comparing, everyone waits for his tally, and blanks every row that is blank in his; and every row filled in his is filled in every tally, so nobody blanks it. So everyone who reaches the end holds exactly Number 2's tally, which is not empty, since it has his own sighting. They all commit to the same answer, an answer someone sighted.

Could anyone wait for ever? No: a dead man is chalked in the end, and a live man's bird comes in the end. So everyone who lives commits.

One Man Never Chalked

With strong slates, the four meet all three demands however many of them are killed.

Proof — that with a strong slate everyone who finishes holds the same tally
  1. Every row of every tally is either blank or that tent's true sighting, since sightings are only ever copied.
  2. Let c be the man never chalked. Everyone who finishes a round received c's bird for that round, since he waited for it.
  3. After spreading, every finished tally holds all of c's. Take a sighting c first learned in round r. If r is before the last round, c sends it in round r + 1 and everyone who finishes that round has it. If r is the last round, three rounds among four men means the sighting reached c through every other man, each of whom wrote it into his tally before passing it on.
  4. After comparing, every finished tally equals c's. A row filled in c's tally is filled in every tally sent in comparing, by the last step, so nobody blanks it. A row blank in c's is blanked by everyone, since everyone waits for c's tally.
  5. c's own row is filled in c's tally and so in everyone's, so every finished tally has a first filled row, the same in all. Everyone who commits, commits to that tent's sighting.
  6. Nobody waits for ever, since a dead man is in the end chalked by every living man and a live man's birds all arrive. So every living man commits.∎

Who is in charge? Nobody. Everybody sends everything to everybody, and the man who holds it together is one nobody can name.

But a slate that never, from dusk on, chalks one living man is a great deal to ask. What if the slates are right about someone only in the end?

Chapter V

In the End, One Man Spared

Suppose less, then: the slates are only in the end strong. For as long as the slates like, every man may be chalked by everyone. Only from some moment nobody sees is one living man left alone. Can the four still agree? They can, provided more than half of them live. With four men, one may die.

How? They take turns. Each round belongs to one tent, in number order: 1, 2, 3, 4, then 1 again. The round number decides whose turn it is, not anyone's command, as on the windy nights*. Each man carries an estimate, an answer, which starts as his sighting, and beside it the round in which he last took it up.

A round, for slates in the end strong (1) Every man sends the round's owner his estimate and the round he took it up.
(2) The owner waits for three of these, himself included, and takes the estimate taken up most recently. He sends it to every tent.
(3) Every man waits for the owner's estimate, or until he chalks the owner. If the estimate comes, he takes it up, notes this round, and sends the owner agreed. If he chalks the owner first, he sends not agreed.
(4) The owner waits for three replies. If all three say agreed, he sends every tent a parting bird naming his estimate.
Committing. A man who takes in a parting bird for the first time sends it on to every tent, then commits to its answer.
The rotating-coordinator algorithm of Fig. 6, §6.2; three is ⌈(n + 1)/2⌉ for n = 4. The parting bird is Reliable Broadcast of the decision, Fig. 4.

Why do they never commit to two answers? Take the first round in which three men sent agreed, say on hold. Those three took up hold in that round. Any later owner waits for three estimates, and any three out of four share a man with those three, so the owner sees at least one hold taken up that recently or later. Take the estimate taken up most recently, and by the same argument round after round, it is hold. So every later owner sends hold, and every parting bird names hold.

Why do they commit? Nobody waits for ever: a dead owner is in the end chalked, and three men live to reply. From some moment one living man is never chalked. When his turn next comes, he gathers three estimates, everyone waits for his, nobody chalks him, all say agreed, and his parting bird goes out.

In the End, One Man Spared

With slates that are only in the end strong, the four meet all three demands provided more than half of them live.

Proof — that the turns never commit anyone to two answers, and in the end commit everyone to one
  1. No two answers. Let r be the first round in which three men sent agreed to its owner, on estimate e. Each of them took up e in round r. Show by induction that every owner of a round k ≥ r who sends an estimate sends e. For k = r it is so. For later k, the owner heard from three men; one of them is among the three of round r, and reported an estimate taken up in round r or later. So the most recent round t among the three satisfies r ≤ t < k, and the estimate taken up in round t was sent by that round's owner, which by induction was e.
  2. Every parting bird was first sent by an owner who had three agreed in a round no earlier than r, so every parting bird names e, and everyone who commits, commits to e. Every estimate is someone's sighting, so e was sighted.
  3. Committing. If anyone living takes in a parting bird, he sends it on before committing, so every living man takes it in and commits. Suppose instead nobody living ever commits. No living man is stuck for ever in any round: three men live to send estimates and replies, and a man waiting on an owner either gets his estimate or, if the owner is dead, in the end chalks him.
  4. From some time, one living man q is chalked by nobody, and the dead are all dead. Take a later round whose owner is q. He gathers three estimates and sends one; everyone waits for it, since nobody chalks him; three reply agreed; he sends a parting bird. That contradicts the supposition.∎

And while the slates are still chalking the living? Rounds fail: a man chalks a live owner whose bird is slow, says not agreed, and the round comes to nothing. Failing is always safe. The chalk can cost time; it can never cost agreement.

A strong slate bore any number of deaths; this one needs more than half alive. Is that a weakness of the arrangement, or would every arrangement need it?

Chapter VI

Why More Than Half, Then

Every arrangement would. The need is real, and it holds even for slates in the end perfect, which in the end chalk the dead and nobody else.

More Than Half

If two of the four may die, no arrangement meets the three demands, even with slates in the end perfect.

Proof — that slates in the end perfect cannot carry the four past two deaths
  1. Suppose some arrangement does. Divide the four into pairs: Numbers 1 and 2, Numbers 3 and 4.
  2. The first night. All sighted hold. Numbers 3 and 4 are dead from dusk, and the other two chalk them all night. That slate is in the end perfect. Numbers 1 and 2 commit, within some time A, and to hold, since only hold was sighted.
  3. The second night. The same with pairs and answers swapped: Numbers 3 and 4 commit to attack within some time B.
  4. The third night. Numbers 1 and 2 sighted hold, Numbers 3 and 4 attack. Nobody dies. Every bird between the pairs sits in the pines until after A and B, and until then each pair chalks the other; after that, all birds arrive and all chalk is rubbed out. That slate is in the end perfect too.
  5. Until then each pair sees exactly what it saw on its own night, and commits: one pair to hold, the other to attack. That contradicts the first demand.∎

What is the difference from a strong slate, then? When the slate is right. A strong slate never chalks one living man, from dusk; that man holds the four together from the start. A slate that is right only in the end may be wrong about everyone for as long as the arrangement needs it to be right, and while it is, the four are two pairs, each able to believe the other dead.

So a slate in the end strong, with more than half alive, is enough. But where does such a slate come from, and what has it left unsaid?

Chapter VII

What the Slate Does Not Say

Does it say how to keep one? No. The slate's promises are granted, not earned. On a night when no bird has a longest flight, and nothing more is known, no man can keep a slate that is even in the end weak: if he could, the four could agree on that night, and they cannot*. The slates of this book are an assumption about the hill, stated as a promise about chalk.

Can one ever be kept? On a windy night, yes. Where a wind blows for a while and then drops, and in still air every bird comes within a glass*, a man can keep a slate in the end perfect. Every man sends a bird to every tent, again and again, to say he is alive. A man chalks a tent when a glass has run out with no bird from it; when a bird from it comes after all, he rubs out the mark and waits longer for that tent next time. A dead man's birds stop, so he is chalked for good. Once the wind has dropped, a live man's birds come in time once the waiting is long enough, so in the end nobody living is chalked. The slate is a way of saying what the wind gives, without saying anything about wind.

Must it be right to be safe? No. If the slates break every promise, chalking the living for ever and never chalking the dead, the two arrangements may never commit anyone. They never commit two men to different answers. Only committing is lost.

What is the weakest slate that will do? In the end weak, with more than half alive, is enough. It is also necessary: any slate with which the four could agree can be made into one in the end weak. That was proved separately, and is not proved here.

What if the four had many things to decide? If they had to decide one after another, and take them in the same order, the same slates would do, and nothing weaker: agreeing once and agreeing on an order can each be turned into the other.

What follows from committing? Nothing. The four attack together or hold together. What happens then is not the subject of this book.

The ideas, plainly

Consensus is impossible in an asynchronous system with even one crash, because a crashed process cannot be told from a slow one. Chandra and Toueg add an unreliable failure detector: a module at each process that lists the processes it suspects, and may be wrong. They classify detectors by completeness (crashed processes are eventually suspected, by every or by some correct process) and accuracy (correct processes are not suspected: never, or eventually; all of them, or at least one).

Weak completeness can be turned into strong by gossiping suspicions, so four classes matter. With S, where some correct process is never suspected, consensus tolerates any number of crashes, by relaying proposals for n − 1 rounds. With ◇S, where some correct process is eventually never suspected, consensus needs a majority of correct processes and uses a rotating coordinator; safety holds however badly the detector behaves, and progress returns once it behaves. The majority is necessary even with ◇P, by a partition argument.

A failure detector packages timing assumptions as a statement about suspicions. In partially synchronous systems, growing timeouts implement ◇P; the detector lets algorithms be written without mentioning time at all. ◇W is the weakest detector for consensus, and consensus is equivalent to atomic broadcast.
