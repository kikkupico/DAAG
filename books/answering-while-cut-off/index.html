Answering While Cut Off

When part of a distributed system is cut off from the rest by a network failure, what should it do when asked a question? Should it answer immediately from its own copy of the data and risk giving an outdated reply, or refuse to answer until communication is restored? Can any system ever guarantee both immediate answers and up-to-date consistency?

To find out, we follow the four Greek mercenaries at the foot of Mount Phyle on a night when hunting hawks intercept their ravens, cutting one tent off from the others while they try to keep track of their shared battle plan.

After Eric A. Brewer, Towards Robust Distributed Systems, 2000 · Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch, Brewer's Conjecture and the Feasibility of Consistent, Available, Partition-Tolerant Web Services, 2002

First, in machine terms

Many copies, one service

Suppose a service stores copies of its data across several machines, and clients can read or write at any machine.

We naturally want three things from such a service: Consistency (every read sees the most recent completed write, as if all clients were talking to a single copy), Availability (every request to a working machine eventually gets an answer), and Partition tolerance (the system keeps its promises even when the network drops messages, splitting the machines into isolated groups that cannot reach one another).

  • Brewer (2000) conjectured, and Gilbert and Lynch (2002) proved, the CAP theorem: no distributed service can guarantee all three at once. When a network partition cuts one group of machines off from another, a machine asked to read must either wait (sacrificing availability) or answer from its own local copy, which may be out of date (sacrificing consistency).
  • Gilbert and Lynch (2012) placed this result in a wider frame: CAP is a fundamental clash between a safety guarantee (never return a stale answer) and a liveness guarantee (always return an answer).

Because real networks cannot prevent cables from being cut or routers from failing, partitions are a fact of life—and the real design question is how to trade off consistency and availability when they happen, and how quickly to recover once the network heals. We tell the story through the four mercenaries, their ravens, and a flock of hunting hawks.

The foot of Mount Phyle

Four tents, out of sight of one another

Four mercenaries are camped at the foot of Mount Phyle, one to a tent, hidden from one another by the folds of the hillside.* Bandits hold the summit above them, and the four keep in touch across the gullies only by messenger raven.

A known flightUntil the final section of this book, the air is calm: any raven that is not intercepted reaches its destination within a known flight time.
A hawk may take any birdHunting hawks prowl the ravines. A hawk may snatch any raven in mid-flight; nobody sees the bird taken, and it never arrives.
Nobody stops, nobody commitsOn these nights no mercenary is killed, no one lies, and nobody steps out to commit. Instead, the four maintain a shared standing plan that any of them can update or query during the night.
A plan to follow

The standing plan

Throughout the night, the four mercenaries keep a standing plan—the current choice (attack or hold) that they intend to follow when the time comes. At dusk, the standing plan starts at hold.

As the night wears on, any mercenary who spots movement on his side of the mountain may move (write) the standing plan to the opposite answer, and at any time any mercenary may ask (read) which plan currently stands. Each tent keeps its own written note of the plan. A move is complete once the man who made it is told that his change stands; a question is complete once the man who asked is given an answer of attack or hold.

What makes tonight different from the other stories at the foot of the hill is that ravens can be lost. Hunting hawks circle the folds of the mountain and can snatch a raven out of the air so that it never arrives. Because nobody can see into the ravines, a man waiting for a raven only learns it was taken when the maximum flight time has elapsed and his perch is still empty—and even then, he has no idea what message the lost bird was carrying. Worse still, when hawks settle in the ravine around one tent, every bird flying to or from that tent is taken for as long as the hawks stay there. That tent is cut off (partitioned) from the rest of the camp.

Try sending ravens across the hill below. With no hawks in the sky, every bird lands within a single flight. Then bring the hawks to Tent 4's ravine, send a bird between Tent 2 and Tent 4, and let a flight pass.

Now imagine that Tent 4 is cut off by hawks. Number 4 is alive and alert inside his tent, and he asks which plan currently stands. What should Tent 4 answer—and can any arrangement satisfy everything the four men want at once?

Two debts, and a condition

What the four owe each other

The mercenaries want their arrangement for keeping the standing plan to pay two debts at the same time—even on nights when hawks are taking birds.

The first debt governs what an answer must say; the second governs whether an answer is always given; and the condition is that ravens may be lost.

First debt

Answering as one

Although each tent keeps its own note, the camp as a whole must behave as if all four men were reading and updating a single shared record (atomic consistency, or linearizability).* In particular, once Number 2 has moved the plan to attack and been told that his move stands, anyone who asks afterward at any tent must hear attack (unless an even later move has changed it again).

Second debt

Every man is answered

Whenever a mercenary moves the plan or asks which plan stands, he must eventually receive an answer (availability). There is no fixed deadline for how quickly the answer must come, only that he cannot be left waiting forever.

The condition

Birds may be lost

Hawks may snatch any raven—including every raven between one tent and the rest of the camp—and the two debts above must still be honored (partition tolerance).

Look at two naive ways a tent might try to behave when Number 2 has already moved the plan to attack, hawks are circling, and Number 3 asks which plan stands. If Tent 3 simply answers from its local note of hold right away, it pays the second debt (Number 3 gets an answer) but breaks the first (the answer is stale). If Tent 3 waits for news from the others until the hawks leave, it never gives a stale answer, but if the hawks stay all night, Number 3 waits forever—breaking the second debt. Try both below.

Two ways to answer badly Number 3 asks

Number 2 has moved the plan to attack and been told it stands. Later Number 3 asks which plan stands.

Could a cleverer protocol do better by using timing? After all, the mercenaries have two strong timing advantages tonight. First, each tent has a sandglass that runs at the same rate as the others, so every man can measure exactly how long he has been waiting (even if the four glasses were not started at the same moment). Second, there is no wind tonight: any raven that is not taken by a hawk is guaranteed to arrive within a known flight time.*

With known flight times and reliable sandglasses in every tent, can the four men design an arrangement that answers as one, answers every man, and tolerates lost birds all at once?

The camps of this book, stated formally
  1. On these nights there is no wind: any raven that is not taken by a hawk arrives within a known maximum flight time, and any mercenary processes an incoming raven within a known local time bound.
  2. Any raven may be lost, and between a cut-off tent and the rest of the camp every raven is lost for as long as the hawks remain in that ravine.
  3. Each tent has a sandglass running at the same rate, allowing each man to measure the duration of any wait, though the four glasses need not show the same time.

Gilbert & Lynch's partially synchronous model with arbitrary message loss, §4.1: a known bound tmsg on delivery of messages that are not lost, and local timers running at the same rate.

The first result

The cut-off tent

They cannot. Out of the three desires—answering as one, answering every man, and carrying on while birds can be lost—no arrangement can ever guarantee more than two. Eric Brewer first stated this trade-off as a conjecture in 2000, and Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch proved it in 2002.

The Cut-Off Tent (The CAP Theorem). When ravens can be lost, no arrangement of the four tents can both answer every man and answer as one—even when flights have a known time bound and every tent has an accurate sandglass.

The proof is simple and inescapable. Imagine two possible nights that both begin with the standing plan at hold and Tent 4 completely cut off by hawks. On the first night, Number 2 moves the plan to attack; on the second night, nobody moves the plan at all. Scroll through the two nights below and watch what Number 4 can observe inside Tent 4.

1 · Dusk

Tent 4 is cut off

At dusk the standing plan is hold. Hawks settle in Tent 4's ravine, snatching every raven that flies between Tent 4 and the other three tents.

2 · The first night

Number 2 moves the plan

On the first night, Number 2 moves the plan to attack. Because every man must be answered, Number 2 eventually receives confirmation that his move stands (say after some time T). Any ravens sent toward Tent 4 are taken by the hawks.

3 · The second night

Nobody moves the plan

On the second night, nobody moves the plan. After more than time T has passed in silence, Number 4 asks which plan stands. Because every man must be answered—and nobody has moved the plan from hold—Tent 4 must eventually answer hold.

4 · Put together

Both nights at once

Now combine the two nights: let Tents 1, 2, and 3 experience the first night (Number 2 moves the plan to attack, completing by time T), and then after time T let Number 4 ask which plan stands, just as on the second night.

5 · So

Number 4 says hold

Because the hawks intercept every raven between Tent 4 and the others, everything Number 4 sees—his note, his sandglass, and his empty perch—is 100% identical to the second night. So Tent 4 must give the exact same answer: hold. Yet Number 2's move to attack finished before Number 4 even asked! Answering as one is broken.

Notice that Number 4 did nothing foolish. From inside Tent 4, the combined night is completely indistinguishable from the quiet second night where nobody ever moved the plan—and on that quiet night, hold is the only valid answer! Because Tent 4 must eventually give some answer on both nights without receiving a single raven from outside, whatever answer is right on one night is guaranteed to be wrong on the other.

Notice too why known flight times and sandglasses could not save Number 4. Waiting a glass tells Number 4 that a bird may have been lost, or that no bird was sent—but no amount of watching sand fall can tell him whether Number 2 moved the plan on the other side of the hill. Nor is there anything special about slicing off one tent against three: if the hawks divide the camp into any two isolated groups, a move in one group followed by a question in the other triggers the exact same contradiction.

Since the four mercenaries cannot have all three guarantees at once, how can they achieve any two of them—and how close can they get to all three?

Proof · that no arrangement both answers every man and answers as one

In the paper's terms the tents are nodes, a lost bird is a lost message, a cut-off tent is a partition, answering every man is availability, and answering as one is atomic consistency. Theorem 2. In the partially synchronous model, with a known bound on delivery of messages that are not lost and local timers at the same rate, no algorithm guarantees both availability and atomic consistency in all executions, including those in which messages are lost. The two nights are the paper's executions α₁ and α′₂, put together they are its α, and T below is a time measured by the glass.

  1. Suppose some arrangement did both. Put tent 4 on one side and the other three on the other, and lose every bird between them for the whole of what follows.
  2. The first night. The plan stands at hold. Number 2 moves it to attack. Since every man is answered, Number 2 is told it stands. Nothing else happens anywhere. Call the time this takes T, by the glass.
  3. The second night. The plan stands at hold. Nothing happens anywhere for at least T. Then Number 4 asks which plan stands, and is answered. Nobody moved it, so answering as one gives hold.
  4. Put them together. Let the first night happen among the three and the second at tent 4, at the same time.   5. Number 4 cannot tell this from the second night. Number 4 began with the same note, the glass has run the same, and no bird has reached tent 4 in either. So Number 4 answers hold.

Any two of the three

The muster-master's arrangement

Although the four cannot have all three properties at once, they can easily have any two of the three. In fact, one simple arrangement—appointing one tent as the muster-master (a centralized primary node)—already delivers two of the three pairings.

Why appoint a muster-master? Whenever four tents each maintain an independent note of the plan, their notes can drift out of sync. If Number 2 changes his note to attack while Tent 3's note still says hold, the camp has two conflicting records. Since "answering as one" requires the camp to behave as if there were a single record, the simplest way to guarantee it is to actually keep a single master record at one tent—say Tent 1.

What the muster-master does. Being the muster-master gives Number 1 no special authority over strategy; his job is purely clerical. Tent 1 holds the only official note of the standing plan. Whenever a mercenary at Tent 2, 3, or 4 wants to move the plan, he sends a raven to Tent 1 and waits; Number 1 updates the master note and sends a confirmation raven back, and only when that raven lands is the move complete. Whenever a mercenary wants to ask which plan stands, he likewise sends a raven to Tent 1 and waits for Number 1 to read the master note and send back the answer. (Number 1 himself, already sitting in Tent 1, can update or read the note on the spot.)

Why this always answers as one. Because Number 1 handles incoming ravens one at a time in the order they arrive at Tent 1, every move and every question across the entire camp falls into a single, unambiguous sequence. Any move that finishes before a question begins must have been updated on Tent 1's note before the question's raven was answered. Thus every answer ever given in the camp is guaranteed to be consistent, whether hawks are out or not.

What it costs when hawks appear. Because Tents 2, 3, and 4 never answer a move or a question until a raven has flown to Tent 1 and returned, any tent cut off from Tent 1 by hawks waits forever for a reply. The muster-master's arrangement keeps the first debt (answering as one) on every night, and pays the second debt (answering every man) only when no birds are lost. Together with the opposite choice—letting each tent answer immediately from its own local note—this gives all three two-out-of-three pairings:

1 · Answering as one + Lost birds (CP)

The muster-master's arrangement

Every move and question goes through Tent 1. When Tent 4 is cut off by hawks, Number 4 gets no reply and waits silently, so no stale answer is ever given.

2 · Answering as one + Every man (CA)

The same arrangement, with no hawks

On a night when no ravens are lost, every bird to and from Tent 1 arrives promptly—so the muster-master's arrangement both answers as one and answers every man.

3 · Every man + Lost birds (AP)

Each tent answers from its own note

Every man answers immediately from whatever note sits in his own tent. Every question gets an instant reply even under the hawks, at the cost of returning stale answers.

Look closely at the third option: if each tent always answers immediately from its own local note, it can give a stale answer even on a calm night with no hawks at all (while a bird is still in flight). Yet on most nights, there are no hawks in the ravines! Surely the mercenaries should not have to give up consistency on hawk-free nights just to make sure nobody waits forever when hawks do show up.

The muster-master's arrangement, stated formally
  1. One designated tent (Tent 1, the muster-master) holds the authoritative note of the standing plan.
  2. A mercenary at any other tent completes a move or receives an answer to a question only when the muster-master's reply raven arrives.
  3. The muster-master processes incoming ravens one at a time, in the order they arrive.

The centralized algorithm, §3.2.1–3.2.2.

Proof · that the muster-master's arrangement answers as one

In the paper's terms the muster-master is the designated node of the centralized algorithm, and answering as one is atomic consistency. Claim. Ordering all moves and questions by the moment the muster-master dealt with them, every answer is what a single record would say, each takes effect between its start and its answer, and anything finished before another began comes first.

  1. Order all moves and questions by the moment the muster-master dealt with them. This is one order, the same whichever tent the man was at.
  2. Every answer is the muster-master's, from the muster-master's one note in that order, so each is what a single record would say.
  3. The muster-master deals with a man's business after it began, since the bird was sent then, and before it ends, since the answer waits for the muster-master. So each takes effect between its start and its answer, and anything finished before another began was dealt with first.∎
A glass to wait

Waiting a glass

With their sandglasses and a known flight time, the mercenaries can get the best of both worlds on calm nights: they can answer every man on every night, and still answer as one on every night when no bird is lost.

How? By combining the muster-master's arrangement with a timeout measured by the sandglass. Because any raven that is not taken by a hawk lands within one flight time, a round trip to Tent 1 and back (plus Number 1's brief moment to write a reply) never takes longer than one sandglass. If a man waits a full glass and still has no reply from Tent 1, he knows for certain that a hawk took either his raven or Tent 1's reply—and only then does he fall back to his own local note:

First

Start a glass

Whenever a man moves the plan or asks which plan stands, he sends a raven to the muster-master at Tent 1 and flips his sandglass (sized for two flights plus Tent 1's reply time).

Second

The answer comes in time

If the muster-master's reply arrives before the sand runs out, he uses the muster-master's answer.

Third

The glass runs out first

If the sandglass runs empty first, a hawk took the bird. The man stops waiting and answers from his own tent's note: a move is declared to stand locally, and a question returns the latest plan Tent 1 has sent him (or his own local move, if he made one since).

The glass earlier tonight Number 2 moved the plan to attack

Number 4's glass

Why does waiting a glass work so well? On any night when no bird is lost, no sandglass ever runs out, so every move and question is handled by Tent 1 in a single consistent order. And on nights when hawks do snatch a bird, the sandglass runs out and the waiting mercenary answers from his own note, so nobody ever waits forever. Consistent answers are sacrificed only when ravens are actually lost.

Look closely, however, at what happens after the hawks leave. Suppose that while Tent 4 was cut off by hawks, Number 4 moved the plan to hold, his raven was taken, and after one glass his move stood locally at Tent 4. Then the hawks fly away. Because that single raven was lost and nothing tells Tent 4 to resend it, Tent 1 never learns of Number 4's move! Hours after the sky is completely clear of hawks, Tents 1, 2, and 3 are still operating under a different plan from Tent 4. You can see this in the interactive above: bring the hawks to Tent 4, have Number 4 move the plan to hold, send the hawks away, and then let Number 2 ask.

A single lost bird during a brief visit by the hawks has left the camp permanently out of sync. To make sure the damage from a partition is only temporary, the mercenaries need a way to catch up once the hawks leave.

Waiting a glass, stated formally
  1. A mercenary who moves the plan or asks sends a raven to the muster-master and starts a sandglass lasting two flight times plus the muster-master's processing time.
  2. If the muster-master's reply arrives before the glass runs out, the mercenary uses that reply.
  3. If the glass runs out first, the mercenary answers from his tent's local note: a question receives the latest plan received from the muster-master (unless the mercenary has made a local move since, in which case it receives that move), and a move is declared to stand.

A timeout of 2tmsg + tlocal, §4.3.

The damage ends

How stale, and for how long

With one more refinement, the four mercenaries can guarantee that the damage from lost birds ends within a fixed span of time after the hawks leave (Delayed-t consistency). Nobody can control how long the hawks stay, of course, but the four can strictly bound how long it takes the camp to heal once the hawks are gone.

Healing requires two things: any move made at a cut-off tent must eventually reach the muster-master at Tent 1, and the muster-master's updated plan must then reach every tent. Because ravens can arrive out of order, every bird a mercenary sends to Tent 1 carries—in order—all of his moves that Tent 1 has not yet acknowledged, and he keeps resending them periodically until Tent 1 acknowledges them. As each move reaches Tent 1, the muster-master assigns it the next sequential stamp (1, 2, 3, …) and broadcasts the current plan along with its stamp to every tent, resending until acknowledged. Each tent always keeps whichever plan has the highest stamp it has seen. Scroll through the steps below to watch how a move made while Tent 4 is cut off propagates through the camp once the hawks leave.

1 · Cut off

Number 4 moves the plan

Hawks circle Tent 4's ravine. Number 4 moves the plan to attack and sends a raven to Tent 1. The hawk takes the bird, and after one glass Number 4's move stands locally at Tent 4.

2 · Sent again

Until acknowledged, send it again

Number 4 keeps resending his unacknowledged move at regular intervals (frequently enough that once the sky clears, a bird is guaranteed to reach Tent 1 within half the recovery span). While the hawks remain, each bird is taken.

3 · The hawks leave

One bird gets through

As soon as the hawks leave, Number 4's next raven reaches Tent 1. The muster-master updates the master note to attack and gives the move the next stamp.

4 · The muster-master sends it round

Every tent learns the plan

The muster-master broadcasts the updated plan and its new stamp to every tent, resending to any tent that has not yet acknowledged it (frequently enough to arrive within the second half of the recovery span).

5 · A span

No bird is lost for a span

Once a single full span has elapsed without any lost birds, every move finished before that span has been stamped at Tent 1 and delivered to all four tents.

6 · A question

Anyone asking now hears attack

Any question asked at any tent after that calm span is guaranteed to see Number 4's move (or an even later move). When Number 3 asks now, he hears attack.

The Span (Delayed-t Consistency). The muster-master's arrangement with resending and stamps answers every man on every night, answers as one on any hawk-free night, and guarantees that once no birds are lost for a span of length t, any operation completed before that span is visible to any operation that starts after it.

What if two mercenaries move the plan in opposite ways while one of them is cut off? Suppose the plan starts at attack (stamp 1), and while Tent 4 is cut off, Number 2 moves the plan to hold (which reaches Tent 1 right away and gets stamp 2) while Number 4 moves the plan to attack (which times out locally at Tent 4). When the hawks leave and Number 4's resend finally reaches Tent 1, the muster-master stamps Number 4's move with stamp 3 and broadcasts it to everyone. Both moves are ordered cleanly by their stamps (stamp 2, then stamp 3), and after one calm span all four tents agree on stamp 3! During the partition itself, of course, the two sides saw different plans—which the Cut-Off Tent proved no available protocol can avoid. Step through this two-writer scenario below.

Notice that every part of this recovery scheme relied on a known maximum flight time: waiting a single glass to detect a lost bird, spacing the resends, and measuring the recovery span itself. What happens if the mountain wind rises again, so that a raven can sit in the pines for an arbitrary amount of time?

The promise of the span, stated formally

All the moves and questions can be set in one order P, with t the span, fixed in advance and measured by the glass, in which:

  1. all moves are ordered among themselves, and every question is placed relative to every move;
  2. every question is answered with the plan of the last move before it in P, or hold if there is none;
  3. each man's own business keeps its order in P;
  4. if no bird was ever lost, P answers as one;
  5. and whatever happens, if something was finished, and then a span longer than t passed with no bird lost, and only then something else began, the first comes before the second.

Delayed-t consistency, Definition 3.

The muster-master's arrangement with a span, stated formally

At every tent, always: every bird from the muster-master carries the plan and its stamp, and a man goes by the one with the highest stamp to have come. Every bird a man sends the muster-master carries all that man's unacknowledged moves, in order.

A question: send to the muster-master and wait a glass. If the muster-master answers in time, give that answer. If not, give the latest of the asker's own unacknowledged moves, or if there is none, the plan with the highest stamp the asker holds.

A move: send to the muster-master and wait a glass. It stands when the muster-master acknowledges it or the glass runs out, whichever is first. Until acknowledged, send it again, often enough that with no bird lost it reaches the muster-master within half of t.

At the muster-master: each move that reaches the muster-master, taken in its sender's order, changes the muster-master's note and takes the next stamp. The muster-master sends the plan and stamp to every tent, again and again to any that has not acknowledged it, often enough that with no bird lost they arrive within half of t.

The modified centralized algorithm, Theorem 4.

Proof · that the muster-master's arrangement with a span keeps the promise

In the paper's terms (Definition 3 and Theorem 4), t is the span, fixed in advance and measured by the local timers, and P is an order on all the moves and questions of a night. Claim. On every night the muster-master's arrangement with a span answers every man, and admits an order P satisfying clauses (1) to (5) of the promise of the span, stated above.

  1. The order. Order the moves by the muster-master's stamps; a move that never reaches the muster-master goes after all the stamped ones, in the order it was made. Place each question immediately after the move whose plan it was given, or at the start if it was given hold from dusk. Then every question is answered with the last move before it: clauses (1) and (2).
  2. Each man's own order. A man's moves reach the muster-master in the order they were made, since each bird carries all the earlier unacknowledged ones first; so the muster-master stamps them in that order, and has stamped them before answering that man's question. A man never answers with a lower stamp than one already held, and that man's own unacknowledged moves are newer than anything the muster-master has sent. So each man's questions land after that man's earlier business: clause (3).
  3. No bird lost. Then no glass runs out, every answer is the muster-master's, and this is the plain muster-master's arrangement, which answers as one: clause (4).
  4. A move, then a span. Suppose a move by Number 4 was finished, then more than t passed with no bird lost. In that span the move reaches the muster-master and the muster-master's stamp for it reaches every tent, each within half of t. So any move begun after the span gets a higher stamp, and any question begun after it is answered with this move or a later one.
  5. A question, then a span. The plan a finished question was given came from the muster-master or from a move of the asker's own. Either way, within the span that move is stamped and sent to every tent, and the last step places everything begun after the span later in P: clause (5).∎

The paper's own proof of this is a few lines long, and says nothing about how a man should treat moves of that man's own that the muster-master has not yet acknowledged; the second step supplies it.

Everything leaned on a known flight

When the flight has no bound

When the wind blows and flights have no time bound (an asynchronous network), waiting a glass no longer tells a mercenary whether his raven was taken by a hawk or is merely sitting in the pines.

On a windy night, a raven can be delayed for any length of time.* Because a waiting mercenary cannot distinguish a lost bird from a slow one, an even harsher impossibility takes hold:

The Wind (Asynchronous CAP Corollary). When flight times have no bound, no arrangement that guarantees an answer to every man on every night can guarantee answering as one—even on nights when not a single bird is lost!

Without a known bound on flight time, a slow bird looks identical to a lost bird—so any protocol that stays available during partitions will also return stale answers whenever messages run late.

Proof · that in a wind, answering as one cannot even be promised when nothing is lost

In the paper's terms a night is an execution, and in the asynchronous model there is no bound on message delay. Corollary 1.1. No algorithm that guarantees availability in every execution guarantees atomic consistency in every execution in which no message is lost.

  1. Suppose an arrangement answers every man on every night, and answers as one on every night on which no bird is lost.
  2. By the Cut-Off Tent, which holds in a wind as well, there is a night on which a bird is lost and a wrong answer is given. It is given after only so much has happened.
  3. Take that night up to the wrong answer. Let every bird that was lost instead be still in the pines, arriving afterwards; in a wind nobody can tell the difference. Continue the night so that every bird arrives.
  4. On this night no bird is lost, and up to the wrong answer it is the same night, so the wrong answer is still given. That contradicts the supposition.∎
Four questions

What being cut off does not say

Because the CAP theorem is so famous, it is often overstated. Four questions clarify its exact boundaries.

Is this the camps' impossibility?

No—the FLP impossibility result in The Limits of Agreement is a cousin, not the same theorem.* Both pit a safety property against a liveness property, but under different assumptions: in FLP, no ravens are ever lost, flights have no time bound, and one man may die; in CAP, no man dies, flights have a known time bound, and ravens can be lost.

Is it about a dead man?

No. A dead man asks no questions and is owed no answers. The Cut-Off Tent is about a living, working mercenary (Number 4) who is cut off from his comrades by lost messages and still demands a reply.

Does it cover everything the four know?

No—it applies only to state that can change and must stay coordinated across tents. Facts that never change (like which tent number each man occupies) or monotonic facts that require no coordination (Knowing When to Wait) can be answered locally at any time without ever being wrong.

Must the four choose once for all?

No. A real system does not have to make a single blanket choice for everything it does. It can insist on the muster-master's strict consistency for critical updates while allowing local notes to answer routine queries, or bound staleness using a recovery span. CAP simply warns us where the trade-off must be paid whenever a partition strikes.

A distributed service cannot be simultaneously consistent, available, and partition-tolerant.

Because network partitions are unavoidable in the real world, the true choice arrives when a link fails: either wait and refuse some requests to keep every answer consistent, or answer every request locally and allow copies to diverge until the partition heals.

This is one instance of a wider truth: safety ("never return a wrong answer") and liveness ("always return an answer") often cannot both be guaranteed in an unreliable network, so practical systems tailor the trade-off by data type and by operation.

The papers behind this book

Eric A. Brewer, Towards Robust Distributed Systems. Invited talk, 19th ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), Portland, Oregon, July 2000. — Formulates the CAP conjecture: among Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance, a distributed shared-data system can achieve at most two.

Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch, Brewer's Conjecture and the Feasibility of Consistent, Available, Partition-Tolerant Web Services. ACM SIGACT News 33, 2 (June 2002), 51–59. — Formalizes Brewer's conjecture using atomic consistency (linearizability), proves the impossibility in both asynchronous and partially synchronous models (Theorems 1 and 2, Corollary 1.1), and presents the centralized algorithm and Delayed-t consistency (Theorem 4).

Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch, Perspectives on the CAP Theorem. IEEE Computer 45, 2 (February 2012), 30–36. — Explains CAP as a trade-off between safety and liveness, compares it with the FLP impossibility result, and discusses how practical systems partition the trade-off.

The ideas, plainly. In 2000, Eric Brewer observed from practical experience building large-scale web services that a distributed shared-data system can simultaneously provide at most two of three desirable properties: Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance. Two years later, Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch gave the conjecture a precise mathematical footing and proved it. They defined Consistency as linearizability (atomic read/write registers, where every read reflects the latest completed write), Availability as the guarantee that every request received by a non-failing node eventually returns a response, and Partition tolerance as the requirement that the system satisfy its guarantees even when the network drops arbitrary messages between isolated subsets of nodes.

The proof is a direct indistinguishability argument. Partition the nodes into two isolated groups, G1 and G2, dropping all messages between them. Suppose a client writes a new value in G1 and waits until the write completes, and afterwards another client reads in G2. Because no messages have crossed the partition, the nodes in G2 cannot distinguish this execution from one in which no write ever occurred in G1. If G2 must be available, it must eventually return a value—and that value must be the initial value, violating linearizability. Local clocks and known message delivery bounds do not prevent this contradiction, because a timeout only reveals that messages were lost, not what value was written on the other side.

When networks are partially synchronous (messages that are not dropped arrive within a known bound tmsg and nodes have synchronized timer rates), a system can achieve consistency and availability in all executions where no messages are lost, while falling back to local responses when a timeout detects message loss. By piggybacking unacknowledged writes and version stamps, the system can also guarantee Delayed-t consistency: once the network heals and delivers messages reliably for a span of length t, consistency is fully restored. In a purely asynchronous network (where message delays are unbounded), even that fallback fails: any always-available protocol can return stale answers even when no messages are ever lost.
