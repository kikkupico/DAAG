A Council of Five Keeping One Law

A council of five, one of whom holds the podium and alone writes the law, sending it line by line to the others' benches by runner.

Read this first, and the book set in Schedia can be read on its own. It says who the council are, what carries word between them and what the notes say. The city and its round hall come last.

The Council of Five

Schedia is governed by a council of five legislators, Okios, Liskovia, Kleon, Melissa and Theron. They serve part time, coming and going as Lamport's legislators do, and nobody lies. Each sits at a bench of their own with a copy of the city's law, which grows line by line, and every copy must hold the same lines in the same order.

One of the five holds the podium, and the podium is an office, not a pulpit. Only its holder may write a new line of law; the others copy what the holder sends them. Each spell at the podium has its own seal, a round stamp laid out like the Tholos, with a slot for a signet in the middle and one at each bench's place; every signet is cut with its owner's initial. The middle holds the signet of the legislator who called the seal and a number; the ring holds the signets of the others who answered the call, and an empty slot leaves no mark; a wreath goes round the holder's signet, or round the middle when the manager holds the podium. The holder presses the seal beside every line and keeps the office for as long as that seal stands. When the podium falls silent, any legislator who notices may call a seal with a higher number, and a new holder takes over.

The law is kept in wax, not ink. Each legislator's tablets stay on their bench and are smoothed when they go home; only the bench's name and the middle of the last seal it served under are cut into its stone. A legislator who steps out comes back to their tablets. One who goes home comes back to smooth wax and must be brought up to date from the others. If a majority went home at once, the law would be lost.

Citizens at the doors send their petitions to the podium, and wait there for an answer.

Runners

Word goes by messenger, as in the Chamber. The podium has a runner for each bench, in a short brown tunic, who carries the holder's lines out to that bench and the bench's word back. A runner may be delayed, but they carry the lines in order and never skip one, so a bench that holds a line holds every line before it.

Nothing else carries word. Nobody can be heard across the hall, from the podium or anywhere else, and a holder who steps out and misses a new seal learns of it only when a note reaches them.

What the Notes Say

Every line in the Tholos carries its seal, and authority rides on that seal, not on standing at the podium. A holder who stepped out and missed the new seal can go on sending lines under the old one, but settles nothing: any majority they reach includes a legislator who has taken the new seal and refuses the old one.

The City and the Tholos

Schedia is a small city on the eastern arm of Paxos, above a sheltered cove on the bay, facing the Chamber across the water. Its name means raft. It was founded by settlers from a different mother city, who came by sea and brought their founders' laws with them, and the Parliament's decrees have never run on the eastern arm. That was ordinary Greek practice: the small island of Amorgos held three independent cities, each settled from elsewhere and each with its own laws. So Paxos has two cities and two law scrolls, which never contradict each other.

The council meets in the Tholos, a round hall on a terrace above the town, the same kind of hall as the Chamber, with the same poor acoustics and one door. It has one thing the Chamber lacks: the podium at the centre, where every bench is the same distance away. So the runners go out from the centre, where in the Chamber messengers cross the floor in every direction.

The name is Athenian. In Athens the Tholos was the round building of the prytaneis, the committee on duty in turn, with one member presiding.

What each part of this place has in common with the real Greek world, so that a reader can tell what is borrowed from what is made up. Close means the real thing is much like it; loose means it is borrowed and changed; invented means the setting made it up; anachronism means the real thing is of another age.
