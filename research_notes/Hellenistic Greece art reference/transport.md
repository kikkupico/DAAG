# Hellenistic Greek Transportation: ships, harbours, roads, vehicles, trade routes

Scope note: Hellenistic (323-31 BC), Aegean focus. Research budget was about 15 tool calls; web sources were mostly Wikipedia and ancientportsantiques.com summaries. Items from background knowledge that I could not source in this pass are listed under Gaps as "from memory, verify", not as findings.

## Merchant ships (hull, sail, steering, anchors, stowage, size)

### Takeaway
The best Hellenistic Aegean merchantman evidence is the Kyrenia ship (launched c. 315 BC, sank c. 294 BC): a 14.7 x 4.4 m round-hulled pine ship with one square brailed sail, a crew of about four, one anchor with lead-filled stock, and a cargo of 381 amphorae. Much larger ships existed (250-500 tons; Madrague de Giens is later, c. 75-60 BC, and Roman-period Italian).

### Cited Findings
- Kyrenia ship: 14.7 x 4.4 m (48 x 14 ft), shell-first construction, mainly pine, mortise-and-tenon planking with copper nails fixing the frames; launched c. 315 BC, sank c. 294 BC after a long working life with many repairs — [Wikipedia, Kyrenia ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrenia_ship)
- Kyrenia final repair added lead sheathing 1.5-2 mm thick over the hull — [Wikipedia, Kyrenia ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrenia_ship)
- Kyrenia rig: a single square sail; about 100 lead rings from a brail (brailing) system were found, i.e. the sail was gathered up to the yard by cords through rings — [Wikipedia, Kyrenia ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrenia_ship)
- Kyrenia crew: evidence (four cups, dining implements in the cabin pottery) suggests a crew of four — [Wikipedia, Kyrenia ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrenia_ship)
- Kyrenia anchor: only one anchor found, a one-armed wooden hook design with a lead-filled stock (the ancient term "stock anchor") — [Wikipedia, Kyrenia ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrenia_ship)
- Kyrenia cargo: 381 amphorae (340 from Rhodes, others from Knidos, Samos, Kos, Palestine, Egypt, Cyprus) holding wine, olive oil, and over 9,000 almonds; also 29 millstones and iron billets — [Wikipedia, Kyrenia ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrenia_ship)
- Kyrenia II replica completed 1985 at Manolis Psaros boatyard, Piraeus, by Harry Tzalas (HIPNT) with Michael Katzev and Richard Steffy; toured widely (Mystic Seaport, Seville, Nara, Hamburg, Greek towns) — [search summary citing Wikipedia/Past Mariners](https://www.pastmariners.org/post/the-kyreneia-ii)
- The page on the wreck's hull dating differs in sources: one summary lists the original launch as "389 (±44) BC" (radiocarbon), versus c. 315 BC in the main article — [Kyrenia ship wiki PDF](https://ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/ETUDESarchivees/Navires/Kyrenia/KyreniaShipWiki.pdf); contrast [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrenia_ship)
- Large Greek merchant type "kerkouros" (c. 500-100 BC): average capacity 250 tons, up to 500; typical 21 x 3 m? (page gives "21 x 3 m, beam/length 1:7", up to 50 x 7 m; figures look inconsistent, treat with caution); combined rowing and sailing; usually docked stern first, alongside docking for crane loading — [Ancient Ports Antiques, merchant ships](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/ancient-ships/merchant-ships/)
- Greek ships came in varying sizes, the largest carrying as much as 500 tons — [Wikipedia, Travel in classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_in_classical_antiquity)
- Madrague de Giens wreck (c. 75-60 BC, off Giens, France; Roman-period but "Hellenistic type" hull): about 400 tons deadweight, 6,000-7,000 amphorae (mostly Latium wine, plus Campanian black-glazed ware), double planking reinforced with lead — [Wikipedia, Madrague de Giens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrague_de_Giens_(shipwreck)); [Ancient Ports Antiques search summary](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/ancient-ships/merchant-ships/)
- Syracusia (c. 240 BC, built by Archias of Corinth for Hieron II, said to be designed by Archimedes): ancient claims of 1,600-1,800 tons cargo (modern estimate c. 55 x 14 x 13 m, uncertain); eight towers with archers, garden, bath, library, gymnasium, temple of Aphrodite, Iliad mosaics, wooden Atlas figures; hull sealed with horsehair and pitch; one voyage to Alexandria, given to Ptolemy and renamed Alexandreia — [Wikipedia, Syracusia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracusia). Source is Athenaeus (Moschion); numbers are literary and uncertain.
- Lembos/lembus: general term for small ships, low freeboard, fast, civil and military; Illyrian pirates used them; Philip V ordered 100 built c. 216 BC for troop transport; term attested from 4th c. BC — [Wikipedia, Lembus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lembos)
- Trade finance: merchants borrowed for voyages, typically under 2,000 drachmas for short periods, interest often 12% but up to 100%; lender bore voyage risk, borrower pledged cargo and ship (bottomry; Classical Athenian evidence, Hellenistic practice continued) — [Wikipedia, Economy of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_ancient_Greece)
- Traders were emporoi (wholesale seaborne merchants); shipwreck counts show a large rise: 2 wrecks for the 8th c. BC vs 46 for the 4th c. BC — same source.

### Inferences
- For a small island scene, the Kyrenia-sized ship (about 15 m, 4-man crew, one square sail, single wooden stock anchor) is the safe, evidenced model; do not paint every merchantman as a large "Syracusia-type".
- Brail rigging means the sail is shown part-gathered to the yard when in port or manoeuvring (vertical cords through rings on the sail face).

### Gaps

Check Casson, Ships and Seafaring in Ancient Times, chap. on Hellenistic merchant ships, and Pomey 2014 (PDF at ancientportsantiques.com, Pomey2014-NaviresHellenist.pdf; my fetch returned unreadable binary, a local copy is saved at /Users/kikkupico/.claude/projects/-Users-kikkupico-Projects-DAAG/e3a51bed-5e2a-45b5-97df-be24b09148d3/tool-results/webfetch-1790890294030-u5fyk7.pdf and could be read with a PDF tool).

## Harbours (moles, quays, shipsheds, lighthouses, customs, warehouses, diolkos)

### Takeaway
Hellenistic harbours combined closed or mole-protected basins, long quays, roofed shipsheds for warships, warehouses behind the waterfront, and, at the great ports, lighthouses; Delos shows a modest-scale Aegean commercial waterfront, Alexandria the monumental extreme.

### Cited Findings
- Zea harbour (Piraeus): 196 shipsheds (neosoikoi) by 330 BC, large closed sheds with gabled roofs built over rock-cut slipways; Piraeus overall had almost 400 shipsheds; Philon's Skeuotheke (c. 330 BC) stored ships' equipment — [Gabrielsen, Piraeus PDF](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/PLACES/GreeceContinental/Piraeus-Gabrielsen2014.pdf); [Zea Harbour Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zea_Harbour_Project). Note this is Classical (4th c. BC) evidence; Hellenistic use continued but verify.
- Athens charged harbour duties at Piraeus (1% or higher, 5% imperial duty in 413 BC; Classical) — [Wikipedia, Economy of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_ancient_Greece)
- Delos: sacred port about 200 m long, protected beach before the Apollo temple, large mole on the north, small vessels; commercial port about 800 m south with shops and warehouses lined along the waterfront and a narrow beach in front; modest transshipment; ships may have anchored offshore and negotiated cargo without unloading; sea level about 2.5 m higher than in antiquity; channel to Rheneia (Remmatia) 4-5 m deep — [Ancient Ports Antiques, Delos](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/a-few-ports/delos/)
- Delos became a free port after 166 BC (Roman rule), attracting Italian traders; Agora of the Italians — [search summary of Britannica/Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delos)
- Rhodes: commercial harbour south of the military harbour, protected on the west by a fortified mole built in the 4th c. BC; fortified towers on the moles; Colossus (c. 280 BC, about 33 m) probably did NOT straddle the harbour mouth — [Rhodes search summary](https://www.rhodesguide.com/travelguide/colossus_rhodes.php); [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Colossus_of_Rhodes/)
- Pharos of Alexandria: built 284-246 BC under Ptolemy II (commissioned by Ptolemy I); about 103-118 m high; three tiers (square, octagonal, circular); fire at the top and a mirror; Roman Alexandrian coins of the 2nd c. AD show Tritons at the corners and a statue on top (Roman evidence for appearance); Heptastadion causeway over 1,200 m created the Great Harbour (east) and Eunostos with the Kibotos basin (west); 1994 French survey found granite blocks, sphinxes, obelisks, columns on the harbour floor — [Wikipedia, Lighthouse of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighthouse_of_Alexandria)
- Diolkos (Isthmus of Corinth): paved limestone trackway, grooves about 1.6 m apart, 3.4-6 m wide, 6-8.5 km long, c. 600 BC to mid-1st c. AD; hauled by men and animals with ropes (maybe capstans); crossing took about 3 hours with 112-180 people; triremes feasible, cargo often transferred — [Wikipedia, Diolkos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diolkos)
- Samos has an excellent ancient mole and harbour (Polycrates, 6th c. BC, still in use Hellenistic) — [Wikipedia, Ancient harbour of Samos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_harbour_of_Samos) (title only; not read in detail)

### Inferences
- A small island harbour at Delos scale: pebble beach quay, a rubble mole, a row of low warehouse/shop units with wide doors on the water edge, ships anchored off or beached stern-first.
- Pharos is the only lighthouse needed for the Aegean-wide reference; small Aegean harbours more likely used a fire on a mole tower or cairn (uncertain, no source found).

### Gaps
- From memory, verify: cranes (polyspastos) on quays, slipways for hauling merchantmen, customs office (telonion) layout, Piraeus Emporion warehouses (stoai), Rhodes harbour chain/closing mechanism, mole tower lights. Not sourced in this pass.

## Land transport (roads, vehicles, riding, inns, speeds, couriers, signals)

### Takeaway
Greek roads were mostly unpaved tracks with local rutted sections and a few built sacred ways; goods went by pack animals or light carts, long hauls went by sea, and communication relied on runners, ships and beacon systems rather than a state post (the Ptolemaic and Seleucid royal posts are a Hellenistic exception I could not source).

### Cited Findings
- Greek roads were poorly developed due to political fragmentation and costs of mountain terrain; exceptions were roads between major cities and sanctuaries built to endure all weather; no state way-stations or milestones as in Rome — [Wikipedia, Travel in classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_in_classical_antiquity)
- Roads were dirt tracks, dusty in summer and muddy in winter; some cut with ruts for cart wheels; carts and wagons usually drawn by donkeys or mules; wagons need graded roads — [Encyclopedia.com, Roads and Land Traffic](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/roads-and-land-traffic); [historylink101](https://historylink101.com/2/greece3/travel-land.htm) (secondary; weak)
- Violence risk on roads; large entourage useful for protection — [Wikipedia, Travel in classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_in_classical_antiquity)
- Inns: a pandokeion offered meals, bed and stabling for mules for small copper payments, near sanctuaries (Olympia, Delphi) and crossroads; but other scholarship says little evidence of a formal inn system, some courtyard inns with stables and beds serving festival tourists — [Greece High Definition blog](https://www.greecehighdefinition.com/blog/2026/6/22/the-role-of-taverns-and-inns-in-ancient-greece) (weak source); [Wikipedia, Travel in classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_in_classical_antiquity)
- Phryctoriae: relay hilltop beacon towers, about 20 miles (30 km) apart; fire passes along; Cyclades beacon tradition; Aeneas Tacticus (4th c. BC) combined fires with water clocks; the pyrseia torch-letter system by Cleoxenes and Democleitos (Polybius, 2nd c. BC) — [Wikipedia, Phryctoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phryctoria); [History Skills](https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/year-7/ancient-greek-wartime-communication/)
- Couriers: hemerodromoi (day-runners), no postal network in Classical Greece; messenger ships — [Wikipedia, Hemerodromoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemerodromoi); [Wikipedia, Courier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courier)
- Hammond, "Army Transport in the Fifth and Fourth Centuries" (GRBS) covers wagons and pack animals, not read — [PDF](https://grbs.library.duke.edu/article/download/5991/5199)

### Inferences
- A rural Aegean island scene: narrow dry-stone-walled terrace paths, donkey/mule trains with panniers and pack-saddles, perhaps one two-wheeled ox-cart on a rutted track near the port; no paving except short stretches near sanctuaries.

### Gaps
- From memory, verify (unsourced): travel speeds (about 25-30 km/day on foot; Casson), wagon types (two-wheeled ox-cart, four-wheeled wagon, harmamaxa), horses ridden bareback or with a cloth/pad, no stirrups, no horseshoes; litters (phoreion/klinē) mainly for elite or invalids; stone bridges of Hellenistic corbelled type (Arkadiko bridge is Mycenaean); Ptolemaic royal post (Zenon papyri record dromoi letters); heralds (kerykes) as official messengers; Seleucid/Persian royal road. Not found in this pass.
- Chariots as racing vehicles vs. ordinary transport: not researched.

## Sailing seasons, navigation, trade routes

### Takeaway
Sailing was concentrated in late spring to early autumn; navigation was by coasting, landmarks and star/wind knowledge; main flows carried grain (Egypt, Black Sea/Bosporan, Sicily) to Aegean cities and wine/oil/ceramics outward.

### Cited Findings
- Hesiod (Works and Days): sail about fifty days after the solstice; spring sailing is possible when fig leaves reach a cow's footprint size but disliked; winter sea "closed" after the Pleiades set (Archaic, but the tradition shapes later practice) — [Hesiod, Perseus/Theoi/Loeb](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodWorksDays.html); [Tammuz 2005, Sailing Seasons](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/ETUDESarchivees/Navires/Documents/Tammuz2005-SailingSeasons.pdf)
- Greece exported wine, pottery, metalwork and imported grain from Sicily, Egypt, Bosporan kingdom — [Wikipedia, Economy of ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy_of_ancient_Greece)
- Kyrenia amphorae show an eastern Mediterranean coasting circuit: Rhodes, Kos, Knidos, Samos and Cyprus, Levant, Egypt — [Wikipedia, Kyrenia ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrenia_ship)
- Sea transport vastly cheaper than land: a Roman trading vessel could cross the Mediterranean in a month at one-sixtieth the cost of overland (Roman-period figure, from Diocletian's edict-based comparisons) — [Wikipedia, Ancient maritime history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_ships) (redirect page content)

### Inferences
- A scene dated to high summer shows the most ships; none under winter canvas except laid-up hulls hauled onto the beach.

### Gaps
- From memory, verify: periploi (Skylax's Periplous, Pseudo-Skylax c. 4th c. BC; Eratosthenes), Pytheas, Hipparchus; Rhodian maritime law and Rhodian sea-law (lex Rhodia), Rhodes' anti-piracy fleet; Delos slave and grain markets; Ptolemaic grain fleet to Rhodes and Athens; cargo wrecks of the Antikythera type (c. 70-60 BC). None sourced here.

## Rural transport (terraces, water, produce to port)

### Takeaway
I found no Hellenistic-specific sourced material in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- Delos sacred port served small vessels, and the commercial port had modest storage, implying small-scale local shipping from nearby islands — [Ancient Ports Antiques, Delos](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/a-few-ports/delos/)
- Carts and wagons were pulled by donkeys or mules; no state road system — [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/roads-and-land-traffic)

### Inferences
- Produce (oil in skin bags or pithoi on mules, wine in amphorae or skins, grain sacks) likely moved by donkey down terrace paths to a beach or small mole, then by lembos-type or small sailing boat; water carried in hydriai on the head (women) or on donkeys. Plausible but unsourced.

### Gaps
- No sourced data on terrace paths, water carriers, or island farm-to-port practice. Suggest Osborne, Classical Landscape with Figures, and Rackham/Moody on Aegean terraces.
