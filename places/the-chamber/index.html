Part-Time Legislators Passing Laws

Part-time legislators who wander in and out, each with their own scroll, passing laws by notes that messengers may carry late, twice or never.

Read this first, and the book set in the Chamber can be read on its own. It says who the legislators are, what carries word between them and what the notes say. The Chamber itself, where Lamport set their parliament, comes last.

The Part-Time Legislators

The legislators of the Paxon Parliament are part-time. They wander in and out of the Chamber whenever they like, and any of them may be away for any length of time. Nobody lies, and nobody is in charge for long.

Each legislator keeps a scroll in indelible ink, which goes with them when they leave and comes back with them. In the front they write the decrees that have passed, in order; in the back, the ballots they have voted in and the promises they have made. A legislator who returns after any absence finds their scroll as they left it, and acts on nothing but what is written there and what messengers bring them.

The one thing the Parliament guarantees is that no two scrolls ever record different decrees under the same number, because any two majorities of legislators share a member.

Messengers

Legislators speak to one another only by messenger. A messenger carries a note from one legislator to another, and what they carry is exactly what was written. Beyond that, nothing is promised. Messengers take as long as they take; a messenger may forget and deliver the same note twice; and a messenger may leave on a long voyage and never deliver their note at all.

Nothing else carries word. The Chamber's acoustics are so poor that oratory is impossible: nobody can be heard across it, so nobody speaks to the room, and a legislator who is away hears nothing until a messenger finds them.

What the Notes Say

A note never carries a vote of the whole room, because the room never gathers. It carries a ballot's number and what one legislator says about that ballot. Each legislator owns their own numbers, so no two ballots ever share one.

The legislator who starts a ballot does not always choose its decree. If anyone in their majority has voted before, they must propose the decree of the highest-numbered vote they report; only if none has may they choose freely.

Lamport names five notes, and a decree passes when they have gone round in this order. Any step may simply stop, when a legislator walks out or a messenger is lost, and an unfinished ballot can never make two scrolls disagree. A note delivered twice is ignored the second time.

The Chamber

Paxos is shaped like a Y. Two arms reach north and face each other across a bay: the western arm holds the Chamber, the eastern arm the city of Schedia and its Tholos. At the fork stands the oracle, and below it the stem widens into the scholars' coast. The island road runs up the stem, forks, and runs out along both arms. Paxos can read the sun, and the Chamber has a meridian line.

The Chamber is Lamport's, not ours, and it is kept as they wrote it. It stands on the western arm, on the road to the harbour town at the arm's tip, so legislators pass it as they come and go. It is drawn as a rotunda because Lamport gives it one physical property, its poor acoustics. A round hall with hard stone walls under one conical roof held up by columns inside, with no podium and no head of the room, fits that description, and its one doorway, closed by a door that can be locked, lets legislators and messengers come and go.

The Parliament makes the law of Paxos: the western arm, the stem and the scholars' coast. Schedia, on the eastern arm, keeps its own laws, and the Parliament's decrees have never run there.

The Parliament Is Still Sitting

Lamport ends their paper with the Parliament's fall: a scribe's error names drowned sailors as the only legislators, government halts, and a coup and an invasion follow. Every other book in this series sets that ending aside, as a matter of convenience. In them the Parliament is still sitting, the Chamber still stands on the western arm, and the scholars of the island's coast still walk up to it.

Reality Checks

What each part of this place has in common with the real Greek world, so that a reader can tell what is borrowed from what is made up. Close means the real thing is much like it; loose means it is borrowed and changed; invented means the setting made it up; anachronism means the real thing is of another age. A dagger (†) marks a citation given from memory and not checked against the text.
