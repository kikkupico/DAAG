Distributed Algorithms of Ancient Greece · Paxos · The Scholars' Coast

Changes That Never Clash

Based on “Conflict-free Replicated Data Types” and “A Comprehensive Study of Convergent and Commutative Replicated Data Types” by Marc Shapiro, Nuno Preguiça, Carlos Baquero and Marek Zawirski (2011)

Can some changes be combined in any order, any number of times, and still come out the same everywhere, so that they never clash and no one ever needs to coordinate?

On the far terraces at the island's southern tip, every terrace keeps a table of pebbles that counts the copies made of each work, and its scribe adds to his own column only. Terraces send each other their tables, and each takes the larger pile in every column. However the tables cross, however often, and in whatever order, every terrace ends with the same count. The book finds why that works, what else can be built the same way, and what cannot.

Setting The Scholars' Coast

In a Nutshell

Every far terrace keeps a table of pebbles, one column per terrace, counting the copies made of each work, and a scribe adds a pebble only to his own terrace's column. Two tables are merged by taking the larger pile in each column. That merge can be done in any order and any number of times with the same result, so terraces that have heard the same news show the same count, with no waiting and no vote. The book follows the idea to counters that go down, to corrections that meet, and to lists of titles with strikes and re-additions, finds where it holds and where it cannot. In real terms, this covers conflict-free replicated data types: strong eventual consistency, state-based and operation-based CRDTs, counters, registers and sets.

Chapter I

The Count No One Settles

At the island's southern tip, as far from the Chamber and the Tholos as Paxos goes, a hillside has been cut into terraces, and on each terrace a scribe keeps a table*. The table counts how many copies of each work have been made on the coast, in pebbles set in grooves on a stone slab. The scribe of a terrace adds a white pebble whenever a copy is made at the library nearest to him. The terraces send one another their tables, by courier, and each terrace must in the end show the same count as every other. The scribe of the first terrace is Shapirios.

What would a count on each terrace be, to start with? A pile of pebbles, one pile for each work. A scribe adds one to his pile for each copy made. Now two terraces each add one pebble to the same work, at the same time, each unaware of the other. Each table shows one copy made; two were made. What should the terraces do when the tables meet? If a terrace keeps the larger pile, both piles are one and the meeting keeps one; a copy is lost. If it adds the two piles together, it gets two, which is right this once, but at the next meeting the same two piles are added again, and again at every meeting after, so the count rises for ever on the strength of copies that were made once.

Could the terraces take turns? That is the usual cure: no scribe adds a pebble until he has settled with the others whose turn it is, and the first to ask goes first. But that needs terraces to wait for one another, and a stopped courier would stop them all. Nothing on this coast waits for a vote, and the count of copies is not worth stopping for. What is wanted is narrower than agreement. A scribe should be able to add a pebble the moment a copy is made, without waiting for anyone. Any terraces that have heard of the same copies should show the same count, whatever order the news reached them in. And nothing should ever have to be taken back.

Is there a way of keeping the count so that the tables never clash, however the news crosses and however often it repeats?

Chapter II

One Column Each

The trouble in the first attempt was that two scribes added to the same pile. What if no two scribes ever did? Each table is kept with a column for every terrace, and a scribe adds white pebbles only to the column of his own terrace. Terrace 2's scribe never touches the column of Shapirios's terrace on any table, and the other way about. The count of copies made is the sum of all the columns.

Why does that mend it? Because a column has one writer. Whatever terrace 1's scribe has put in his column is the only change that column will ever see, and it is a change that only goes up. When two scribes each add a pebble, they add them to different columns, and neither addition can overwrite or double the other. On any table, a column is simply the number of copies its terrace has made so far, as far as that table has heard.

How does one terrace learn another's pile? By being sent its table, and that raises the question of what to do with a table that has arrived. A terrace holds its own table, with its own column up to date and the others as stale as the news that has reached it. The table that arrives has its sender's column up to date and the rest stale in a different way. Which pile in a column is right?

When two tables meet, which pile in each column should the terrace keep?

Chapter III

Take the Larger

A terrace keeps the larger pile in each column. A column only ever grows, because the scribe who owns it adds and never removes. So of two piles in the same column, the larger is simply the later, and holds everything the smaller holds and more. A terrace that takes the larger pile in each column loses nothing it has heard and gains whatever the other table knew that it did not.

What is then true of merging? Three things, and the whole idea rests on them. It does not matter which table is merged into which, since taking the larger of two numbers is the same either way round. It does not matter how several tables are grouped, since the larger of three numbers is the same however the comparisons are paired. And merging the same table twice changes nothing, since a table merged into one that already includes it leaves every pile as it was. A table can therefore be lost, delayed, copied, or arrive out of turn, and the terrace that eventually takes it in ends at the same place as if it had come once and on time.

Why is adding the totals wrong, then? Because a total is a sum of piles, and two tables each hold part of the same piles. Adding them counts the shared part twice, and again at every later merge. Taking the larger in each column counts each pile once, since it never combines two piles of the same column.

Does this ever need the terraces to agree on anything? No. Take any terraces that can still pass tables to one another, even if the roads to the rest are cut: they come to show the same count among themselves, and when the roads open again the rest join them. Any number of terraces can fall silent without stopping the others. The tables settle by merging and by nothing else.

Proof — that the state-based counter is a CvRDT, and that every pair of replicas converges
  1. This is Specification 6 of the comprehensive report (the G-Counter). The payload is a vector P of n naturals, one entry per replica. The update increment at replica g sets P[g] := P[g] + 1; the query value returns Σi P[i]; the merge of X and Y is Z with Z.P[i] = max(X.P[i], Y.P[i]) for each i.
  2. The order is X ≤ Y ⇔ ∀i, X.P[i] ≤ Y.P[i]. Componentwise maximum is the least upper bound of two vectors under this order, since it is an upper bound of both and any other upper bound is at least as large in every entry. So the states form a semilattice, and merge is its join.
  3. Each update increases one entry and leaves the rest, so the state never decreases: s ≤ s • increment. The object is a monotonic semilattice, hence a CvRDT (Theorem 1 of the short paper).
  4. Now let xi and xj be any two replicas that can exchange states infinitely often by some channel that eventually delivers (Proposition 2.1 of the report). Let xi′ = xi ⊔ xj and xj′ = xj ⊔ xi. By the commutativity of ⊔, xi′ = xj′, and by idempotence and associativity further merges of the same states change nothing. Both replicas have the same causal history, C(xi) ∪ C(xj), and the same state.
  5. The report states two assumptions of this vector form: the payload does not overflow, and the set of replicas is known. A grow-only set of unique marks, one per increment, can serve as the same counter without knowing the replicas.∎

What does a terrace send, in practice, a whole table or only what is new?

Must a terrace send its whole table every time, or could it send just the change?

Chapter IV

Sending the Table or the Change

A terrace can send the other terraces its whole table, as the count of copies was kept, or it can send a short notice of each change, one more copy at terrace 1. The first is the table way and the second the notice way. Both can keep every terrace's count the same, and they ask very different things of the couriers.

What does the table way ask? Almost nothing. A table may arrive twice, late, or before an older one, and merging by the larger pile makes no difference, as merging by the larger pile showed. A courier may be lost on the road and no one need know, provided some later table gets through. The cost is size: a terrace that keeps tables for many works sends them all, to carry a handful of changes. Sending only the piles that differ is possible, but it needs a terrace to know what the other holds, and that is the notice way's trouble back again.

What does the notice way ask? A great deal. A notice that is taken in twice counts twice, because nothing in one more copy says which copy, and a notice lost leaves a pile short for good. So each notice must arrive exactly once. And notices that depend on one another must arrive in the order of their dependence: a strike of a title must not arrive before the addition it strikes. Such delivery needs no vote and no leader, and a courier can be asked to carry notices that way, but it is a promise about the roads that the table way does not need. What the notice way buys is that a notice is small.

Are the two ways really different in what they can keep? No: each can do what the other does. A terrace can send a whole table as if it were a notice, take this table in, since merging commutes and so may be done in any order and any number of times. And a terrace can keep, as part of its table, the set of every notice it has received and every one it has acted on, so that a table carries the notices and merging two tables merges those sets. In both directions the one is a disguise of the other; the paper proves it. So a count that can be kept either way can be kept the other, and the choice is about the roads and the cost.

Proof — that every SEC state-based object can be emulated by an op-based one, and conversely
  1. This is Theorems 3 and 4 of the short paper (§3.2).
  2. State-based by op-based. Given a CvRDT (S, ≤, s0, q, u, m), define a CmRDT with the same state and query. Its prepare-update t(a) records s′ = s • u(a) and its effect-update u′(s′) is s • m(s′), delivered to every replica with no precondition. Merge always commutes, so these effect-updates commute, and with reliable delivery the object is a CmRDT.
  3. Op-based by state-based. Given a CmRDT (S, s0, q, t, u, P), take the state (sm, M, D), where sm is the CmRDT's state and M, D are add-only sets of the updates known and the updates delivered. Order (sm, M, D) ≤ (sm′, M′, D′) by M ⊆ M′ ∧ D ⊆ D′.
  4. A local update runs t and u and adds the update to M. A recursive function d delivers any known update in M \ D whose precondition P holds, adds it to D and applies it to sm, until none is enabled. The merge takes the union of the M sets, then runs d.
  5. Every update is delivered exactly once to the embedded object, in an order satisfying P, and the triple forms a monotonic semilattice (the merge is a union, hence a least upper bound). So the emulation is a CvRDT, and it is SEC because the embedded CmRDT is.∎

So counts that only go up are settled. Is that all that counting means? A count of copies made only rises, but a count of copies on the shelves must fall when a copy is lent out and lost.

Can a count be made to go down without breaking the one thing that made merging safe, that every pile only grows?

Chapter V

Made and Lost

Taking the larger pile works because a pile never shrinks. A scribe who removed a pebble from his own column would make the pile smaller, and a table that still had the old, larger pile would put it back at the next merge: the removal would have no effect at all. So a count cannot go down by removing pebbles.

What can be done instead? Keep a second kind of pebble. Black pebbles stand for copies lost, white for copies made, and each terrace has two sets of columns, a white set and a black set. A scribe adds a white pebble to his own white column for a copy made, and a black pebble to his own black column for a copy lost. Each set merges by the larger pile in every column. The count of copies there are is the white pebbles, less the black. Both sets only grow, so both merge safely, and their difference can fall.

So may the count go as low as it likes? No, and this is the first thing the idea cannot do. Suppose the count of copies of a work is zero or more, and the coast wants it to stay so. Take a work with exactly one copy left. Two terraces' tables each show one copy; each scribe, at the same moment and unaware of the other, marks it lost, because a scribe may not mark a copy lost if his table shows none. Then the tables meet: two black pebbles, one white, and the count is minus one. Each scribe followed the rule his own table gave him, and the rule was not enough.

Is there a rule each scribe can keep alone that prevents it? A stronger one works: a terrace may mark no more copies lost than were made at its own terrace. Then the count can never fall below zero, because every black pebble is matched by a white one in the same column. But that is much stricter than intended: a terrace whose library never made a copy could mark no copy lost, however many it held. The other cures are to agree before marking a copy lost, which is to wait, or to reserve beforehand how many losses each terrace may mark, which is agreeing in advance. The paper's verdict is that there is no cheap way: a limit on the whole coast's count cannot be seen from any one terrace's table, so keeping it means agreeing.

If a count has to be given up for a limit, what of things that are not counts: a correction to the text of a work?

Chapter VI

Two Hands on One Work

What does a table of corrections need? A work's text, with the tally of the edition that wrote it. When two libraries correct the same work in ignorance of each other, there are two editions and the terraces must end with one result. The first rule is the plain one: the edition with the larger tally wins, and the other is thrown away*. Merging two tables takes the larger tally for each work. That is the same trick as the larger pile, applied to a number that only rises, and it settles every pair of corrections the same way at every terrace.

Is the loser lost for good? Yes, and that is a cost, not a flaw in the rule. The two libraries wrote in ignorance of each other and the terraces chose between them by the tally alone, which is the same at every terrace and so agrees. Nothing says the later edition was the better, only that it is later. And it needs the tally to follow the order of events: an edition written by someone who had seen another must carry a larger tally than the one it answers, or the rule would throw away the answer and keep the question.

Is there another rule? Yes: keep both. Each edition is kept with a note of which editions it was written after, and an edition is dropped only when some kept edition was written after it. Two corrections written in ignorance of each other are then both kept, and a reader who asks is shown both. Every terrace shows the same two, since the rule depends on what the editions were written after and not on which arrived first. The choice between them is left to the reader, not made by the terraces.

Which should the coast use? Neither is forced by the idea. Both merge in any order and any number of times, both leave every terrace with the same result, and either may be the right one for a given work. A paper on this subject lists a number of such choices for one data type, each a different meaning for the same clash, and all of them settle. What the idea guarantees is that every terrace settles the same way; it does not say the way is the right one.

If a correction can be kept or thrown away without anyone deciding, what of a list of titles, where things are added and struck out?

Chapter VII

Titles Added and Struck

The terraces also keep a list of titles of the works made on the coast. The simplest list is one that is only ever added to: a scribe writes a title on it and no one ever strikes one off. Two lists merge by joining the titles, and since joining is the same either way round, in any grouping, and twice over, they never clash. This is the catalogue of titles. It is the plainest of the lists, and the others are built from it.

What if a title has to be struck off? A first way is to keep a second list beside the first, the struck-off list, and to say that a title is on the catalogue if it is on the first list and not on the second. Both lists only grow, so both merge. The cost is that a title once struck can never come back: writing it on the first list again changes nothing, since it is on the second. A scribe may strike only a title he has seen on the first list, and a strike that reaches a terrace before the add it strikes is still held against it. This is the cheap way, and the rule is fixed: a strike beats an add.

Is there a way that lets a title be added again? There is, and it is the one that behaves most like an ordinary list. Every time a title is written on the catalogue, it is written with a mark that no one else will ever use, and the mark is never shown. A title is on the catalogue if any mark of it is. To strike a title, a scribe strikes all the marks he can see on it, and only those. Now what happens when one library strikes a title and, at the same time, another writes it again? The second library's mark is one the first never saw, so the strike does not touch it, and the title stays. A title added after a strike is on the catalogue; an add and a strike made in ignorance of each other leave the add standing. The paper names it the observed-remove set: a strike removes what it has observed.

Are there other ways? Several. Each title can carry a tally, and the later of its latest add and its latest strike wins, which makes the outcome depend on how tallies are given. Each title can carry a count, going up with adds and down with strikes, which converges but behaves oddly: a title struck twice has a count of minus one, and an add then has no effect, so that after adding a title to an empty list it stays empty. All of them settle, and each gives a different meaning to the same clash. No one of them is the list; they are different lists that happen to be safe to merge.

These lists settle without any one order of the adds and strikes. Is that a weakness, or something different in kind?

Chapter VIII

Not the Same as One Order

What would it mean for the terraces to keep a list as an ordinary list is kept, with every act in one order? That, after every addition and every strike, the list is what it would be if the acts had been done one after another in some order that every terrace shares. A list kept like that is simple to reason about, and it asks the terraces to settle that order between them, which is what needs everyone to wait.

Do lists of titles ever give a result that no single order could? They do. Take the list in which an add beats a concurrent strike. Library 1 adds title E and then strikes title F, while, at the same time, Library 2 adds title F and then strikes title E. A terrace takes in both. Library 1's strike of F removed only the marks of F it had seen, and it had seen none, since Library 2's add was concurrent; likewise Library 2's strike of E. So the list has both E and F on it. In any single order of the four acts, one of the two strikes is last, and the title it strikes would be off the list. No order gives both. The terraces have converged to a list that no one-at-a-time history could have produced.

Does the other way hold, that one order gives a result the terraces cannot? Not when nobody fails. If the terraces did settle one order, they would all end in the same list, so they would converge too. But settling one order, in the worst case, needs the terraces to agree, and agreement cannot be had if many of them may fall silent. The idea here does not ask for agreement: a terrace is always free to read and to add, whatever the roads, and it tolerates all but one of the terraces being down at once. So the two kinds of guarantee are not comparable: each allows results the other does not.

Proof — that SEC and sequential consistency are incomparable
  1. This is §3.3 of the short paper. SEC does not imply sequential consistency. Take a Set CRDT with add-wins semantics: after add(e) ∥ remove(e), e ∈ S. Let replica p0 execute add(e); remove(e′), and concurrently replica p1 execute add(e′); remove(e), and let p3 merge both.
  2. Each remove acts on the add it observed at its own replica, and concurrent with the other replica's add, so by the add-wins specification the final state at p3 satisfies e ∈ S ∧ e′ ∈ S.
  3. In any sequentially consistent execution of the four updates in a single order, either remove(e) or remove(e′) is last, and that element is not in S. So the final state above occurs in no sequentially consistent execution.
  4. Sequential consistency does not imply SEC. In the absence of crashes a sequentially consistent object is SEC, since there is a single order after which all replicas terminate in the same state. In general, however, sequential consistency requires consensus, which cannot be solved in the presence of n − 1 crashes, whereas a SEC object is available and converges with up to n − 1 crashes. Therefore the two are incomparable.∎

So what do the terraces give up, and what do they get? They get a count and a catalogue that are always available to read and write, that never wait on a courier, and that settle by merging alone. They give up two things, set out below.

What is it that this idea cannot give, however it is arranged?

Chapter IX

What It Does Not Say

Does it keep a limit? No. A count that must never go below zero, or a catalogue that must never hold two titles together, is a rule about the whole coast, and no terrace can see the whole coast from its own table. The paper shows the failure with the count and says the only remedy is to agree, before or sometimes. The idea keeps what each terrace can see from its own table; it keeps nothing that depends on every terrace's.

Does it say which of the clashing results is the right one? No. Every terrace settles the same way, and the way is chosen by whoever designs the list: the strike may beat the add, or the add the strike; the later correction may beat the earlier, or both may be kept. Terraces agree on the result and not on whether it is what anyone wanted. A paper that studies many of these choices is the first to say that any of them may be reasonable for some use.

Does it keep the tables small? Not by itself. The struck-off list only grows, each mark of each title stays on the list until it is struck, and a column is kept for every terrace. To throw away old notices, a terrace must know that every other has taken them in, and that is a thing no terrace can know without hearing from every other. A method exists, and it works only while no terrace is silent; the paper accepts that, because it affects how much is kept and not whether the terraces agree.

What does it assume? That no terrace lies: a scribe who adds pebbles to another's column, or marks a title with a mark someone else has used, can break it. That, in the vector form of the count, the set of terraces is known and the piles are never so large they run out of room. That, for the notice way, notices are delivered exactly once, in the order of their dependence. And that for the corrections, tallies follow the order of events. These are not a flaw; they are what the guarantee is built on.

Is the list of examples complete? No. The paper gives a general conflict-free design for the more difficult structure of cross-references between works, with arcs added and removed as the references are found, and separate designs for ordered sequences; they are left to the paper. What it has shown is the shape of the idea, which is not a list of types: the merge must be a join, and the changes must only go up, or else they must commute.

The ideas, plainly

Shapiro and his co-authors ask when replicas of some data can accept changes without ever talking to one another first, and still end the same. Their answer, strong eventual consistency, asks only that replicas that have received the same updates are in the same state. They give two sufficient conditions. In the state-based form the states are ordered so that each update moves a state upward and merging two states is their least upper bound; then merging commutes, groups freely and may be repeated. In the operation-based form every pair of concurrent operations commutes and operations are delivered once each, in causal order. Each form can imitate the other.

A counter that only counts up is a vector with one entry per replica and a per-entry maximum for merge; a counter that also counts down is two of them. A register can keep the later write or keep both. A set that only grows is a union; a set that also loses elements can let removal win, or let adds win by tagging every add and having a removal take away only the tags it has seen. These all converge, and each means something different by a clash.

What the approach does not do is keep a rule that spans all the replicas, such as a count that never goes below zero; for that, replicas must agree. It does not decide which meaning of a clash is right, and it is not the same as keeping one order of all the updates: it permits results no single order would give, and it is available where one order would need agreement.
