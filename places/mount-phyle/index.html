Mount Phyle

A siege that never ends: bandits on the crown with their loot, four mercenaries at the foot, and word between them only by raven.

Read this first, and any book set on the mountain can be read next, in any order. It describes the hill, the four camps below, the crown above and the ravens, which every book here takes for granted, and it says what each book changes.

I

The Hill

Mount Phyle is the limestone massif at the middle of Arche, four hundred metres high. Eight wooded spurs run down from its top, with gullies between them, and the hill's own crows roost in the pines of the folds. On the very top is the crown: a jumble of crags and clefts inside a ruined ring wall, which has two ways through, a narrow slot on the north side and a wider timber-framed gap on the south.

The hill is under siege. Bandits hold the crown with their loot. Four mercenaries have been hired to clear them out and are camped at the foot. The siege has no end in any book: no assault is made, no wall is carried, and nobody surrenders. The books describe arrangements, not events, and nothing follows from any decision anyone reaches.

II

The Camps

Four small tents stand at the foot of four gullies, at the four quarters of the hill, one mercenary to a tent. The tents are numbered 1 to 4, and each mercenary goes by his tent's number: Number 1 to Number 4. They are alike. Each was hired separately and is paid separately; none commands another, and none of them lies.

Between any two tents stand two wooded spurs, so no man can see another. They light no fires and sound no horns, which would tell the hill where they are and that they are talking. And no man leaves his quarter to carry word, because leaving it is the very thing they must agree on.

Their question has two answers: which gate, north or south. All four must go in at the same one, since two at each is no assault. Going is a definite act. A man goes by striking his tent and taking the path round the slope to his gate, and once he has done it his quarter is open behind him and he will not be back before dawn. What he thinks before that does not count.

Any of them may be killed. A dead man's tent simply falls silent. Nobody succeeds him and no bird goes out to report it, so to the others a dead man looks exactly like a live one whose bird has not yet come.

III

The Crown

On the crown, Number 1, the chief, sits on the peak with the loot. Numbers 2, 3 and 4 hold three posts, each tucked into its own crag below the rim and watching one approach. No post can see another or the peak. Nobody shouts, raises a banner or lights a brand: the tents below are listening, and a voice that every post heard at once would leave a lying chief no way to tell different men different things, and the crown's question would vanish.

The bandits never die in these books, but some of them may lie, and nobody below or above ever learns which. Each carries a seal ring cut with his own number, and an honest man's seal cannot be counterfeited. Their question, too, has two answers: hold the hill and keep the loot, or scatter and abandon it, all doing the same thing. On later nights they keep a book of the loot instead, one copy at each post.

The chief is a job as well as a man. In the crown's arrangements whoever's word is going round is "the chief" for that round, and the job passes from man to man. Number 1 holds it first.

IV

Ravens

Word passes on the mountain only by raven. A man writes on a strip, rolls it into a bronze tube, ties it to the leg of a bird belonging to the tent or post he is writing to, and lets it go. What a bird carries is exactly what was written, and it is known whose it is. The camps carry nothing from one house of the ring road to another.

A goatherd crossing the mountain by the smugglers' tracks may pass between the tents, but he takes as long as he takes and may never come down at all. A strip given to him would only be one more slow bird, less sure than a raven, and nothing on the hill either needs him or is changed by him.

How long a bird takes is where the books differ. Below the crown, on the nights of the first book, a bird may sit among the crows of the folds for an hour or for the night: there is no longest flight. Above, the hops between the posts are short and clear, and no bird has ever taken longer than a sandglass takes to run. Each post has a sandglass; the four were turned together once, at dusk on the first night before the posts were manned, and since no man leaves his place they are never turned together again. Sandglasses are the only timers on the hill.

Where the allegory leaks

Real ravens do not carry messages home; homing doves do, and a homing bird flies one way only, to its own loft. So each man really does need birds belonging to the tent or post he writes to, and his stock of them is limited. This makes the men's lot harder than the model's, which allows as many messages as a protocol cares to send. It does not help them, and nothing in any book turns on it. The birds here are called ravens and fly as many times as they are needed.

V

One Change a Night

Every book on the mountain keeps the same hill, the same four at the camps and the same four on the crown. What changes from book to book is one condition of the night, never the place.

At the camps.
The first nights: birds with no longest flight, and one man may die. Nothing else.
Windy nights: while a wind blows, a bird may sit it out for any time; the wind always drops in the end, and in still air every bird arrives within a glass. On these nights each tent has a glass, turned as the last light left the crown.
Nights of lost birds: no wind, and a bird that arrives does so within a known flight, but a hawk may take any bird. The four keep a standing gate that any of them may move or ask after.
Nights of the coin: each man carries Attic silver and, when he cannot settle, tosses a coin alone in his tent.
Nights of the slate: each tent keeps a slate, chalking the tents its man believes dead, often wrongly.

On the crown.
The first night: no wind, short bounded flights, glasses turned together; some men lie.
Later nights: the same wind blows over the whole hill; the four keep the loot book, and the job of numbering its entries passes from man to man.

Whatever the night, nothing is ever settled by force, nobody is ever found out, and the arrangements are judged only by what they guarantee.
