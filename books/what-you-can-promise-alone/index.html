What You Can Promise Alone

When a machine cannot stop to check with the others on every change, what can it still promise on its own? That no one sees a reply before the message it answers? That no one sees a change half made? That two writers who each read a value and write it one higher never lose one of the two?

We find out by exploring what the libraries of the scholars' coast could still promise the readers of their compendium on days when the couriers between them stopped running.

We find out by asking what a replica can still promise its clients when it must always answer: which guarantees it can keep on its own while the network between replicas is partitioned, and which it cannot.

After Peter Bailis, Aaron Davidson, Alan Fekete, Ali Ghodsi, Joseph M. Hellerstein and Ion Stoica, Highly Available Transactions: Virtues and Limitations, 2014

A library, a reader, and no courierA replica, a client, and no network

Every library on the scholars' coast has a reading room where readers consult the entries of its copy of the compendium, and where scholars correct them. The standard

The couriers stopThe network stops

A library makes a promise alone (high availability) when every reader who can reach an open library has their business done, whichever couriers are stopped and however long they stay stopped.What does it mean for a replica to make a promise alone (high availability)? It means that every client that can reach a running replica gets its transaction completed, no matter which messages are lost or how long the partition lasts.

A library that has to wait for a majority of the coast, as the Chamber and the Tholos do, cannot answer when cut off. Nor does it count to say I am probably right, as the coast's odds do.* The question here is which hard promises hold even on the day every courier on the road is stopped.A replica that has to wait for a majority (as consensus protocols do) cannot answer when cut off. Nor does it count to say I am probably right, as with probabilistic staleness.* Here we ask which hard, deterministic promises hold even when every message is lost.

Replying your request is refused does not count as answering either: if refusing every visitor counted, a locked door would be available. Under transactional availability, every visit that reaches an open library must truly complete (commit), unless the reader chooses to cancel it.Furthermore, simply replying I abort your request does not count as availability! If refusing every client counted as answering, a replica that rejected every request would be “available.” Under transactional availability, every transaction that reaches a running replica must genuinely complete (commit), unless the client itself chooses to cancel it.

The most basic promise

No half-made correctionsNo half-made writes

Suppose a scholar sits in a reading room correcting three entries in one visit. While the second correction is still being written, and before the scholar has decided whether to finish the visit or tear up the notes, another reader comes in and asks for the first entry.Suppose a client is revising three items during a single transaction. While it is still writing the second item, and before it has decided whether to commit or abort, another client asks for the first item.

The library must never show a reader an unfinished or abandoned correction (dirty read). Keeping that promise (Read Committed) needs no help from other libraries: the library keeps a visitor's corrections aside on a desk until the visit is declared complete, and only then puts them into its copy, or throws them away if the visitor withdraws.The replica must never show another client an unfinished or abandoned write (dirty read). Keeping that promise (Read Committed) requires zero help from other replicas: the replica simply holds a transaction's writes aside until the client declares the transaction complete, and only then exposes them (or discards them if the client aborts).

A second, smaller promise

Every correction in a visit carries the same tallyEvery write in a transaction carries the same timestamp

Two scholars at two cut-off libraries may each correct entries X and Y in one visit. If one scholar's correction stood on X and the other's on Y, the copies would end with a mixture neither intended (dirty write, G0). Every correction in a visit carries one shared tally, the visit's hour and the visitor's number, and the larger tally stands,* so whichever visit has the larger tally stands on both X and Y.What if two clients at two cut-off replicas both write items X and Y in a single transaction? If one transaction's write won on X while the other's won on Y, the data would end up with a mixture of the two (dirty write / G0) that neither client intended. By stamping every write in a transaction with a single shared timestamp (the transaction's timestamp plus the client's ID) and resolving clashes by largest timestamp,* whichever transaction has the larger timestamp wins on both X and Y.

Proof · that Read Uncommitted and Read Committed are achievable with high availability

High availability: every client that can reach a non-failing replica gets a response, without that replica contacting any other. Read Uncommitted (Adya's PL-1) forbids G0: a cycle in the graph with an edge from Ti to Tj when Tj overwrites a write of Ti. Read Committed (PL-2) also forbids reading aborted or intermediate writes (G1a–c). Claim. Both can be provided with high availability.

  1. This is §5.1.1. Read Uncommitted (Adya's PL-1) requires that writes to each object are totally ordered and that the order is consistent across objects: the graph of transactions with an edge from Ti to Tj when Tj overwrites Ti's write must have no cycle (no G0). Mark every write of a transaction with one timestamp unique across transactions (a client's identifier combined with a sequence number) and let each replica apply last-writer-wins by timestamp.
  2. Then for any two transactions T1 and T2 that write to the same two items x and y, all of T1's writes carry the same timestamp, and so does all of T2's; whichever has the larger timestamp wins on both. It cannot happen that wx(1) precedes wx(2) while wy(2) precedes wy(1). No replica contacts any other to decide, so every replica can respond.
  3. Read Committed (PL-2) adds that no transaction reads uncommitted or intermediate versions (Adya's G1a–c). A client buffers its writes until it commits, or sends them to a server that does not reveal them to other readers until it is notified of the commit. Then a reader is never shown a write of a transaction that later aborts, nor an intermediate write.
  4. This provides no recency or monotonicity, and the paper notes it satisfies the implementation-agnostic definition rather than the lock-based behaviour of centralised implementations.∎
All or none

Corrections bound togetherWrites bound together

Suppose a scholar adds two things in one visit: a new entry X, on the comet's path, and in the entry Y on the comet a cross-reference to X. The courier carrying X may reach a library before the courier carrying Y, or the other way round.Suppose a client writes two items in one transaction: it updates item X (a map) and also item Y (a guide that refers to X). When messages carry those two writes across the system, the message carrying X might reach a replica before the message carrying Y.

A reader who saw the new Y but not yet X would follow a reference to an entry that is not there (fractured read). To prevent that (Monotonic Atomic View), the scholar binds the corrections of a visit under one cord: each carries the visit's tally and a list of every entry corrected in the visit. A library does not show an arriving correction at once; it keeps it in the back room until it knows that every correction under the cord has reached all the libraries (pending stable), and until then it shows readers the older versions.If a client saw the new X and then read the old Y, it would see a broken half-state (fractured read). To prevent that (Monotonic Atomic View), the client tags the writes of a transaction with one manifest: each write carries the transaction's timestamp and the list of all items written in it. A replica does not expose an incoming write the moment it arrives; it holds the write aside until it knows that every write of that transaction has reached all the replicas (pending stable), and until then it continues to return the older versions!

Holding back is not waiting

A library never makes a reader wait; it only delays showing new arrivalsThe replica never makes a client wait; it only delays exposing new arrivals

Ttheir is what keeps the promise available: when a reader asks for X or Y while the couriers are stopped, the library answers at once from the older versions, which are complete in its copy. Once every library has the whole cord, the new versions may be shown, and once a reader has seen any entry from a cord, the reader's note of the visit records the cord's tally, so any other library asked in the same visit shows at least that cord's versions.Notice the trick that makes this highly available: when a client reads X or Y during the partition, the replica answers immediately using the older, pre-transaction version that is already complete. Once every replica has received all the writes of the transaction, the new versions become eligible to show; and once a client sees any item from a transaction, its session records that transaction's timestamp so any other replica it asks in the same transaction also shows it at least that version.

Sketch · that Monotonic Atomic View is achievable with high availability

Monotonic Atomic View (MAV): once some effect of a transaction Ti is observed by a transaction Tj, all later reads of Tj observe all of Ti's effects, or later versions. A write is pending stable once every replica for the transaction's final writes has received them. Claim. MAV can be provided with high availability.

  1. This is the algorithm sketched in §5.1.2. MAV requires that once some effect of a transaction Ti is observed by Tj, all later reads of Tj observe all of Ti's effects (or later versions): if Tj reads a version written by Ti, a later read cannot return an earlier version of an item Ti wrote.
  2. Begin with the Read Committed algorithm. Replicas withhold new writes from readers until all replicas for the final writes of the transaction have received them (the writes are pending stable). Each write carries the transaction's single timestamp and the list of items written.
  3. When a client reads an item, the returned timestamp and item list give a lower bound on the versions the client must read for the other items in the list. The client attaches the current bound to each later request.
  4. A replica responds with a write matching the bound or a pending-stable write with a higher timestamp. Each replica holds, per item, the highest pending-stable write and the set of writes not yet pending stable. Since the bound refers only to writes already pending stable, which every replica holds, a replica can always respond, so the algorithm is master-less and operations never block on replica coordination.
  5. The paper gives the full algorithm in its technical report; this is its outline.∎
Within a visit, and across visits

The same answer twice

A reader who asks for the same entry twice in one visit should see the same version both times (Item Cut Isolation, Repeatable Read).What if a client reads the same item twice during a single transaction and wants to see the exact same version both times (Item Cut Isolation / Repeatable Read)?

That is easy: the reader, or the reading room, notes the version shown the first time and hands back that copy if the reader asks again in the visit, unless the reader corrected it meanwhile. Keeping older versions in the back room, a library can do the same for a whole list of entries on one subject (Predicate Cut Isolation).That is effortless: the client (or its session) simply remembers the version returned by the first read, and if it reads that item again during the same transaction, returns the cached copy (unless the client itself modified it during the transaction). By keeping older versions around, the replica can do the same for a whole set of items matching a condition (Predicate Cut Isolation).

Across visits a reader may go to Library 1 today and Library 2 tomorrow, and never wants to see a version older than one already read (Monotonic Reads). The second shelfThe second group

Staying with one libraryStaying with one replica

Suppose a reader leaves a correction at Library 1, goes out while the couriers are stopped, and comes back the next morning to read the entry (Read Your Writes).Suppose a client writes at replica 1, disconnects during the partition, and reconnects later to read the item (Read Your Writes).

A reader who walks into Library 2, cut off from Library 1, finds that Library 2 has never heard of the correction. It cannot wait for the couriers; it must answer from its own copy, which holds only the old version. So no library can promise a reader their own corrections if the reader moves between libraries that are cut off from each other.If it reads from replica 2, which is cut off from replica 1 by the partition, replica 2 has never received its write! To get Read Your Writes, the reader must stay with one library (sticky availability), coming back to the same door each visit, or carry copies of their own corrections to show any other library, which amounts to the same thing.To get Read Your Writes, the client must stay with one replica (sticky availability), returning to the same replica each time, or else carry copies of its own writes along to show any new replica it visits (which amounts to acting as its own sticky replica).

What is promised

A reader who stays with one library always sees their own correctionsA client that stays with one replica always sees its own writes

When a reader sticks to one library, that library already holds every correction the reader ever wrote, in the exact order they wrote them (PRAM consistency). The only catch is that if that one library burns down or closes its doors, the reader's unpropagated corrections are trapped inside until it reopens.When a client sticks to one replica, that replica already holds every write the client ever made, in the exact order it made them (PRAM consistency). Proof · that Read Your Writes is not achievable with high availability, and is with sticky availability

Read your writes: whenever a client reads an item after updating it, the read returns the updated value or one that overwrote it. Sticky availability: a client always reaches the same replica, and gets a response whenever it can. wx(1) is a write of 1 to item x, and rx(a) a read of x returning a. Claim. Read your writes cannot be provided with high availability, and is provided with sticky availability.

  1. This is §5.1.3. Read your writes requires that whenever a client reads an item after updating it, the read returns the updated value or one that overwrote it. Consider a client that executes T1 : wx(1) and then T2 : rx(a).
  2. Suppose the client executes T1 against a server partitioned from all others. For transactional availability the server must allow T1 to commit.
  3. If the network topology then changes so that the client can execute T2 only on a different replica, partitioned from the one that executed T1, that replica has no way to learn of wx(1). By transactional availability it must respond, so it either stalls indefinitely, violating availability, or returns a value other than 1 or one that overwrote it, violating read your writes.
  4. If the client remains sticky with the server that executed T1, that server holds the write and the scenario is excluded. So read your writes, and with it PRAM (monotonic reads, monotonic writes and read your writes) and causal consistency, require stickiness and are provided by default in a sticky system.∎

The ceiling of what can be promised without waiting

Causal consistency plus atomic views is as strong as a sticky library can goCausal consistency plus atomic views is as strong as a sticky replica can go

The first two shelves together, holding back incomplete cords (MAV), holding back answers until the entries they answer arrive (Writes Follow Reads), and keeping each reader at one library (Read Your Writes, Monotonic Reads and Writes), give transactional Causal Consistency. Proof · that Lost Update and Write Skew cannot be prevented with high availability

rx(v) is a read of item x returning v, and wx(v) a write of v to x. Lost Update: T1 reads an item, T2 updates it, and T1 then writes from its original read. Write Skew: the same across several items bound by a constraint. Transactional availability: a transaction that reaches a replica for each of its items eventually commits, or aborts by its own choice. Claim. Neither anomaly can be prevented with high availability.

  1. This is §5.2.1. Lost Update: T1 reads an item, T2 updates the same item, and T1 writes based on its original read, losing T2's update. Take T1 : rx(100) wx(100 + 20 = 120) and T2 : rx(100) wx(100 + 30 = 130), submitted on opposite sides of a network partition.
  2. By transactional availability both must be allowed to commit. Whichever of 120 and 130 a replica then chooses, the state could not have arisen from a serial execution of T1 and T2, since a serial execution gives 150.
  3. To prevent it, one of the transactions must not commit. Each side's server could try to detect that another write occurred, but this requires knowing the version of the latest write to x, which reduces to a requirement for linearizability, provably at odds with high availability by Gilbert and Lynch's proof of the CAP theorem.
  4. Write Skew generalises Lost Update to several items: T1 : ry(0) wx(1) and T2 : rx(0) wy(1), with a constraint that only one of x and y may be 1. Run on opposite sides of a partition, both commit and the constraint is violated, though each would have preserved it in isolation; any prevention needs the same knowledge of the other side and is likewise unavailable.
  5. Hence Snapshot Isolation, Cursor Stability and consistent read (which require preventing Lost Update), and Repeatable Read and one-copy serializability (which require preventing both) are not achievable with high availability.∎

Proof · that no recency guarantee, including linearizability, is achievable with high availability

A recency guarantee bounds how out of date a read may be: linearizability (a read returns the last completed write), safe and regular register semantics, or any bound in time. High availability: every client that reaches a non-failing replica gets a response. Claim. No recency guarantee can be provided with high availability.

  1. This is §5.2.2. A recency guarantee bounds how out of date a read may be: linearizability (reads return the last completed write), safe and regular register semantics, and any bound such as "no more than five seconds out of date".
  2. Let a write complete at a replica on one side of a partition and let a client then read the item at a replica on the other side. The partition may last indefinitely, with no message crossing.
  3. By availability the reading replica must respond, and it can respond only from information it received before the partition began, which does not include the write. So the response is out of date by at least the time the partition has lasted.
  4. Since the partition can last longer than any fixed bound, no recency bound is enforceable. This is the argument of Gilbert and Lynch for the CAP theorem, applied to every recency guarantee.∎

In the authors' geo-distributed benchmarks across continents, transactions that coordinated through a single master or quorum took 10 to 100 times longer than Highly Available Transactions (HATs) answered locally. Setting Libraries That Agree in the End

The paper behind this book

Peter Bailis, Aaron Davidson, Alan Fekete, Ali Ghodsi, Joseph M. Hellerstein and Ion Stoica, Highly Available Transactions: Virtues and Limitations. Proceedings of the VLDB Endowment, Vol. 7, No. 3 (40th International Conference on Very Large Data Bases, Hangzhou, September 2014), 181–192. — A taxonomy of highly available, sticky available and unavailable isolation and consistency models, with proofs of unavailability and proof-of-concept algorithms.

The ideas, plainly.

Bailis and co-authors ask which of the guarantees that databases and distributed systems offer can be kept by a replica that must always answer, even when it is cut off from every other replica for as long as the cut lasts. They call such systems highly available, and with transactions, highly available transactions. They go through the known guarantees and sort them into those that can be kept, those that can be kept if each client stays with one replica, and those that cannot.

The first group includes read committed, which stops a client reading writes that are not finished, monotonic atomic view, which makes the writes of one transaction visible all together or not at all, repeatable reads within a transaction, and monotonic reads and writes and writes-follow-reads. The second adds read-your-writes, PRAM and causal consistency. Each is built by holding writes back from readers until it is safe to show them, never by making a reader wait.

The third group is made of the guarantees that need a replica to know about concurrent operations elsewhere: preventing lost update and write skew, which rules out snapshot isolation, repeatable read in its other senses and serializability, and any bound on how out of date a read may be. These are fundamental, not weaknesses of particular systems. The paper concludes that many applications can live with the first two groups, that some operations will need the third and should use it sparingly, and that high availability buys the lowest delay.

The paper has six authors, Peter Bailis, Aaron Davidson, Alan Fekete, Ali Ghodsi, Joseph M. Hellerstein and Ion Stoica, and all are credited here. Nobody in this telling is named; the librarians and readers are told apart by what they do.
