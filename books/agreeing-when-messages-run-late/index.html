Agreeing When Messages Run Late

When messages can be delayed without limit, no protocol can guarantee that a group of computers will ever reach agreement. Yet real networks behave well most of the time: periods of chaos eventually give way to calm. Can we design a protocol that never makes a mistake no matter how late messages run, and is guaranteed to reach a decision once messages start arriving on time again—even though no computer can tell when the calm has arrived?

To see how, we return to the four Greek mercenaries camped at the foot of Mount Phyle on a stormy night when high winds delay their ravens for hours, and watch how they manage to agree whether to attack or hold once the wind finally drops.

To see how, we follow the protocol round by round. Four processes face a network that delays messages for a while and then settles—and must stay safe throughout, and decide once it settles.

After Cynthia Dwork, Nancy Lynch and Larry Stockmeyer, Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony, 1988

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Cynthia Dwork, Nancy Lynch and Larry Stockmeyer, Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony. Journal of the ACM 35, 2 (April 1988), 288–323. Introduces the two models of partially synchronous communication and processors, the basic round model and Algorithms 1–3, the lower bounds on fault tolerance (Theorems 4.3 and 4.4), and distributed clock synchronization under partial synchrony.

The ideas. Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson proved that deterministic consensus is impossible in a completely asynchronous system if even a single process can crash, because a crashed process cannot be distinguished from a slow one. Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer asked how much timing synchrony is actually needed to bridge the gap. Their answer is partial synchrony: either a fixed upper bound Δ on message delay exists but is unknown to the processes, or the bound Δ is known in advance but holds only from some unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST) onward.

In both models, consensus becomes possible, and the paper establishes the exact fault-tolerance thresholds: with crash or omission faults, a strict majority of processes must remain correct (N ≥ 2t + 1); with Byzantine faults, more than two-thirds must remain correct (N ≥ 3t + 1), even when messages carry unforgeable cryptographic signatures. Both lower bounds rely on partition arguments: if too many processes can fail, the network can delay messages between two groups until each group is forced to decide on its own.

The protocols organize rounds into four-round phases, rotating phase ownership among the processes in turn. In each phase, the owner proposes a value that enough processes report as acceptable; processes lock that value and send acknowledgments; the owner decides upon receiving enough acknowledgments; and a lock-release round allows newer locks to supersede older ones from incomplete phases. Safety holds regardless of message delays because once a value is decided, the processes holding locks on it prevent any opposite value from gathering the quorum needed for a new proposal. Termination follows once message delays stabilize because the next phase owned by a correct process after GST is guaranteed to complete.

This clean separation—guaranteeing safety under arbitrary network delays while relying on eventual synchrony only for liveness, using rotating proposers and locks carried across phases—became the blueprint for modern consensus protocols from Paxos and Raft to Tendermint and HotStuff.
