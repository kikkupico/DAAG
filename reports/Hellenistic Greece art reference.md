# Hellenistic stone cities reward careful, colourful drawing

For artwork set on Aegean island poleis between 323 and 31 BC, the evidence supports a clear visual grammar. Cities are small self-governing communities under protector kings or leagues. They are built in regular ashlar stone with Ionic and Corinthian fashions, Doric survives on stoas and courts, and roofs are pan-and-cover terracotta. Streets form grids on terraced slopes, men dress in chiton, himation, chlamys or exomis with petasos or kausia, and merchants sail small round-hulled ships under a single brailed sail. Cities are governed by councils and assemblies that record honours for benefactors on stone stelai. The project's canon (unbased Doric columns, pan-and-cover roofs, petasos, exomis and pera for working figures, deliberately bare-headed messengers) is consistent with the sources. Only the bare head is a choice rather than evidence. The most important cautions are Roman intrusions (treadwheel cranes, testudo, artemon sails, stirrups), Classical items (klismos, Chalcidian helmets) and images that are Roman copies or later reconstructions. The full illustrated reference, with schematic diagrams and public-domain images, is the standalone page `hellenistic-reference.html`.

## Doric without bases, tiled roofs and civic stoas define the built world

Hellenistic temples favour Ionic and Corinthian; one overview calls the Doric temple "virtually abandoned" ([Wikipedia, Hellenistic architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_architecture)). Doric columns have **no base** and stand on the stylobate, while Ionic and Corinthian have moulded bases ([Wikipedia, Ancient Greek architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture)). Doric therefore fits stoas and courts better than temples, an inference. Roofs are low gables of **flat tegulae capped by half-round imbrices**, in use since about 700-650 BC ([Wikipedia, Roof tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roof_tiles)). Colour followed white, blue and red schemes ([Wikipedia, Ancient Greek temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_temple)). The civic core is a set of types: two-storey stoas such as Attalos's of 159-138 BC with 42 shops ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoa_of_Attalos)), hillside theatres, near-square council halls and peristyle gymnasia. Private houses face inward on courtyards, with prostas houses at Priene and cistern-equipped peristyle houses at Delos ([Wikipedia, Priene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priene)). Walls carry multi-storey artillery towers ([Wikipedia, Hellenistic fortifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_fortifications)).

## Grids on slopes, mixed with winding old quarters, organise towns

Priene is the model: a compass-aligned grid on a steep slope, a few broad streets crossed by many narrow ones, about 80 blocks, an agora beside the council hall, and theatre, gymnasium and sanctuary on higher terraces ([Wikipedia, Priene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priene)). Delos shows the other pattern, a winding Theatre Quarter with mixed rich and modest houses ([EFA](https://www.efa.gr/delos-la-ville-et-l-habitat-3/?lang=en)). Harbours have moles, towers at the mouth and waterfront shops ([Ancient Ports Antiques](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/a-few-ports/delos/)). The countryside has terraces and tower-farms ([BSA, Island Towers](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-of-the-british-school-at-athens/article/abs/island-towers-the-case-of-thasos/1A51F6EA8964B6F9A4C4E01E9FE4277B)). Measured street and plot figures are in the dimension tables described below.

## Tools, weapons and clothing combine old kits with new machines

Masons kept iron point, flat and tooth chisels; the building crane was a shear-legs derrick with windlass, and the treadwheel is Roman ([Wootton et al.](https://artofmaking.ac.uk/media/uploads/uploads/stoneworking_tools_and_toolmarks-wootton_russell_rockwell-v1.0.pdf); [Vitruvius X](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/10*.html)). Rhodian stamped amphorae, mould-made lamps (from the start of the 3rd century BC) and Megarian bowls are the signature objects ([Getty](https://www.getty.edu/publications/ancientlamps/typological-classification/)). The phalangite carries a **5-7 m sarissa** and a small neck-slung shield ([Wikipedia, Sarissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa)); the thureophoros carries a Celtic-derived oval shield adopted in the 270s BC ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thureophoros)). Torsion artillery appears by 338-326 BC ([Wikipedia, Catapult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catapult)), and the euthytonon/palintonon meaning is debated. Dress is draped rectangles: chiton, himation, chlamys, exomis, with petasos and kausia ([Wikipedia, Ancient Greek clothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_clothing)). Tanagra figurines supply women's costume and preserved paint ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanagra_figurine)), and after Alexander the clean-shaven face became fashionable ([The Collector](https://www.thecollector.com/beards-ancient-greece/)).

## Small ships, mule tracks and stone decrees carry trade and government

The Kyrenia ship, about 15 m, had one square sail brailed through lead rings, a crew of about four and a mostly Rhodian wine cargo ([Wikipedia, Kyrenia ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrenia_ship); [Pomey](https://books.openedition.org/obp/14374)). Sources disagree on its dates, cargo count and ring count, and the Syracusia's tonnage is also contested. Roads were rudimentary tracks and mules did most hauling ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Travel_in_classical_antiquity)). Government ran through council, assembly and honorific decrees inscribed on stone, funded by euergetism ([Wikipedia, Euergetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euergetism)). The Theangela decree for Semos (c. 230-220 BC) shows the chain from vote to envoys ([Attalus](http://www.attalus.org/docs/other/inscr_20.html)). Money follows the Attic reckoning of 6 obols to a drachma, 100 drachmas to a mina and 60 minas to a talent ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_units_of_measurement)). Delos's Apollo lent at 10 percent ([Attalus](https://www.attalus.org/docs/other/inscr_223.html)).

## Dimensions for modelling are tabulated by section and on one cheat sheet

Each topic section of `hellenistic-reference.html` ends with a "Dimensions for modelling" table (item, dimensions, period, certainty, source), and the "Modelling cheat sheet" section collects the most useful numbers with five schematic scale diagrams (human and door, column proportions, vessel size ladder, weapon lengths, Kyrenia against a trireme). Headline figures: men about 1.71 m and women about 1.53 m (low-certainty skeletal means); Doric columns about 7 diameters (Priene Sacred Stoa 7.4 d, 0.70 x 5.20 m), Ionic about 8.5-9 d, Corinthian about 10 d (Olympieion 17.25 x 1.70 m, a Hadrianic completion); Priene streets 4.44 m main and 3.5 m cross (survey), insula 47.2 x 35.4 m, Sacred Stoa 116.46 x 11.81 m with 4.40 x 3.62 m shops; theatre seat height 0.40 m; Kyrenia ship 14.0-14.7 m by 4.4 m; sarissa 5-7 m (model 5.8 m) against a 1.71 m man; Attic tetradrachm about 17.2 g and 24-27 mm.

Conflicts stay visible rather than smoothed. Priene streets (survey 4.44 and 3.5 m against Wikipedia's 6 and 3 m), the sarissa head (a 13.5 cm reconstruction against the 51 cm Vergina find, possibly a butt-spike), the himation (modern ranges only; 1.2-1.5 x 2.5-3 m is the recommended estimate, 4-5 m an upper bound), Rhodian amphora height (96 cm in one record, 77-79 cm in studies), the Kyrenia length and cargo counts, Philo's catapult formula against Vitruvius's ballista table, the helepolis literary heights (125-140 ft) and the inconsistent Delos cistern figures are each flagged. Door height, storey height and kline size have no measured source, so the page gives labelled defaults (a 2.0 x 0.9 m door, 3.5 m storey) and lists what is still unmeasured in every section.

## Conclusion

The value of the reference is in its guardrails as much as its facts. A small set of clearly dated items (thureos after 279 BC, mould lamps from 300 BC, Pergamon Altar after 166 BC) lets artists place a scene in time. Many tempting images are Roman copies, modern reconstructions or later fantasies. The largest gaps are the lack of an island-specific source, decree and ballot procedure for small cities, and public-domain imagery for the Great Altar, Rhodes, ships and artillery.
