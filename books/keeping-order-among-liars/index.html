Keeping Order Among Liars

Can a replicated service keep all of its commands in the exact same order when some of its machines lie — including the leader — and when messages across the network can be delayed for arbitrarily long?

To find out, we follow four bandits on a hilltop who maintain four separate copies of their loot scroll, ensuring that every honest copy records the same entries in the same order even when one of them — their chief included — is a liar.

After Miguel Castro and Barbara Liskov, Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, 1999

First, in machine terms

Many copies, one order

To survive hardware failures or break-ins, a critical service can be run on several computers at once (state machine replication). For the copies to stay in sync, every honest machine must execute the exact same commands in the exact same order — even when some of the machines actively lie.

Order matters because the outcome of a command depends on what ran before it, as the interactive example on the right shows. And here a faulty machine may do far worse than crash: it may send conflicting messages to different peers, and the leader (the primary) responsible for numbering commands may itself be the traitor. Up to f out of 3f + 1 machines may be faulty — which for a group of four machines means one liar. Worse still, messages across the network can take arbitrarily long, so the protocol must never allow two honest copies to disagree on the order (safety), while guaranteeing that commands still go through once network delays stop growing without bound (liveness).

  • Three phases of messages (Castro and Liskov, 1999 — pre-prepare, prepare, and commit) lock in the sequence number of each command so that no two honest copies ever put different commands in the same slot.
  • A view change replaces a silent or lying leader without losing any command that an honest copy has already committed.

The first mechanism keeps the log in order under a single leader; the second carries that order safely across leaders. We tell the story with four bandits on the crown of Mount Phyle, a shared scroll of their loot, and their trained rats.

The crown of Mount Phyle

Four posts, none in sight of another

Number 1, the chief, sits inside the stone ring wall with the strongboxes of loot, while Numbers 2, 3, and 4 keep lookout posts out on the slopes, none within sight or earshot of another.* Any one of the four bandits may be a liar, and with four posts in all, a single liar is the most the honest three can tolerate.

What the four hold

The loot scroll

Because no post can see another, the four bandits keep a loot scroll — one complete copy at each post — recording where every strongbox is hidden and whose share each piece of treasure is.

The scroll grows one entry at a time. An entry is a single update to the ledger — such as the third strongbox lies in the north cleft or the silver cups belong to Number 3 — and any bandit may file one. Crucially, every honest copy of the scroll must record the entries in the exact same order, because the meaning of a new entry depends on what the scroll already says. Two copies that wrote the same entries in different orders could end up giving the same strongbox to two different bandits.

Furthermore, these are later nights of the siege. The four bandits turned their sandglasses together once on the first night, and their glasses have long since drifted out of step.*

The four bandits require two guarantees for their loot scroll, and the two guarantees hold under very different conditions:

First guarantee · Safety

Order

Every honest copy writes the exact same entries in the exact same order. Together, the copies behave as if there were only a single scroll written by one hand at a single moment between when an entry is filed and when it stands (linearizability).*

Second guarantee · Liveness

Going on

Every entry filed by an honest bandit eventually stands (commits) in the scroll, provided the wind drops and stays calm for long enough.

Why the conditions differ

Safe in any wind, live in calm air

Order must never be violated, no matter how hard or how long the wind blows, because nobody can tell when the wind has dropped. Progress, by contrast, cannot be guaranteed while the wind pins the rats in their holes — otherwise the mercenaries at the foot of the hill could solve consensus with arbitrarily slow birds, which is impossible.*

We have four copies of the scroll, one liar among the four bandits, and a wind that can delay any rat for an unknown time. First, someone must be put in charge of assigning each entry its place in line.

Stated formally · what the four owe each other

Safety: the replicated service is linearizable (no two non-faulty replicas execute different requests at the same sequence number). Liveness: clients eventually receive replies to their requests, provided at most ⌊(n − 1)/3⌋ replicas are faulty and network delay(t) does not grow faster than t indefinitely. (Castro and Liskov, §§2–3.)

Who numbers the entries

The chief's job

To keep every copy of the scroll in the same order, each entry is given a sequential number (1, 2, 3, …) so that every post can write entries in number order. Assigning those numbers is the job of a single leader at a time, whom we call the registrar (the paper's primary). The job does not belong permanently to Bandit 1, because on the crown leadership is a rotating job, not a fixed rank.*

The registrar's job starts with Number 1 inside the ring wall. Whenever the job passes, it rotates around the posts in number order: 1, 2, 3, 4, and back to 1. Each spell under a single registrar is called a term (a view v), and because terms are numbered sequentially, the term number alone tells every bandit who the current registrar is.

When a bandit wants to add an entry to the scroll, they seal it and send it to the current registrar. The registrar assigns it the next sequence number and announces that numbering to the other posts. Once the four posts have cross-checked the numbering, every honest bandit writes the entry into their scroll in number order and sends the filer a sealed confirmation that the entry stands.

Because any of the four bandits may hold the registrar's job — including the liar — no one can trust the registrar's word alone. Every part of the protocol that follows is designed to defend against a lying registrar who tries any of three tricks:

First trick · Equivocation

Giving two entries the same number

The registrar assigns the same sequence number (say, entry 7) to two different updates.

Second trick · Splitting

Telling different posts different things

The registrar sends one numbering to some posts and a conflicting numbering (or nothing at all) to the rest.

Third trick · Stalling

Sitting on a filed entry

An honest bandit files an entry, and the registrar simply ignores it and numbers nothing.

How a filer knows their entry stands: on the crown, each bandit is one of the four replicas, so an honest bandit knows their entry stands the moment it stands in their own copy of the scroll. (In the paper's general client-server setup, an outside client that does not hold a copy of the log waits for f + 1 = 2 matching sealed replies from different replicas; since at most one replica lies, two matching confirmations guarantee that at least one honest replica has committed the entry.)

To defeat the registrar's first two tricks — numbering two different entries with the same number or splitting the posts — the bandits must cross-check every numbering before they write anything into their scrolls.

One entry, from filing to standing

Two echoes

The bandits verify the registrar's numbering by echoing it to one another twice. An entry stands only after the registrar has numbered it (pre-prepare) and the bandits have completed a first echo (prepare) and a second echo (commit). Every message is sealed in wax, and every bandit keeps the sealed messages they receive in a log so they can prove what happened later. Scroll through the six steps below to follow a single entry from filing to standing.

1 · The filing

An entry is filed

Number 2 wants to record an entry in the scroll. They stamp the entry with their seal and send it by rat to the current registrar, Number 1.

2 · The numbering

Number 1 assigns a number

The registrar assigns the entry sequence number 7 and sends a sealed numbering (pre-prepare) to all the other posts. Suppose the wind holds back the rat heading to Number 4 — nobody waits for it.

3 · The first echo

The numbering is echoed

Numbers 2 and 3 check that they have not already accepted a different entry for number 7 in this term. Each accepts the numbering and sends a sealed first echo (prepare) to every post: "In this term, I heard that entry 7 is this update."

4 · Readied

Three matching seals ready the entry

Once a bandit holds the registrar's numbering plus two matching first echoes from posts other than the registrar — three distinct seals in all on the same entry and number — the entry is readied (prepared) at that post. Posts 1, 2, and 3 now have entry 7 readied.

5 · The second echo

Readiness is echoed

Each bandit who has the entry readied sends a sealed second echo (commit) to every post: "In this term, entry 7 is readied at my post."

6 · Standing

The entry stands

Once a bandit holds a readied entry plus three matching second echoes (including their own), the entry stands (committed-local) at that post. Entry 7 now stands at Posts 1, 2, and 3 — even though Number 4 was cut off by the wind the entire time.

As soon as an entry stands at a post and every lower-numbered entry (1 through 6) has already been written, the bandit writes entry 7 into their scroll and sends a sealed confirmation to the filer. Because rats can arrive out of order, entry 9 might stand before entry 8; when that happens, the bandit holds entry 9 in memory and waits to write it until entry 8 stands as well, never leaving a gap in the scroll.

Notice that no step ever waits for all four posts. Every stage requires only three matching seals out of four (2f + 1), so a single post whose rats are pinned by the wind — or a single liar who refuses to answer — can never block the other three from making progress.

Why two echoes are needed: the two echoes solve two distinct problems. The first echo (reaching readied) locks in the order within a single term: even if the registrar lies and sends different entries for number 7 to different posts, no two honest posts can ever ready different entries for number 7 in the same term. The second echo (reaching standing) locks in the order across terms: once an entry stands at even one honest post, enough honest bandits have it readied that any future registrar in a later term will be forced to keep it.

Both guarantees rest on a single arithmetic fact: why three matching seals out of four are always enough, even when one of those three seals belongs to a liar.

Stated formally · an entry, from numbering to standing

In the paper's terminology, the registrar is the primary, the numbering is a PRE-PREPARE, the first echo is a PREPARE, readied is the predicate prepared(m, v, n, i) (holding the pre-prepare and 2f matching prepares from backups), the second echo is a COMMIT, and standing is committed-local(m, v, n, i) (prepared plus 2f + 1 matching commits). Writing in number order is deterministic state-machine execution in sequence-number order. (Castro and Liskov, §4.2.)

  1. Numbering (Pre-prepare). The registrar of term v assigns sequence number 7 to entry X and sends a sealed message ⟨PRE-PREPARE, v, 7, X⟩ to all posts.
  2. First echo (Prepare). A post accepts the numbering if the seal belongs to term v's registrar, the post is currently in term v, it has not already accepted a different entry for number 7 in term v, and 7 lies within the allowed sequence-number window (see settling checkpoints below). Upon accepting, the post sends ⟨PREPARE, v, 7, X⟩ to all posts. Once a post holds the numbering and two matching first echoes from posts other than the registrar — three distinct seals on (v, 7, X) — the entry is readied at that post.
  3. Second echo (Commit). Once (v, 7, X) is readied at a post, that post sends ⟨COMMIT, v, 7, X⟩ to all posts. Once a post holds the readied entry and three matching second echoes (possibly including its own), the entry stands at that post.
  4. Execution. Each post writes a standing entry into its copy of the scroll as soon as every lower sequence number has been written, and sends a sealed confirmation to the filer.
The whole trick

Why three seals, and four bandits

Three matching seals out of four are always trustworthy — even when one of the seals is a liar's — because any two groups of three chosen from four bandits must overlap in at least two bandits. Since at most one bandit on the crown is a liar, at least one of the two bandits in the overlap is guaranteed to be honest (quorum intersection).

Suppose a lying registrar tries to split the hill by telling two posts that entry 7 is X and telling the other posts that entry 7 is Y. For X to become readied anywhere, three distinct bandits must seal X for number 7 in this term. For Y to become readied anywhere, three distinct bandits must seal Y for number 7 in this term. Those two groups of three necessarily share at least one honest bandit — and an honest bandit never seals two different entries for the same sequence number in the same term! Therefore, X and Y can never both become readied.

Result · One Entry to a Number

Within any single term, no two conflicting entries can both become readied under the same sequence number at honest posts.

Proof · that the three seals of readiness cannot be split

In the paper's terms, an entry is a request m, a term is a view v, a sequence number is n, and there are 3f + 1 = 4 replicas (f = 1). Invariant (§4.2). If prepared(m, v, n, i) is true, then prepared(m′, v, n, j) is false for every non-faulty replica j and every m′ ≠ m with different digest.

  1. For an entry to be readied at any post in term v, that post must hold seals for that entry, sequence number, and term from three distinct bandits (the registrar's numbering plus two first echoes from other posts).
  2. Suppose for contradiction that two different entries X and Y were both readied for sequence number 7 in term v. The set of three bandits who sealed X and the set of three bandits who sealed Y are subsets of four bandits, so by the pigeonhole principle they share at least 3 + 3 − 4 = 2 bandits. Since at most one bandit is a liar, at least one bandit in their intersection is honest.
  3. That honest bandit must have sealed both X and Y for sequence number 7 in term v. However, an honest bandit accepts and echoes at most one entry per sequence number in any given term, and an honest bandit's wax seal cannot be forged. Contradiction.∎

Why four bandits are the minimum for one liar — even with seals: earlier we saw that on the first night of the siege, when the sandglasses were synchronized and every rat arrived within a known round, wax seals allowed just three bandits to agree despite one liar. Why do we need four bandits (3f + 1) now? Because now the wind can delay rats for an unknown time! Since one bandit might be a liar who stays deliberately silent, no post can ever afford to wait for all n bandits; it must be able to act after hearing from n − f of them. And because the wind might delay an honest bandit's rat while the liar answers promptly, the n − f replies a post hears may include all f liars and miss f honest bandits. Worse still, two different quorums of size n − f must still overlap in at least f + 1 bandits so that their intersection is guaranteed to contain at least one honest bandit: (n − f) + (n − f) − n ≥ f + 1, which simplifies to n ≥ 3f + 1.* Wax seals stop a liar from forging someone else's word, but they cannot tell you whether a missing rat was held back by a liar or by the wind.

Within a single term, then, a lying registrar can never get two different entries readied under the same number. What a lying registrar can still do is stall — refusing to number anything at all.

When nothing stands

Changing the job

If a registrar sits on filed entries and numbers nothing, the registrar's job must pass to the next bandit (a view change). Because nobody on the crown can tell a registrar who is deliberately stalling from an honest registrar whose rats are pinned by the wind, the bandits do not try to judge guilt. Each post simply limits how long it will wait.

Whenever a bandit has a filed entry waiting that has not yet stood, they turn their sandglass. If the sand runs out before the entry stands, the bandit asks for a change: they stop accepting messages for the current term and send every post a sealed request to move to the next term (a VIEW-CHANGE message). Attached to that request is the sealed proof of every entry that has been readied at their post — namely, the registrar's numbering and its two matching first echoes.

Once the next term's registrar has collected three sealed requests for the new term (including their own), they open the new term by sending every post a bundle containing those three requests together with a fresh numbering (a NEW-VIEW message). Crucially, the new registrar has no freedom to invent this numbering: it is completely dictated by the three sealed requests! For each sequence number, if any of the three requests proves that an entry was readied at that number, the new registrar must re-propose the entry readied in the latest term; if no request shows an entry for a number below the highest readied slot, the registrar must fill that gap with a harmless blank entry (a null request). Because the three sealed requests travel along with the new numbering, every bandit can independently verify that the new registrar followed the rule to the letter before echoing anything in the new term.

Why a new registrar can never erase an entry that already stood: suppose entry X stood at some honest post as number 7 in an earlier term. Before X could stand there, three bandits had to seal second echoes for it. At least two of those three bandits are honest, which means X was readied at both of their posts. Now, to start the new term, the new registrar must present three sealed change-of-job requests out of four posts. Any three posts out of four must include at least one of those two honest bandits! That honest bandit's request carries the sealed proof that X was readied as number 7, forcing the new registrar to keep X as number 7. Result · Order Across Terms

If an entry stands under a sequence number at any honest post, every honest post that ever has an entry stand under that sequence number is guaranteed to have the exact same entry, regardless of which terms they stood in.

Proof · that a new registrar must keep what stood before

In the paper's terms (§4.5.1): non-faulty replicas agree on the sequence numbers of requests that commit locally, even if they commit in different views at different replicas. Recall that committed-local(m, v, n, i) holds when prepared(m, v, n, i) holds and replica i has collected 2f + 1 matching COMMIT messages.

  1. Suppose entry X stands (commits locally) as sequence number 7 at some honest post in term v. Then three distinct posts sent second echoes (COMMIT) for (v, 7, X). Since at most one bandit lies, at least two honest bandits had (v, 7, X) readied (prepared) in term v.
  2. Consider the earliest term v′ > v in which any entry is readied for sequence number 7. To open term v′, its registrar must present three signed VIEW-CHANGE requests. By pigeonhole intersection among four posts, those three requests share at least one honest bandit with the two honest bandits who readied X in term v.
  3. Because no entry was readied for number 7 in any term strictly between v and v′, the highest-term readied proof for number 7 in the three VIEW-CHANGE requests is (v, 7, X) (since by the single-term invariant no conflicting entry could have been readied in term v). Therefore, the rule of NEW-VIEW forces the registrar of term v′ to re-propose X for number 7, and honest posts verify this proof before echoing. By induction over all subsequent terms, only X can ever be readied or stand for number 7.∎

The paper sketches this inductive invariant in §4.5.1; the complete formal proof (including checkpoint truncation) appears in Castro's doctoral thesis.

Stated formally · changing the job

In the paper's terms: VIEW-CHANGE messages carry the set P of prepared certificates; NEW-VIEW carries 2f + 1 signed VIEW-CHANGE messages and the deterministically computed set O of new PRE-PREPARE messages, using null requests to fill any gaps. (Castro and Liskov, §4.4.)

  1. The registrar of term v + 1 waits to receive three valid VIEW-CHANGE requests for term v + 1 (including its own).
  2. The new registrar broadcasts NEW-VIEW containing those three signed requests together with a fresh numbering O. For each sequence number between the highest stable checkpoint and the highest sequence number in P, if some request proves an entry was readied at that number, the registrar re-numbers the one readied in the latest term; otherwise it numbers a no-op blank entry.
  3. Each post verifies the signatures on the three requests and checks that O was computed according to the deterministic rule, then enters term v + 1 and runs the two-echo protocol for the entries in O.

Two additional safeguards keep term changes from happening too slowly or too easily. First, if an honest bandit receives two change-of-job requests for a higher term from other posts, they immediately join that change even if their own sandglass has not yet run out (since at least one of those two requests came from an honest comrade); this keeps a lagging post from being left behind. Second, no single liar can force a change of job on their own, because entering a new term requires three requests. A lying registrar can sabotage their own term by stalling, but the job then rotates away to an honest bandit in the very next term.

While a fierce wind blows, however, sandglasses will also run out on honest registrars whose rats are simply delayed by the gale, causing the job to pass again and again. How do the bandits stop changing terms once the wind finally drops?

What stops the changes

Going on once the wind drops

While the wind pins the rats in their holes, sandglasses expire on honest registrars and the job rotates from post to post. No entries stand during the gale — yet no conflicting entries ever stand either. Once the wind calms down, however, the premature term changes must stop, or the posts would keep interrupting each other before any entry could finish its two echoes.

To make sure the changes stop, each bandit doubles their sandglass (exponential backoff) every time a term ends without progress. If a bandit asks for Term 2 and their glass runs out before Term 2 starts or finishes, they ask for Term 3 — but they wait twice as long before giving up on Term 3. Eventually, the sandglasses grow longer than the time it takes to complete the three rat runs of a term (the numbering and the two echoes). As soon as an honest registrar takes office with a long enough sandglass in calm air, all three steps finish before the glass runs out, and the waiting entries stand.

Result · Going On

Once the wind drops and stays calm for long enough, every entry filed by an honest bandit eventually stands.

Castro and Liskov state the exact mathematical condition required for progress: the time a message takes to arrive (counted from when it was first sent, with retransmissions) must not grow faster than elapsed time t indefinitely. Because the sandglass doubles at every failed term, an exponentially growing timeout will eventually overtake any delay that does not itself grow exponentially.

Stated formally · how long the wind must stay down

In the paper's terms, if t is the time when a message is first sent (with periodic retransmission until acknowledged) and it arrives at time t + delay(t), liveness requires only that delay(t) does not grow faster than t indefinitely. Because the view-change timeout doubles on each consecutive view change, the timeout eventually exceeds the time required to complete a viewchange and execute a request under a non-faulty primary. (Castro and Liskov, §2 and §4.5.2.)

The four bandits now have guaranteed order in any wind and guaranteed progress once the wind drops. One practical problem remains: because a future change of job might require proof of old readied entries, every bandit has been saving every sealed rat they ever received. Left unchecked, that pile of wax seals would grow forever.

So the store does not grow for ever

Settling checkpoints

To keep their store of old messages from growing without bound, the bandits periodically prune their logs using settled checkpoints (stable checkpoints). Every hundred entries (say, at entry 100, 200, 300, …), each bandit writes a compact summary (a cryptographic digest) of their scroll's state up to that entry, stamps it with their seal, and sends it to every post. Once a bandit holds three matching sealed summaries for entry 100, that summary becomes a settled checkpoint: it proves that at least two honest bandits have the exact same scroll state through entry 100.

A settled checkpoint does three jobs at once. First, it lets a bandit throw away all stored numberings and echoes for sequence numbers 100 and below, replacing hundreds of old messages with a single three-seal checkpoint proof (which is also sent in any future change-of-job request). Second, it lets a slow post that fell behind catch up simply by fetching the state at the settled checkpoint from any of the three posts that sealed it. Third, it bounds the sequence numbers a registrar is allowed to assign (between a low water mark at the last settled checkpoint and a high water mark a fixed distance above it, such as +200), preventing a lying registrar from exhausting the numbering space by jumping to sequence number 1,000,000.

Stated formally · settling a checkpoint

In the paper's terms: replicas generate checkpoints every K requests (e.g., K = 100); a checkpoint becomes a stable checkpoint once a replica collects 2f + 1 matching signed CHECKPOINT messages for its state digest; the stable checkpoint sequence number sets the low water mark h, and the high water mark is H = h + k (e.g., k = 200). (Castro and Liskov, §4.3.)

  1. Every 100 entries executed, each post broadcasts a signed CHECKPOINT message containing the sequence number and a digest of its scroll state.
  2. Collecting three matching CHECKPOINT messages makes that checkpoint settled (stable), allowing the post to discard all pre-prepare, prepare, and commit logs at or below that sequence number.
  3. A post accepts a numbering from the registrar only if its sequence number lies strictly above the last settled checkpoint h and at or below H = h + 200.

With two echoes, a view-change rule, doubling sandglasses, and settled checkpoints, the four bandits have a complete, practical system. Before we return to the machine room, let us be clear about the boundaries of what this protocol does and does not promise.

The edges of the promise

What it does not say

Every protocol has boundaries that are better stated plainly than left for the reader to guess. Here are six questions about what the loot scroll guarantees — and what it leaves outside its scope:

One

Does anything stand while the wind blows?

No. While the wind pins the rats, no new entry can finish its echoes. Safety (order) holds in any wind, while liveness (progress) waits for the wind to drop — and by the FLP impossibility theorem, no deterministic protocol could ever do better.

Two

Can a liar file a false entry?

Yes. Consensus guarantees that every honest copy records the same entries in the same order; it cannot judge whether an entry that an authorized bandit files is truthful about the outside world. Access control and validation rules are separate checks applied by the state machine.

Three

Does anyone learn who the liar is?

No, and the protocol never needs to know. It works regardless of which of the four bandits is lying, without ever convicting them — because a silent post or a stalled term might just as easily be the work of the wind.

Four

Does it keep the scroll secret?

No. Every replica holds the full scroll in order to verify and execute entries, so a lying bandit can read everything in it. Confidentiality requires separate encryption.

Five

Must every rat carry a public-key wax seal?

In our story every rat is sealed for simplicity, which corresponds to public-key digital signatures. Because public-key signatures were slow in 1999, Castro and Liskov's practical optimization uses them only for rare VIEW-CHANGE and NEW-VIEW messages, replacing all everyday echoes with much faster symmetric message authentication codes (MAC vectors) — which is why the paper is called Practical BFT.

Six

What follows once an entry stands?

The scroll simply keeps the agreed record of where the loot lies and whose share it is; what the bandits do with their shares afterward lies outside the protocol.

A service replicated across 3f + 1 machines keeps every honest copy in the exact same order regardless of network delays or up to f liars, and makes progress as soon as delays stabilize.

Safety requires no timing assumptions at all; liveness requires only that message delays do not grow faster than time indefinitely.

In practice, fast symmetric message authentication codes (MACs) replace almost all public-key signatures during normal operation.

Setting The Crown of Mount Phyle

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Miguel Castro and Barbara Liskov, Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance. Proceedings of the Third Symposium on Operating Systems Design and Implementation (OSDI), New Orleans, February 1999. Introduces the three-phase normal-case protocol, view changes, stable checkpoints, the partial-synchrony liveness mechanism, and the MAC-based cryptographic optimization.

The ideas. Castro and Liskov present the first practical state-machine replication protocol that tolerates f arbitrary (Byzantine) faults out of 3f + 1 replicas in an asynchronous network, guaranteeing linearizable safety with no timing assumptions at all and liveness once message delays stop growing faster than time indefinitely.

Computation proceeds through numbered views, in each of which a single replica acts as the primary (chosen round-robin as p = v mod n) and assigns sequence numbers to client requests. Normal-case operation uses three phases: PRE-PREPARE (the primary proposes a sequence number for a request), PREPARE (replicas broadcast their agreement on that numbering), and COMMIT (replicas broadcast that they hold a prepared certificate of 2f + 1 matching messages). Because any two quorums of size 2f + 1 out of 3f + 1 overlap in at least f + 1 replicas — at least one of which is non-faulty — the prepare phase guarantees that no two conflicting requests can ever prepare at the same sequence number within a view. The commit phase ensures that if any non-faulty replica commits a request locally, at least f + 1 non-faulty replicas have prepared it.

When a backup's timer expires waiting for a request to execute, it votes for a view change, sending its prepared certificates to the next primary. The new primary gathers 2f + 1 signed VIEW-CHANGE messages into a NEW-VIEW message and re-proposes the highest-view prepared request for each sequence number. Because 2f + 1 view-change messages must intersect the f + 1 non-faulty replicas that prepared any locally committed request, committed requests are preserved across views. Exponentially doubling view-change timeouts and joining a higher view upon seeing f + 1 view-change messages ensure liveness once delays stabilize, while periodic 2f + 1 stable checkpoints garbage-collect the log and bound acceptable sequence numbers between water marks h and H.
