Arche · The Setting

The Ring Road

Three trading houses round a mountain that none of them can see past, joined by a road on which no messenger can pass another.

Read this first, and any book set on the ring road can be read next, in any order. It describes the island, the houses, the road and what carries word, which every book here takes for granted.

↓ Scroll. Things unfold.

The Island

A single limestone massif, Mount Phyle, fills the middle of Arche and leaves only a strip of coast round it. Three trading houses stand evenly spaced round that coast, each with a harbour town about it. The mountain is broad as well as high, so it stands between every pair of houses: no house can see another, and no line of sight crosses the island.

The houses pass goods and orders among themselves, and trade with the port towns about them. The books are set in the trading season, which on Arche is the season of low cloud. The cloud silences the sundials. Each counting room has a water-clock, filled by clerks who cannot see one another's, and the three keep no common hour. Nobody on the island knows what time it is anywhere but in his own room, and not truly there.

The Houses

The houses are known by number: House 1, House 2 and House 3. They are alike. Each has a storehouse of goods, a counting room with a book in which every piece of its business is written in order, and clerks who keep the book. Nothing sets one house apart from the others except the part an example gives it: the house an order is sent to, the house that begins a count. Where the houses must be put in some order, the order is by number.

A house knows only what is in its own book and what has reached its gate. It cannot look into another house's book, and it learns of another house's business only when a slip about it arrives.

The Road

A coastal ring road joins the houses, cut into the cliffs, with a lane each way. The law of the road is old and nobody on Arche breaks it: nobody overtakes. A fast messenger caught behind a slow cart walks behind it until the cart turns in at a gate.

By the same law a slip always goes by the direct stretch between two houses, never the long way round past a third house's gate. Three stretches, each travelled both ways, make six tracks, one for each ordered pair of houses, and each carries only its own traffic.

Messengers carry written slips along these tracks, and carts and pack-trains carry goods on the same tracks behind and in front of them. A messenger may be fast or slow, and a cart may be held up behind a lame mule for hours, so nobody can say how long anything will take. But nothing is lost: no messenger or cart falls from the cliff. And nothing is passed: whatever a house sends down a track arrives at the other end in the order it was sent.

Nothing Else Carries Word

The road is the only way word goes between the houses, and the books depend on that.

The mountain. Mount Phyle is held by a siege, with bandits on its crown and mercenaries at its foot, and in the trading season its summit is lost in cloud. The houses light no beacon on it and send no word over it. A goatherd who knows the smugglers' tracks sometimes crosses, but he may take two hours or two days or never come down, and no house sends its business that way. When he does carry news, it is news the houses' account scrolls cannot see.

The sea. Reefs and tide-races off every headland stop small boats coasting from one cove to the next. Ships go out to sea, not round the island.

The camps. The mercenaries' tents at the foot of the mountain carry nothing from one house to another.

What Every Book Here Assumes

Every book set on the ring road starts from the same few facts, and states them again in its own words.

(1) Three houses, alike, each acting only on its own book and on what reaches its gate.
(2) Six one-way tracks, one for each ordered pair of houses. What goes down a track arrives, in the order it was sent, after a time nobody can bound.
(3) No common hour. The sun cannot be read, and the water-clocks do not agree.
(4) Nothing else carries word.

Each book then asks one question of that island. How can the houses agree on what came first when they share no clock? What does it take for a record kept in several places to act as one? How can the goods of the whole island be counted while the carts keep moving? The island does not change from book to book; only the question does.

Reality Checks

What each part of this place has in common with the real Greek world, so that a reader can tell what is borrowed from what is made up. Close means the real thing is much like it; loose means it is borrowed and changed; invented means the setting made it up; anachronism means the real thing is of another age. A dagger (†) marks a citation given from memory and not checked against the text.

Place. Arche, the islandThe name is the Greek word archē, “beginning, first principle, rule”. For a round island with one great massif, the nearest real match is Samothrace, whose mountain Homer makes Poseidon's lookout over Troy (Iliad 13.12–14)†. The geometry itself is invented.Loose
Place. the ring road, one lane each way, no overtakingThe real cliff road of the Greek coast is the Scironian road from Megara to Corinth. In classical times it was a ledge fit for walkers and pack animals; the emperor Hadrian widened it so that vehicles could pass one another in opposite directions (Pausanias 1.44.6). A lane each way is a Roman standard, so a Hellenistic cliff road would be single track.Loose

The traveller's hat, the petasos, is Hermes' emblem, but the setting leaves it off: on a runner it reads as a modern brimmed hat. A pinched-crown hat, a sleeved tailored shirt, a buckled sandal and a stitched flap bag are all of a later age.Close
