Distributed Algorithms of Ancient Greece · Arche · The Ring Road

Ordering Without Clocks

If no two machines agree on the time, how can they agree on what happened first? And when two things happen far apart, how can anyone tell whether one led to the other or neither knew of the other?

Three houses around a central mountain, acting as a distribution network along a narrow coastal road where no messenger may pass another. To serve claims on a first-come basis when sundials fail under cloud, they invent a rising number that settles every dispute about what came first — and then discover what a single number must forever fail to see.

Chapter I

The Ring Road

Three trading houses stand evenly spaced round the coast of Arche, passing goods and orders between them: House 1, House 2 and House 3. Merchantmen from foreign lands unload at all three. Mount Phyle fills the middle of the island, so no house can see another, and word goes between them only on written slips carried by messengers.

The messengers use the coastal ring road: two cliff cuttings, one walked only sunwise and one only against the sun, each too narrow for one messenger to pass another, and a slip always goes by the direct stretch between two houses. So slips from one house to another arrive in the order they were sent. A messenger may be fast or slow, but he cannot be passed.

Nothing else carries word. The mountain is held by a siege* and capped by cloud. The same cloud silences the sundials, and the water-clocks in the three counting rooms, filled by clerks who cannot see one another, keep no common hour.

The trouble this causes has a definite shape. Say a cargo of foreign grain lands at House 3, and both House 2 and House 1 need it. House 2 sends a messenger to House 3 with a claim on the cargo. House 1, knowing nothing of this, sends a messenger to House 3 claiming it as well. Two messengers are on the road at once. Which claim was truly made first? If House 3 serves whichever messenger reaches its gate first, it may favor the house that happened to have a faster messenger rather than the house that asked first. The houses needed a way to serve claims on a strict first-come, first-served basis, without any shared clock to tell them what came first.

Chapter II

What Happened Before

Lamportios, who kept the books of House 1, began by noticing that the houses were asking for something that did not exist. They had been asking which of two independent events happened first in absolute time. What their business actually required was narrower: they needed to know when one event could have been the cause of another.

He set down three rules, and they are the whole foundation. One event came before another (written a → b) when:

The three rules of coming-before (1) They happened in the same house, and the book of that house has the first written above the second.
(2) The first is a house dispatching a slip, and the second is another house receiving that same slip.
(3) Or there is some third event that the first came before, and which came before the second.

If none of the three rules connects two events, Lamportios called them unrelated (or concurrent, written a ∥ b). Unrelated does not mean simultaneous. It means that neither event could have known of the other: no chain of slips joins them, whether or not a messenger could have been sent in time. The sun may have seen one of two unrelated events before the other, but nothing the houses hold can tell them which: no slip joins the two, and in the trading season the sun cannot be read.

Chapter III

The Rule of the Rising Number

To turn this partial order into something clerks could calculate with, Lamportios introduced a tally: a number kept in each house's counting room, written on every slip sent out, and updated according to two mechanical rules.

The two rules of the tally (1) A house advances its tally by at least one between any two entries in its own book.
(2) When dispatching a slip, a house writes its current tally on it. When receiving a slip, a house sets its own tally higher than the number on the slip, and never lowers it, before entering the arrival in its book.
The Clock Condition

Whenever event a came before event b, the tally of a is strictly smaller than the tally of b:
a → b ⟹ C(a) < C(b)

The rule guarantees that walking forward along any chain of messengers or local ledger lines always means strictly increasing numbers. But the converse is completely false: a smaller tally does not mean the event came before.

Two houses on opposite sides of Mount Phyle, having heard nothing from each other all morning, might assign tallies 2 and 3 to two completely unrelated events. A clerk reading only the numbers would conclude that the event marked 2 happened before the event marked 3, even though neither house had any knowledge of the other. The scalar tally manufactures a sequence where nature left none.

Chapter IV

Breaking Ties

A partial order cannot decide which house gets a cargo. When both House 2 and House 1 ask for the same grain, “the two claims are unrelated” does not help House 3. The houses needed a total order: an agreed rule that decides between every pair of events, yields the exact same answer at every house, and never contradicts causality.

Lamportios achieved this by using the houses' numbers to break ties, fixed once and for all: House 1 before House 2 before House 3.

The rule of precedence An event a at house i is earlier than event b at house j (written a ⇒ b) if:
(1) C(a) < C(b);
(2) or C(a) = C(b) and i is less than j.
Proof — that precedence is a total order extending coming-before
  1. Total comparability. For any two distinct events a and b: if their tallies differ, clause (1) decides. If their tallies are equal and they occurred at the same house, rule (1) of Chapter III already gave the later one a higher tally, so they must belong to different houses, whose numbers differ, and clause (2) decides.
  2. Antisymmetry and transitivity. Compare by the pair (C(e), house). This is the standard lexicographical ordering of pairs drawn from two total orders, which is strictly total and transitive.
  3. Consistency with causality. If a → b, then by the Clock Condition C(a) < C(b) strictly. Precedence places a earlier by clause (1) alone; house numbers are never consulted. Hence a → b ⟹ a ⇒ b.∎

Notice what precedence does and does not give House 3. Where one claim caused the other, it serves them in that order, and no faster messenger can change it. Where the two claims were unrelated, precedence does not find out which was "really" first; nothing the houses hold can find it, as Chapter II showed. It replaces the question with an answer every house reads the same way. That answer is fair in the only sense the houses can work out from their slips: no house can win by owning a quicker messenger. Judged by the sun instead, first come is a different promise, and the tallies do not keep it*.

Chapter V

The Shared Storehouse

The island maintains one common dry storehouse for Pontic grain, cut into the rock beside the road. Only one house may load from it at a time. The houses needed an arrangement where requests are granted strictly on a first-come, first-served basis, with no central master in charge.

A keeper at the door, granting whichever request reaches him first, would not do. That is the trouble of Chapter I again: a request can reach him ahead of one that was sent earlier and caused it, because it had a shorter road to run.

Using the total order of precedence, Lamportios gave the five rules of the storehouse. Each house maintains a private queue of requests ordered by precedence.

The five rules of the storehouse (1) To request: House i writes a request under its current tally, enters it in its queue, and sends a messenger carrying it to every other house.
(2) On receiving a request: House j enters the request in its queue, advances its tally, and dispatches an acknowledgement slip back to i.
(3) To release: When done, house i removes its request from its queue and dispatches a release slip to every other house.
(4) On receiving a release: House j removes that house's request from its queue.
(5) To enter: House i enters the storehouse when its own request stands first in its queue, and it has received from every other house a slip carrying a tally greater than its request.

The second condition of Rule 5 is the linchpin. It is not enough that your request is the earliest in your own ledger. A request older than yours may be on the ring road right now in a messenger's pouch. Hearing from another house with a higher tally proves that that house has progressed past the time your request was made, and therefore has no older request in transit. Because the road is single-file, no older message can be trailing behind that slip.

Proof — that two houses never enter the storehouse at once
  1. Suppose for contradiction that House 1 and House 3 both hold the storehouse at once, on requests R₁ and R₃. Precedence is total, so one is earlier; assume R₁ ⇒ R₃.
  2. By Rule 5, when House 3 entered, it had received from House 1 some message carrying a tally T > C(R₃).
  3. House 1 dispatched R₁ to House 3 under tally C(R₁) before dispatching any message with tally T. Because the coastal road is single-file, slips from House 1 to House 3 arrive in FIFO order.
  4. Therefore, House 3 received R₁ before receiving the message with tally T. So R₁ was already in House 3's queue when House 3 entered.
  5. Since R₁ ⇒ R₃, R₃ did not stand first in House 3's queue. Rule 5 was violated, and House 3 could not have entered.∎
Chapter VI

The Order That Came Over the Mountain

The tally solves ordering within the network of messengers. But real life contains an embarrassment: causality that moves outside the road.

Suppose a foreign merchant at House 3 concludes a sale, then pays a goatherd to cross Mount Phyle to House 1 by the smugglers' tracks. It is not a road anybody uses by choice: the tracks run between the mercenaries' tents and under the bandits' wall, a man who takes them may be turned back or not come down at all, and nobody can say whether he will take two hours or two days. That is why the houses do not send their slips that way. But this time the goatherd is lucky, and arrives at House 1 in two hours. House 1 immediately issues an order based on the goatherd's news, and that order bears a tally from House 1's own book. House 1 has had a quiet morning; its tally stands at 3. House 3's busy counting room had reached 9 when the sale was entered. House 1's order, which the sale caused, carries a smaller tally than the sale itself — and six hours later House 3's messenger arrives by the road with the official letter, too late to correct anything.

The tally cannot see the goatherd, because he carried no slip and the rules of Chapter III never touched him. What was wanted is the Strong Clock Condition: if event a precedes event b in the physical world, by any path at all, the tallies must satisfy C(a) < C(b). No rule about slips can give that, since the path that matters carried no slip. Only clocks that run with the world can.

Lamportios' remedy has three parts, and all three are needed. Each house keeps a physical clock whose rate is true to within a small fraction κ. Each slip carries the sender's clock reading, and on receiving it a house sets its own clock forward, if it is behind, to that reading plus the least time the road could possibly have taken. Provided messengers go down every stretch of road regularly, and the road has a longest crossing in fair weather, resetting clocks this way keeps the three clocks within some bound ϵ of one another. And the condition then holds only if ϵ is smaller than the fastest crossing any goatherd could make: a man who reaches House 1 sooner than the clocks can disagree can still arrive, by House 1's clock, before he left.

The houses do not keep such clocks. The remedy needs a longest crossing and messengers on every stretch at regular hours, and the trading season gives neither: a messenger takes as long as he takes, and nobody on the island knows the hour. Arche's clocks are the water-clocks of the counting rooms, which keep no common hour, and the tallies are all the order the houses have.

Chapter VII

The Column Room

The scalar tally answers who goes first. It cannot answer whether two events were independent. A clerk holding two entries with tallies 4 and 5 cannot tell if entry 5 was prompted by entry 4, or if they occurred in total ignorance across the island.

Fidgeos at House 2 and Matternos at House 3 discovered the remedy. Instead of a single tally, each house keeps an array — a column with three rows, one for each house on the island. V[i] is the latest tally that this house knows house i has reached.

The rules of the column (1) When entering any event of its own, the dispatch of a slip included, house i increments its own row: Vi[i] := Vi[i] + 1.
(2) When sending a slip, house i stamps its full column Vi on the slip, as it stands after that increment.
(3) On receiving slip with column U, house j merges each row by taking the maximum, then increments its own row: Vj[k] := max(Vj[k], U[k]), and Vj[j] := Vj[j] + 1.
Chapter VIII

What No Tally Can See

With columns, causality is not merely respected; it is isomorphic to the timestamps. Comparing two vector timestamps by component-wise dominance settles causality completely.

Vector Equivalence

For any two events a and b:
a → b ⟺ V(a) < V(b)
And two events are unrelated if and only if neither column dominates:
a ∥ b ⟺ ¬(V(a) ≤ V(b)) ∧ ¬(V(b) ≤ V(a))

Proof — that vector timestamps detect causality and concurrency exactly
  1. If a occurs at house i with timestamp V(a) and a → b, there is a causal chain of local events and messages from a to b. By Rules 1–3, every step non-strictly increases all components and strictly increases the component of the executing process. Thus V(a)[i] ≤ V(b)[i]. Since at least one step strictly increments a component, V(a) < V(b).
  2. Conversely, suppose a occurs at house i and does not precede b (a ↛ b). Then no slip carrying information about a has reached b. By Rule 3, the value of row i at b reflects only events at i that causally precede b. Since a is not among them, V(b)[i] < V(a)[i]. Thus V(a) ≰ V(b).
  3. Therefore, a → b if and only if V(a) < V(b). Concurrency a ∥ b holds if and only if neither column dominates the other.∎
