The Hall of Two Doors

A hall at the fork of the island that rules on nothing, and only sorts every question into one of two doors.

Read this first, and the book set in the hall can be read on its own. It describes the island of Paxos and the hall at its fork, between the assemblies and the scholars.

The Island of Paxos

Paxos is shaped like a Y. Two arms reach north and face each other across a bay: the western arm holds the Chamber of the Paxon Parliament, the eastern arm the city of Schedia and its Tholos. The arms join at a fork, where the hall of two doors stands, and below the fork the stem widens into the body of the island, the scholars' coast. The island road runs up the stem, forks, and runs out along both arms.

Paxos can read the sun. Unlike Arche in its trading season, the island tells time by day, and the Chamber has a meridian line. Word goes by messenger and by courier along the island's roads, and takes as long as it takes.

The Hall

The hall of two doors stands at the fork of the island, on common ground that belongs to neither city, as the Messon on Lesbos was the common sanctuary of that island's cities. It has two doors and no more. One faces north, up the bay between the arms, towards the Chamber and the Tholos: the two bodies on Paxos that assemble. The other faces south, down the stem, towards the scholars' coast, which does not.

The hall does not rule on anything. It sorts. Every question brought to it leaves by the door its own shape decides.

Two Kinds of Question

Some questions only grow. Has at least one copy of this work been made? can be answered yes as soon as it is true, and once it is yes it is yes for ever, whatever news comes later. A question of that kind needs no assembly, and it leaves by the scholars' door.

Other questions rest on absence. Is this every copy that has been made? or Has no one claimed this? can be answered only by hearing from everyone, since a single late letter could overturn the answer. A question of that kind needs everyone to wait on everyone, and it leaves by the door of the assemblies.

Putting both cities and the scholars on one island makes the difference a walk: the scholars could take any question to an assembly, and the hall shows which questions need one.

What each part of this place has in common with the real Greek world, so that a reader can tell what is borrowed from what is made up. Close means the real thing is much like it; loose means it is borrowed and changed; invented means the setting made it up; anachronism means the real thing is of another age.
