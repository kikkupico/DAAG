# Hellenistic Greek weapons, siege machinery, ships and land transport (323-31 BC): second pass

Method and limits, read first:
- Philo of Byzantium (Belopoeica, Schiefsky/Marsden edition) and Marsden's books were NOT read directly. The Harvard PDF returned 403 and Attalus returned 404. Artillery facts come from Wikipedia, Vitruvius Book 10 (via a summarising fetch of the Thayer text, so figures need checking against the text), and Antigone Journal.
- The Met Open Access API (collectionapi.metmuseum.org) was blocked (Incapsula HTML, 403 via WebFetch). Met objects below are the CC0 "MET DP..." files uploaded to Wikimedia Commons, with the Commons description and licence. Met accession numbers were not retrievable.
- Pomey (OpenEdition chapter) and two journal PDFs were read in full as text. Kyrenia II and sailing-trial material is experimental archaeology, not ancient evidence.
- Dates are flagged. Vitruvius (c. 25 BC) and Heron (1st c. AD) are Roman-era writers reporting Hellenistic engineers.

## 1. Torsion and tension artillery, fortifications, siege machinery

### Takeaway
Hellenistic artillery grew from the hand-held gastraphetes (Classical origin) through stand-mounted tension bow-machines (oxybeles) to torsion machines in spring frames, sized by one module (the spring-hole diameter). Torsion engines were in use by 338-326 BC (Athens) and 332 BC (Tyre). Towers with artillery chambers, wall-thickness rules and the giant helepolis (Rhodes, 305-304 BC) are the visual set pieces. Euthytonon versus palintonon is DEBATED.

### Cited Findings
- Gastraphetes ("belly-releaser"): composite-bow hand crossbow cocked by pressing the stock against the belly; described by Heron (1st c. AD) from Ctesibius. No pictures or finds survive. DATE DEBATED: Marsden's long-dominant view is invention at Syracuse in 399 BC, but recent work says Diodorus describes the katapeltikon, not the gastraphetes, so the gastraphetes predates 399 BC and perhaps 421 BC (Biton credits Zopyros). Classical, not Hellenistic. — [Wikipedia: Gastraphetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastraphetes)
- Oxybeles: a larger gastraphetes on a stand, with winch and trigger, tension (bow) powered; supplanted by torsion ballistae. It was a siege engine with a slow rate of fire. — [Wikipedia: Oxybeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxybeles); [Wikipedia: Ballista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballista)
- Torsion: earliest clear evidence is an Athenian arsenal inscription of 338-326 BC listing catapults with sinew springs. Likely spurred by Philip II's engineers. — [Wikipedia: Catapult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catapult)
- First recorded torsion use: Tyre, 332 BC. Hair (human or horse) was preferred for skeins as well as sinew. Rhodes received about 3/4 tonne of women's hair in 250 BC and Seleucus gave 1,000 talents of hair in 225 BC. Little improvement after Demetrius Poliorcetes (c. 300 BC). — [Wikipedia: Greek and Roman artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_and_Roman_artillery) (summarised fetch)
- Spring frame and parts: two arms each pass through a bundle of twisted skeins held in a frame with a "capital"; bronze or iron caps (washers, peritreta, always metal) secure each bundle and adjust with pins; arms are bowstring-linked; a slider on the stand carries the bolt; a winch and claw span it. Washers are the main Hellenistic artillery pieces archaeologists find. — [Wikipedia: Ballista](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballista); [Wikipedia: Greek and Roman artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_and_Roman_artillery)
- Euthytonon ("straight-spring"): arrow-thrower, arms outside the frame; palintonon ("V-spring"): stone-thrower, arms inside the frame, sometimes shot arrows. Scholarly debate persists on the precise meaning of the two terms. The Lithobolos article instead calls the euthytonon a "single-arm torsion catapult", which conflicts with the other page; do not adopt the single-arm reading. — [Wikipedia: Greek and Roman artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_and_Roman_artillery); contradicted by [Wikipedia: Lithobolos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithobolos)
- Palintonon is described in chapter 3 of Philo's Belopoeica (via search snippet; Philo not read) — [search result: Schiefsky Belopoeica](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/schiefsky/files/philo_final_proofs_2015-01-29.pdf) (403 on fetch)
- Module formulas: bolt-thrower hole diameter = bolt length / 9 (Vitruvius). Stone-thrower: D = 1.1 x cube root of (100 W) dactyls, W in Attic minae (mina 436.6 g, dactyl 19.3 mm). Arm length = 7 hole diameters; Vitruvius's channel 19 holes. Philo's principle: every part is proportional to spring size. — [Antigone Journal: Ancient Artillery](https://antigonejournal.com/2024/10/ancient-artillery/); [Wikipedia: Greek and Roman artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_and_Roman_artillery); [Vitruvius X via Thayer](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/10*.html) (summarised, chapter numbers unverified)
- Tuning: Vitruvius says skeins were tightened until both sounded the same note when struck. — [Vitruvius X via Thayer](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/10*.html) (summarised)
- Performance: arrow-firers maximum about 500 m, a 1-talent (26 kg) stone to about 370 m, field range about 200 m to first bounce; crews of two for arrow-firers. Philo: effective range against fortifications about 150 m with a 27 kg (60 lb) stone, and heavier Demetrian machines threw up to 75 kg stones. — [Antigone Journal](https://antigonejournal.com/2024/10/ancient-artillery/); [Wikipedia: Greek and Roman artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_and_Roman_artillery); [Wikipedia: Lithobolos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithobolos)
- CONFLICT, wall thickness Philo says is needed to withstand stone shot: about 5 m at 150 m for a 27 kg stone ([Lithobolos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithobolos)); "at least 4.62 m" ([search summary of Hellenistic fortification sources](https://warhistory.org/@msw/article/hellenistic-military-developments-iv)); "10 cubits (about 3 m)" ([Greek and Roman artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_and_Roman_artillery)). Ten cubits is about 4.4-4.6 m, so "3 m" looks wrong. Report as 3-5 m, not resolved.
- Hellenistic towers: single-storey squares gave way to multi-storey towers with large rectangular artillery embrasures in upper chambers plus arrow slits; towers first built into the wall, later separate; round or polygonal towers harder to bond into flat walls. — [search summary (Ober, Hellenistic fortification)](https://warhistory.org/@msw/article/hellenistic-military-developments-iv); [Wikipedia: Hellenistic fortifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_fortifications)
- Aigosthena (Megarid, Hellenistic): eight artillery towers, one of the best-preserved Hellenistic circuits and tallest surviving towers in Greece; masonry is isodomic, pseudo-isodomic and near-polygonal; citadel about 90 x 187 m; west main gate and a postern. Storeys, windows and wall thickness were not in the source. — [Wikipedia: Aigosthena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aigosthena)
- Messene: 369 BC walls (Classical, Theban) with about 30 towers, square and U-shaped, 9.5 km circuit (search summary; figures vary: 7 m thick, 9 m high are from a tourist-grade summary). Most substantial restored remains are Hellenistic and Roman. NOT an artillery-tower reference in the Hellenistic sense. — [Wikipedia: Messene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messene); [search result: Peter Sommer](https://www.petersommer.com/blog/archaeology-history/messene)
- Siege towers: Hellenistic towers up to about 40 m high and 20 m wide (Helepolis, Rhodes 305 BC) needed level ground; builders covered them with iron plates or fresh hides. — [Wikipedia: Siege tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_tower)
- Vitruvius on Helepolis (Epimachus, for Demetrius): 125 ft high, 60 ft wide, about 360,000 lb, resisted 360-lb stones; Rhodes countered by cutting a hole in the wall opposite and pouring water and filth to make a quagmire. Vitruvius also gives Diades' ram-tortoise (30 cubits wide, kriodoke roller) and Hegetor of Byzantium's tortoise (60 ft long, 8 wheels, 106 ft ram, 100 men, 4,000 talents). Spelled "Agetor" in the summary. Numbers are the Roman author's, via a summary. — [Vitruvius X via Thayer](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/10*.html)
- Ram: Vitruvius credits Ceras of Carthage with the first wheeled, roofed ram, so slow it was called the "tortoise" (testudo). Greek "chelone" is the same device. The soldiers' testudo shield formation is Roman. — [Wikipedia: Battering ram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battering_ram); [Wikipedia: Testudo formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testudo_formation)
- Rhodes 305-304 BC (earlier note) and 800 incendiary cylinders fired by defenders (summary of Diodorus, via Greek and Roman artillery). — [Wikipedia: Greek and Roman artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_and_Roman_artillery)
- Ship-mounted artillery: Demetrius at Salamis 306 BC; Syracusia reportedly carried an 18-ft machine firing stones up to 180 lb. — [Wikipedia: Lithobolos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithobolos)
- Mines and counter-mines: Rhodian counter-mines at Rhodes. — [Wikipedia: Siege of Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Rhodes_(305%E2%80%93304_BC))

### Inferences
- A visual canon for an artillery tower: two or three storeys, large rectangular upper windows (probably with shutters, not sourced), arrow slits below, ashlar courses. Window shape and shutters need Ober or Winter.
- Marsden-type machines should show a rectangular wooden frame with two bundles of skeins, bronze washers with holes around the rim, two arms, a bowstring, a slider and a rear winch. Roman reliefs and Trajan's Column are later; do not copy their metal frames for Hellenistic art.

### Gaps
- Philo, Biton and Marsden's tables not read; euthytonon/palintonon geometry unresolved.
- Siege ramps (agger), saps and mine-breaking details, chelone variants, Messene tower storeys and embrasure shapes, Rhodian city wall: nothing sourced beyond the above.

## 2. Armour, kit, guards, militia, pirates, naval rams

### Takeaway
Hellenistic infantry kit is a mix: Phrygian and Boeotian helmets, linen cuirasses, small pelte for peltasts, oval thureos for medium troops, and the aspis in citizen levies. Muscle cuirasses and Attic helmets mostly mark officers and heroic or royal art. Chalcidian helmets are Classical and fading. Citizen militias, ephebic garrisons and hired Cretans, Thracians and Galatians fill local defence.

### Cited Findings
- Muscle cuirass: bronze (later also boiled leather), cast or hammered in front and back halves, about 25 lb; Thracian cavalry tombs; in later art it is worn by generals, rulers and deities while soldiers used other types. Companion cavalry wore bronze muscle cuirass or linothorax with shoulder guards. — [Wikipedia: Muscle cuirass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muscle_cuirass); [Wikipedia: Companion cavalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companion_cavalry)
- Greaves: bronze with felt padding; "well-greaved" is a Homeric epithet. Hellenistic prevalence not stated in this source (the Met greaves below are 4th c. BC). — [Wikipedia: Greave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greave)
- Chalcidian helmet: hemispherical dome, cheekpieces, neck guard, nasal bar, ear loops; popular 5th-4th c. BC; probably worn by some Macedonians; evolved into the Attic type. Attic helmet: like Chalcidian but no nasal bar; the Attic form survives mainly as an archaising motif in art for generals; no Roman-period Attic helmet remains. — [Wikipedia: Chalcidian helmet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcidian_helmet); [Wikipedia: Attic helmet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attic_helmet)
- Helmet and sarissa basics are in the first pass (Phrygian, Boeotian, sarissa length, head size debate). — [Wikipedia: Sarissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa)
- Aspis: deeply dished wood shield over 0.9 m, about 7 kg, central porpax and rim grip; only the Bomarzo shield survives. Classical origin; use for citizen hoplites. — [Wikipedia: Aspis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspis)
- Pelte: crescent wicker shield covered with goat or sheepskin, rimless; peltasts are Thracian in origin and wear the fox-skin Phrygian cap, patterned tunics, fawnskin boots and zeira cloaks in vase art. — [Wikipedia: Peltast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peltast)
- Thureos: oval Celtic-derived shield with spine; see first pass. — [Wikipedia: Thureophoros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thureophoros)
- Dory: about 2-3 m, 5 cm shaft of cornel or ash, flat iron leaf head; the earlier hoplite spear. Xyston: cavalry lance 3.5-4.25 m, cornel, a point at each end; later also called kontos. — [Wikipedia: Dory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dory_(spear)); [Wikipedia: Xyston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xyston)
- Prodromoi: skirmisher cavalry with javelins and sword, sometimes linen or leather armour and bronze helmets; Macedonian prodromoi (sarissophoroi) carried a lance of about 14 ft. — [Wikipedia: Prodromoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prodromoi)
- Companion cavalry: Alexander's day xyston, muscle cuirass or linothorax, Boeotian helmet, no shield, kopis or xiphos; Classical-Macedonian, renamed xystophoroi after Alexander. — [Wikipedia: Companion cavalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companion_cavalry)
- Rhomphaia: Thracian single-edged blade 60-80 cm with a tang about 50 cm, straight or slightly curved, so used on a long grip like a polearm; falx is more curved. — [Wikipedia: Rhomphaia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhomphaia)
- Galatians: torcs found in 3rd-c. BC Hidirsihlar tumulus; Dying Gaul (Roman copy of a Pergamene Hellenistic original) shows a torc; Galatians were widely hired mercenaries; Attalus I defeated them c. 240-230 BC. — [Wikipedia: Galatians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galatians_(people))
- Kausia: flat Macedonian hat, purple with diadem for kings, worn by poorer classes. — [Wikipedia: Kausia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kausia)
- Ephebes (Athens, Classical rules; foreigners admitted in Hellenistic times): after the first year the state gave each a spear and shield; chlamys and petasos made up their equipment; they served as garrison and frontier patrol. — [Wikipedia: Ephebe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephebe)
- Aeneas Tacticus (mid-4th c. BC, Classical): his siege manual covers choosing trustworthy guards, securing walls and gates, and torch-and-water-clock signalling. — [Wikipedia: Aeneas Tacticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeneas_Tacticus)
- Pirates: after Alexander, brigand crews and state-hired mercenaries were sources of piracy; Delos and Cilicia had slave markets (up to 10,000 slaves in a day per Strabo); Rhodes and the Islanders' League policed pirates. Lembus and hemiolia are in the first pass. — [Wikipedia: Piracy in the ancient Mediterranean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piracy_in_the_ancient_Mediterranean)
- Naval ram: the Athlit ram is a single 465 kg bronze casting around timber; the front wall is 6.8 cm thick; lost-wax cast. Date is Hellenistic-adjacent (Classical to early Hellenistic); check before using. — [Wikipedia: Ram (ship)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ram_(ship))
- Lindos relief (rock-cut near the staircase to the Lindos acropolis): it is a warship STERN, not a prow. Dated about 180 BC or 2nd c. BC; base for a bronze statue of Hagesandros son of Mikion; shows the curved stern and steering oars. Sources conflict on the sculptor (Pythokritos or Hagesandros) and are tourism sites, so treat as weak. — [lindos-rhodes.gr](https://lindos-rhodes.gr/engraved-warship-stern-at-lindos/); [search results: Lindos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindos)
- Trireme sheds and decoration: eyes, nameplates, painted figureheads and ornaments (Classical trireme evidence). — [Wikipedia: Trireme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme)

### Inferences
- A local night watchman on an island plausibly carries a spear, pelte or thureos, wears chlamys and petasos, and is an ephebe or militiaman. Not directly sourced for Hellenistic islands; the evidence is Classical.
- Mixed kit in one band (Phrygian helmet, linothorax, thureos, javelins) fits the sources.

### Gaps
- Not found: Sekunda detail, Chalcidian or Attic helmets in Hellenistic use, thureos finds, linothorax pteruges counts, lembos crew gear, bandit gear, night-watch kit on islands, Galatian dress beyond the torc, Hellenistic ram form (the Athlit date is not stated in my source).

## 3. Ships and boats

### Takeaway
Pomey defines a "Hellenistic type": shell-first, mortise-and-tenon planking, wine-glass section, alternating floor timbers and half-frames, with Kyrenia (hull dated 325-315 BC, sank c. 295-285) as the prototype. Rigging, steering-oar and cargo-stowage evidence are mostly experimental archaeology on Kyrenia II. Painted eyes, hull colours, fishing boats, and Hellenistic cranes are unsourced.

### Cited Findings
- Hellenistic hull type (Pomey): cross-section with a retour de galbord (wine-glass), axial frame of keel, stem and sternpost, rabbeted keel, carvel planking assembled by mortise-and-tenon, alternating floor timbers and half-frames nailed or treenailed, mast-step timber on the back of the floor timbers. Lead sheathing appears first on Kyrenia and is mentioned on the Syracusia and the Marsala wreck (mid-3rd c. BC). — [Pomey, Naval Architecture (OpenEdition)](https://books.openedition.org/obp/14374)
- Kyrenia is dated 295-285 BC for sinking and built about 325-315 BC, with many repairs. — [Pomey (OpenEdition)](https://books.openedition.org/obp/14374)
- Giant ships: Syracusia (3rd c. BC) "2000 to 4000 tonnes" per Pomey; the first-pass source gave 1,600-1,800 t. Conflict; literary figures. The common large type is the myriophoros (10,000 amphorae, about 500 t). Madrague de Giens (75-60 BC, Roman Republic): nearly 40 m x 9 m x 4.5 m, about 400 t, double planking with lead sheathing. Other Hellenistic-type wrecks: Grand Congloue, Mahdia, Albenga, Dramont A. — [Pomey (OpenEdition)](https://books.openedition.org/obp/14374)
- Super-galleys appear from Demetrius Poliorcetes (end 4th c. BC) up to Ptolemy IV's "forty" with 4,000 rowers. — [Pomey (OpenEdition)](https://books.openedition.org/obp/14374)
- Kyrenia load: Katzev reports 17 tons of materials found, 29 millstones averaging 57 kg, and a Rhodian amphora full of wine at 49 kg; a main cargo of 220 large Rhodian amphorae was replicated for loading trials (the first pass cites 340 Rhodian amphorae from Wikipedia; possibly different counts, not reconciled); brail rings: "over 150" lead rings (first pass said about 100). — [Katzev 2008, Near Eastern Archaeology 71](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/ETUDESarchivees/Navires/Kyrenia/KyreniaLiberty-Katzev2008.pdf)
- Kyrenia sail: a single broad square sail "typical of those seen in ancient vase painting"; lines ran through rings sewn to the sail (brailing). — [Katzev 2008](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/ETUDESarchivees/Navires/Kyrenia/KyreniaLiberty-Katzev2008.pdf)
- Experimental archaeology (not ancient evidence): the replicas stowed about three layers of amphorae with smaller ones nested on top; loaded 12 t and carried about 10 t earlier; Steffy suspects the ancient hull was heightened to carry heavier loads; a "mast derrick" lifting boom with an iron hook like one found in the excavation sped loading, but Katzev says "We cannot say convincingly" that Kyrenia had one; goat-skin tied wet over amphora mouths worked as a stopper. — [Katzev 2008](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/ETUDESarchivees/Navires/Kyrenia/KyreniaLiberty-Katzev2008.pdf)
- Kyrenia II sailing: heading about 61 degrees off the wind; it broke several steering oars while close-hauled; single square sail and steering oars are not very manoeuvrable. Speed on the Wikipedia page: original 4-5 knots; replica 2-6 knots on trials. — [Davey 2015, Buried History 51](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/ETUDESarchivees/Navires/Documents/Davey2015-SpritsailArtemon.pdf) (read as extracted PDF); [Wikipedia: Kyrenia ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyrenia_ship)
- Artemon / spritsail: Davey argues the small forward square sail is a Roman-period feature shown in iconography (coins, mosaics), used for steering assistance with a single square sail. ROMAN evidence; no Hellenistic image verified. — [Davey 2015](https://www.ancientportsantiques.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/ETUDESarchivees/Navires/Documents/Davey2015-SpritsailArtemon.pdf)
- Steering oars: two stern steering oars on the trireme (Classical). — [Wikipedia: Trireme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme)
- Anchors: Greeks used stone baskets, sand-filled sacks and lead-filled logs as weights; the stock-and-fluke form seen in Nemi ships is Roman (1st c. AD). — [Wikipedia: Anchor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchor)
- Cranes: Greek compound pulley cranes appear in construction contexts; the "heyday" is Roman. No Hellenistic harbour crane source found. — [Wikipedia: Crane (machine)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crane_(machine))
- Piraeus sheds: see first pass (Classical). Trireme sheds about 4 m interior height at Zea. — [Wikipedia: Trireme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trireme)

### Inferences
- A Kyrenia-size coaster (about 14.7 m) is the safe small merchant ship; show the brailed sail with ring lines, two stern steering oars, a stern deck and a boom-hoist as an option only.
- Roman mosaics (Palestrina, Ostia, Themetra) show the Hellenistic hull type in later copies per Pomey, so a Palestrina ship is valid shape evidence but late 2nd c. BC Roman Republic.

### Gaps
- Painted eyes and hull colours for Hellenistic merchant ships (only Classical trireme "eyes" found), fishing boats, caiques, rowing boats, Rhodian polyreme reliefs beyond the Lindos stern, harbour cranes, ship-sheds at Hellenistic ports, amphora dunnage (brushwood) — not found; the first-pass memory notes remain unverified.

## 4. Land transport

### Takeaway
Roads were narrow tracks; mules and donkeys carried most goods, ox carts only heavy loads. Riding was without stirrups. No second-pass evidence on litters, bridges, inns or couriers.

### Cited Findings
- Greek roads: "rudimentary", typically under 2 m wide and rarely over 3 m, gradients up to 30 percent in mountains; Argos-Tegea has paving and switchbacks; oxen about 1 mph vs mules about 3 mph; four-spoked wheels on chariots and light carts, solid wooden wheels on heavy carts; platform with wicker sides. Period mixed (Classical-Roman). — [Encyclopedia.com: Roads and Land Traffic](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/roads-and-land-traffic) (summarised fetch)
- Donkeys and mules were ridden, harnessed or used as pack animals carrying three or four times a human's load; mules generally carry up to about 90 kg. — [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/roads-and-land-traffic); [Wikipedia: Mule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mule)
- Pack saddle: wooden tree on a blanket with breeching and crupper (general modern description, not Greek). — [Wikipedia: Pack saddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pack_saddle)
- Stirrups: earliest hard foot support is an Indian big-toe loop of the late 2nd c. BC, so Hellenistic Greek riders use none. — [Wikipedia: Stirrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirrup)
- Travel speeds: Pausanias walked a little under 3 mph; ordinary overland pace 24-40 km/day; a horse rider up to 56 km/day; a loaded ox cart about 12 km/day (secondary summaries, mixed periods). ORBIS speeds are Roman. — [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/roads-and-land-traffic); [search results: ancientsociety.com](https://ancientsociety.com/rome/how-long-did-it-take-to-travel-in-ancient-rome/)
- Chariots: Greek chariots drew two horses on a central pole; scythed chariots appear in 4th c. BC accounts with a single driver. Chariots were war and racing vehicles, not transport. — [Wikipedia: Chariot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariot)
- Terracotta donkey carrying vessels (figurine) exists as a PD Commons file (see section 5).

### Inferences
- A rural Aegean path scene: narrow path, donkey with two paired vessels or panniers on a pack saddle, rider on a pad or blanket without stirrups.

### Gaps
- Litters, bridges, inns, couriers and royal posts, mule saddle types in Hellenistic art, harmamaxa: nothing new sourced.

### Inferences
- For Hellenistic helmets and armour, the Met CC0 items are 4th c. BC and Italic as much as Greek, so label them "late Classical/early Hellenistic". The Alexander mosaic and Agios Athanasios fresco remain the best Hellenistic-period reference for Macedonian kit.
