First, in machine terms

Many computers, one decision

A group of computers must all settle on the same one of two values.

  • Messages take any time at all. Every message arrives, but no delay is too long to be possible.
  • One machine may stop at any moment, without a word.
  • Fischer, Lynch and Paterson (1985): no protocol that acts only on what it has received can guarantee that the group ever decides.

This edition tells it with four mercenaries, their ravens, and a hill they must take together or not at all.

One question, two answers

Attack, or hold

The four mercenaries must all attack the hill together, or all hold and keep it closed. Half attacking while half hold is worse than either.

Each man formed an opinion at dusk from what he could see of his own quarter. A man commits by leaving his tent, to climb or to guard his gully, and he will not be back before dawn. The four want three things of whatever plan they follow.

First demand

No two commit differently

Nobody attacks while another holds.

Second demand

Both answers stay possible

What they do must depend on what they saw.

Third demand

Somebody eventually commits

A plan under which nobody ever moves keeps the first two for free.

And they may not draw lots. Each man acts on what has reached him and nothing else.

Two easy plans try each

The hill from above. The badge beside each tent is what that man saw at dusk: A for attack, H for hold.

Each easy plan keeps two demands. Find the one it gives up.

The difficulty, entire

A dead man, or a slow bird?

The ravens always arrive. They take as long as they take, come in no particular order, and bring exactly what was written.

A man may always walk to his perch and find nothing there. That is not news. A man waiting on a tent that has not answered is in one of two situations, and nothing tells them apart.

Three words the argument needs

When is the matter settled?

Somewhere in the night the matter gets settled, without everyone having been heard. To say when, the argument needs three words.

A standing

How the night stands, entire

What each of the four men holds in his head, together with every raven aloft and not yet taken in.

An arrival

One man takes in one raven

It is the only kind of thing that happens. Going to the perch and finding nothing counts as a turn, and changes nothing.

Open or closed

Does the order of arrivals still matter?

A standing is open if the night can still end in holding, and can still end in attacking. It is closed if only one answer remains.

Separate folds run them in either order

Each line is one tent, at one moment after another, and each ring further out is later. A raven is an arc from one tent to another. A hollow dot is a man who has not committed.

Tents 1 and 2 have ravens to trade, and so have tents 3 and 4. Which pair goes first?

That is the first tool, and the dullest fact here: two runs of arrivals with no man in common do not interfere. An arrival belongs to one man and to the air, and to nobody else.

Proof · courses with no man in common commute

In the paper's terms a standing is a configuration, an arrival an event, a course a schedule, a fair course an admissible run, and open and closed are bivalent and univalent. Lemma 1. Let courses σ₁ and σ₂ both be applicable at a standing, and let the set A of men who act in σ₁ and the set B of men who act in σ₂ be disjoint. Then each can be run after the other, and both orders reach the same standing.

  1. A standing is what is in each man's head together with the ravens aloft. An arrival changes two parts of it: the one man who takes the raven in, and the air — one raven removed, perhaps others added.
  2. Let σ₁ touch a set of men A and σ₂ a set B, disjoint. Running σ₁ alters only men in A, which σ₂ never reads; and conversely.
  3. Neither disables the other through the air: ravens are addressed, so those σ₂ consumes are addressed into B, and σ₁ removes only ravens addressed into A. Both orders are therefore applicable.
  4. In either order, the men of A have taken exactly the arrivals of σ₁ and those of B exactly the arrivals of σ₂, each in its own order, and the air has had the same ravens removed and added. So the standings reached are the same.∎
The night can begin open

The dusk that settles nothing

The sightings at dusk do not always settle the night. Line up some possible beginnings so that each differs from the next in one man's sighting, with all-hold at one end and all-attack at the other.

Suppose every beginning were already settled by the sightings alone. The second demand forces the two ends opposite ways. You settle the three in between: click a beginning to change it.

A row of beginnings click the middle three

Nobody died to make that argument work. The other three could not tell a silent man from a slow one, and so could not tell two different beginnings apart. However the four arrange their affairs, the sightings can fall so that the night begins open.

Proof · some beginning is open

In the paper's terms a standing is a configuration, an arrival an event, a course a schedule, a fair course an admissible run, and open and closed are bivalent and univalent. A beginning is an initial configuration: the four sightings, with no raven yet aloft. Lemma 2. Whatever the four have arranged, some beginning is open.

  1. Suppose every beginning were closed, each already fated by the sightings alone.
  2. Both fates occur: the second demand forces holding where all four saw hold, and attacking where all four saw attack.
  3. Call two beginnings neighbours when they differ in one man's sighting. Walking from a hold-fated beginning to an attack-fated one by single changes, there must be neighbours B₀ and B₁ fated to hold and to attack, differing only in the sighting of one man p.
  4. Take a fair course from B₀ in which p never stirs; this is permitted, since at most one man may be silent. Being fair, it ends in holding.
  5. Run the same course from B₁. Since p never acts and his sighting is the only difference, every other man sees exactly the same arrivals in the same order, so the course ends in holding here too.
  6. But B₁ was fated to attack, and a fated beginning admits no course to the other answer. Hence some beginning is open.∎
No arrival has to close it

The raven that would settle it can always be held back

An open night cannot stay open for ever, surely? Some arrival, sooner or later, must be the one that settles it. No arrival has to be. Scroll slowly: the picture moves with you.

1 · An open night

One raven is about to land

The night stands open: it can still end either way. One raven is waiting to be taken in. The birds are reliable, so it will land. Must it settle anything when it does?

2 · Hold it back

Other arrivals can come first

The top row is the night going on while that raven waits, one arrival at a time. Under each standing is the standing once the raven lands there.

3 · Suppose

Suppose its landing always settled the night

Then every standing in the bottom row is closed, for holding or for attacking. Both must appear, because the night at the top could still end either way.

4 · Neighbours

Somewhere, two neighbours split

Two standings in the top row are one arrival apart. Land the raven at the first and the night is settled for holding. Land it at the second and the night is settled for attacking.

5 · Another tent's arrival

Then the order cannot matter

If that one arrival is another man's business, it and the raven are separate folds: either order reaches the same standing. So one more arrival leads from a night settled for holding to a night settled for attacking. That cannot be.

6 · The same tent's arrival

Then let that man go quiet

If both arrivals are one man's business, let him go quiet, as a man does whose birds are in the pines. The other three must finish without him. From where they finish, the raven alone still leads to holding, while his other arrival and then the raven still leads to attacking. A finished night would still be open. That cannot be either.

7 · So

The raven lands, and the night is open still

The supposition fails. Somewhere in the bottom row a standing is open. No single raven is ever the one that has to settle the matter.

Proof · an arrival can always be deferred and the night left open

In the paper's terms a standing is a configuration, an arrival an event, a course a schedule, a fair course an admissible run, and open and closed are bivalent and univalent. Lemma 3. Let C be an open standing and e an arrival applicable at C. Let 𝒞 be the set of standings reachable from C without allowing e, and 𝒟 the set got by allowing e at each standing of 𝒞. Then 𝒟 contains an open standing.

  1. Let C be open and e an arrival applicable there. Write 𝒞 for the standings reachable from C without allowing e, and 𝒟 for those got by allowing e at each standing of 𝒞. Suppose no member of 𝒟 is open.
  2. Both settlements occur in 𝒟. C is open, so some course from it ends in holding and another in attacking. If such a course never allows e, its standings lie in 𝒞 and allowing e at the end gives a member of 𝒟 settled the same way; if it does allow e, the standing just after is already in 𝒟.
  3. So 𝒞 holds neighbours that split. 𝒞 is connected, so walking from the standing whose image is settled for holding to the one whose image is settled for attacking gives C₀ and C₁ = f(C₀), one arrival apart, whose images D₀ and D₁ are settled opposite ways.
  4. Case one: f is another man's arrival. By the lemma of separate folds e and f commute, so D₁ = f(D₀). But everything reachable from the hold-settled D₀ is settled for holding, and D₁ is settled for attacking.
  5. Case two: f is the same man's arrival, say p's. Take a course σ from C₀ in which p never stirs and which does finish; permitted, since p may be the one silent man. Call the standing it reaches A.
  6. σ has no man in common with e or f, so by the lemma it may be run from D₀ and from D₁ as well, reaching e(A) and f then e applied to A — settled for holding and for attacking respectively. So A is open, though σ finished there.
  7. Both cases are impossible, so 𝒟 holds an open standing.∎
Two facts, used without rest

A fair night in which nothing is ever settled

The night can begin open, and no single arrival need close it. Keep the four men in a queue, and let whoever orders the arrivals use both facts for ever.

The man at the head of the queue takes in the raven longest aloft for him, or walks to his perch and back if none is waiting. Then he goes to the back. The stretch before each arrival is chosen so that the night is open after it.

No plan guarantees that anybody ever commits

Nobody dies in it. What limits the four is the possibility of a death, which looks exactly like a slow bird.

Nothing goes wrong. Nobody commits to a wrong answer, because nobody commits at all.

One would have been enough. The demand was only that somebody commit, and even that fails.

The hill has nothing to do with it. Any number of men from two upward, any two answers: the same conclusion.

Three ways out

What would have to change

The slowness of the birds is not the trouble, nor their disorder. The trouble is two things together: no longest flight, and a dead man looking exactly like a slow bird.

One

A longest flight

Give the birds a longest flight, and enough silence means something.

Two

Some report of who has died

One that in the end names every dead man, and in the end stops naming at least one living one. It is enough while more than half live.

Three

Every death before dusk

If no man can die once the evening has begun, a majority can manage.

None of the three is on offer at the foot of the hill. The first is on offer at the top, where the bandits' rats have a longest run.*

Back to machines

The camp was a group of machines

Every tent is a process, and every raven a message. Hover or tap to see who is who.

At the foot of the hill
In the machine room

When messages may take any time and even one machine may stop, no protocol can guarantee that the group ever decides.

Agreement itself is never broken. What cannot be promised is an end to the waiting.

Practical protocols live with this by changing the rules a little: delays that are bounded in the end, an imperfect report of who has failed, or a coin.

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Michael J. Fischer, Nancy A. Lynch and Michael S. Paterson, “Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process,” Journal of the ACM 32, 2 (April 1985), 374–382.

The ideas. No deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system in which even one process may crash. The proof turns on bivalence: a configuration from which both decisions are still reachable. There is always a bivalent initial configuration, and from any bivalent configuration the step that would resolve the indecision can be deferred and then taken without resolving it — so a scheduler can build an admissible run that never decides. Three details carry the weight: the run the proof constructs contains no failure at all, the single permitted fault being spent on the argument; agreement and validity are never violated, only termination is lost; and the result bites even though the requirement is merely that some process decide. The obstruction is not slowness but the inability to distinguish a crashed process from a slow one. Relax it — bound the delays, even only eventually, or supply an imperfect oracle about who has failed — and consensus becomes solvable again; the paper's own §4 relaxes it by letting processes die only before the protocol begins, and gives a protocol that works whenever a majority are sound.
