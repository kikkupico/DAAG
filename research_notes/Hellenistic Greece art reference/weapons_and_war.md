# Hellenistic Greek weapons, armour and warfare (323-31 BC): visual reference

Note on method: sources are Wikipedia article summaries (secondary) and Wikimedia Commons API metadata. Sekunda, Marsden, Polybius, Diodorus and Philo were not read directly. Items I know from background knowledge but could not source are listed under Gaps, marked "unverified".

## Macedonian phalanx and infantry kit (sarissa, shield, helmets, armour)

### Takeaway
The phalangite carried a 5-7 m ash or cornel sarissa (about 4 kg), a small neck-slung shield, a Phrygian-type helmet, linen or bronze body armour and a kausia hat. Total kit was lighter than a classical hoplite's.

### Cited Findings
- Sarissa about 5-7 m (16-23 ft). Theophrastus gives 12 cubits (about 5.8 m); Polybius gives 14-16 cubits (about 6.3-7.3 m) for later Hellenistic armies. Shaft of ash (lighter, flexible) or cornel (stronger). Metal head and metal butt-spike. Head size is debated (13.5-51 cm estimates). — [Wikipedia: Sarissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa)
- Peter Connolly's 5.8 m cherry-wood reconstruction weighed 4.05 kg (9 lb). Older estimates were 5.5-6.5 kg. — [Wikipedia: Sarissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa)
- DEBATED: the two-section joined shaft with a metal sleeve is "largely abandoned" as impractical. The Vergina leaf-shaped iron piece (Andronikos) may be a sarissa head or a ceremonial butt. — [Wikipedia: Sarissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa)
- The shield (pelte, called "telamon" in the source) is about 24 inches across, bronze-plated wood, hung from a neck strap so both hands hold the pike. Whole kit about 40 lb, roughly 10 lb lighter than a hoplite's. The xiphos is the backup sword, and the kausia hat appears in tomb frescoes. Formation of 16x16 (256 men); rear ranks angle pikes at about 45 degrees. — [Wikipedia: Macedonian phalanx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_phalanx)
- Phrygian (Thracian) helmet: bronze skull from one sheet, tall forward-leaning apex (sometimes a separate riveted piece), large cheekpieces sometimes modelled as beards. It replaced the Corinthian from the 5th c. onward. Philip II's cavalry used it, and it appears on the Alexander Sarcophagus. Modern type names are archaeologists' labels. — [Wikipedia: Phrygian helmet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrygian_helmet)
- Boeotian helmet: open-faced, domed skull with a wide down-sloping brim, shaped like a folded sun hat. Mainly cavalry. Xenophon recommended it for horsemen. Made from a single bronze sheet over a limestone former. Variants appear on the coins of Eucratides I and Hermaeus (Greco-Bactrian). — [Wikipedia: Boeotian helmet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeotian_helmet)
- Linothorax: layered linen cuirass with a shoulder piece and pteruges (strip flaps) at the hem. In art from about 575 BC, still in use in the Hellenistic period. Alexander is reported to have worn a "double linen breastplate". — [Wikipedia: Linothorax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linothorax)
- DEBATED: construction (quilted, twined weave, or metal-backed). The glued-linen idea (Connolly, tested by Aldrete) rests on a "misremembered, twice-translated" source. — [Wikipedia: Linothorax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linothorax)
- Hypaspists carried hoplite kit (large concave shield, spear, linothorax, sword) and guarded the phalanx flanks. In the successor kingdoms they became elite or royal guard units. — [Wikipedia: Hypaspists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypaspists)
- Hypaspists/Argyraspids: "Royal Cohort" of Antiochus III. — [Wikipedia: Hellenistic armies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_armies)

### Inferences
- A phalangite silhouette: tall pike held two-handed, small round shield on the left forearm/neck strap, hat or Phrygian helmet, linen cuirass or none, short sword at the hip. Rear ranks hold pikes tilted high.
- Helmet types (Phrygian, Boeotian, Thracian) are modern labels, so mixed kit in a single unit is plausible for art.

### Gaps
- Greaves, muscle cuirass, Attic/Chalcidian helmet specifics: not found in sources fetched. Unverified background: muscle cuirasses are mostly officers and kings and survive in Hellenistic art. Chalcidian is a 5th-4th c. type with fading use. Check the Met and British Museum collections.
- Chalkaspides (bronze-shield units) and details of the Argyraspids' silver shields: not retrieved.

## Light and medium troops, cavalry, elephants, sidearms

### Takeaway
The thureophoros (oval Celtic-derived shield, spear, javelins, sword) is the standard medium infantryman of 3rd-1st c. BC Greece. Cretan archers (composite bow) and Rhodian slingers (lead glandes) are the standard missile specialists. Cataphracts, Boeotian-helmeted cavalry and turreted elephants fill out field armies.

### Cited Findings
- Thureophoroi: large oval thureos shield with a metal strip boss and central spine, long thrusting spear, javelins, sword, iron or bronze "Macedonian" helmet. Common in the 3rd-1st c. BC. — [Wikipedia: Thureophoros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thureophoros)
- The thureos was probably adapted from a Celtic shield after the Gallic invasion of Greece (279 BC). A rival theory is the Italian scutum after Pyrrhus. The Aetolian and Achaean Leagues and Boeotia adopted it in the 270s BC. The Achaeans dropped it about 208-207 BC for the phalanx. — [Wikipedia: Thureophoros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thureophoros)
- Thorakitai are heavier thureophoroi in mail. A 3rd-c. BC fresco shows a Macedonian in chainmail with a thureos. — [Wikipedia: Thureophoros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thureophoros)
- Medium infantry link phalanx and light troops. Peltasts are light skirmishers. Cavalry range from cataphracts to light skirmishers, including Tarantine cavalry and mounted archers. — [Wikipedia: Hellenistic armies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_armies)
- Cretan archers: composite bow (wood core, sinew and horn), some in linothorax, leather or helmets, with small shield and sword. — [Wikipedia: Cretan archers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cretan_archers)
- Slingers: lead glandes ("acorns"), almond-shaped, often inscribed (e.g. "DEXAI", "take that"). Rhodian slingers outranged Persian stone slingers (Xenophon, 401 BC, Classical). Acarnanians were also noted slingers. — [Wikipedia: Sling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sling_(weapon))
- Cataphract: horse and rider covered in scale or lamellar over mail, 4 m kontos lance, about 1,300 plates, about 40 kg. NOTE: the article is mostly about Parthian and later cavalry, so treat the numbers as not specifically Hellenistic. — [Wikipedia: Cataphract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataphract)
- Kopis: forward-curving single-edged blade, early up to 65 cm and later Macedonian about 48 cm. Xenophon (Classical) recommends it for cavalry. The kopis/machaira distinction is unclear in ancient texts. Xiphos is the straight double-edged sword. — [Wikipedia: Kopis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kopis)
- Elephants: Seleucids acquired 500 Indian elephants (305-303 BC) and used them at Ipsus 301 BC. Ptolemy IV's smaller African elephants carried turrets at Raphia 217 BC. Polyperchon used 60 in 318 BC. Elephants carried howdahs and armour and stood in the centre of the line. — [Wikipedia: War elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_elephant)

### Inferences
- Thureophoros silhouette: tall oval shield with a vertical spine, Phrygian-type helmet, spear and javelins, tunic with or without mail (thorakites).
- Gaul/Galatian mercenary and Thracian looks can borrow the Celtic oval shield and Phrygian helmet. Detail needs other sources.

### Gaps
- Prodromoi, Companion cavalry kit, Thracian rhomphaia, Galatian torc and nudity, doru specifics, javelin types (akontion): not retrieved. Unverified background: the Thracian rhomphaia is a long curved-blade polearm, and Galatians are shown in art with torcs, long moustaches and sometimes nude. Verify via Pergamene sculpture (Dying Gaul, Ludovisi Gaul) and Sekunda.
- Cataphract details specific to Seleucids (Magnesia 190 BC): not found.

## Siege warfare, artillery and fortifications

### Takeaway
Hellenistic besiegers used huge wheeled towers (helepolis), rams and torsion artillery. Defenders answered with counter-mines, ditches, mud and fire. Walls were low but strong, with towers carrying artillery.

### Cited Findings
- Helepolis at Rhodes: reported at about 40 m (130 ft) high, 20 m wide, 160 short tons, eight wheels about 15 ft high, 3,400 men in relays to move it, 200 on a capstan. It carried catapults of different sizes (180-lb down to 30-lb) and dart throwers on upper floors. Iron plating was damaged by the Rhodians, who flooded the approach with mud and sewage. The Rhodians then used the abandoned equipment to fund the Colossus. Figures are ancient (Diodorus) numbers, not verified. — [Wikipedia: Helepolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helepolis)
- Another source gives 125 ft tall by 60 ft wide, and a ram needing 1,000 operators. — [Wikipedia: Demetrius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demetrius_I_of_Macedon). Dimensions conflict between the two articles, so give ranges.
- Rhodes 305-304 BC: Demetrius had about 40,000 troops, 200 warships and 150 auxiliaries. The Rhodians had about 6,000 citizens, 1,000 resident foreigners and promised-freedom slaves. They used counter-mines, ditches and fire ships. Demetrius failed to take the harbour. Peace ended the year-long siege. — [Wikipedia: Siege of Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Rhodes_(305%E2%80%93304_BC))
- Gastraphetes ("belly-releaser"): composite-bow crossbow, spanned by pressing the stock against the belly. Heron's Belopoeica (via Ctesibius) describes it. No archaeological remains. Reconstructions are in Athens' Museum of Ancient Greek Technology. DEBATED: the invention date (before 399 BC). — [Wikipedia: Gastraphetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastraphetes)
- Torsion artillery: earliest evidence is Athenian inventories about 338-326 BC. Early machines have a square wooden frame with sinew skeins twisted around wooden arms. Later: metal washers, combined spring frames, padded heel blocks, ratchet triggers. Skeins of deer-foot or ox-neck sinew in olive oil (Vitruvius and Heron debated hair versus sinew). — [Wikipedia: Torsion siege engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_siege_engine)
- Fortifications: walls "relatively low, but powerful", ashlar blocks or mud brick. Towers began as single-storey squares, then became separate and round or polygonal. — [Wikipedia: Hellenistic fortifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_fortifications)
- Philo of Byzantium (c. 280-220 BC, Alexandria) wrote the military sections on artillery and fortification. — [Wikipedia: Philo of Byzantium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo_of_Byzantium)
- Demetrius also fortified Athens, Sicyon and Corinth. — [Wikipedia: Demetrius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demetrius_I_of_Macedon)

### Inferences
- A lone town wall for an artist: ashlar courses, square or polygonal towers with upper-storey artillery windows, no great height.

### Gaps
- Not found: euthytonon versus palintonon descriptions (Wikipedia page missing), oxybeles and lithobolos specifics (ranges, ball weights), siege ramps, mines, tortoise (chelone), embrasure shapes, Philo's wall rules. Unverified background: euthytonon is a straight-pull bow-form engine, palintonon a stone-thrower with two torsion springs; Marsden and Philo Belopoeica should supply formulae.

## Naval warfare

### Takeaway
Hellenistic fleets ran from light lembi and hemioliai to quinqueremes and a few giant showpiece ships. Rams and marines on deck decided fights.

### Cited Findings
- Quinquereme: about 45 m, about 100 tonnes, about 420 crew (300 rowers, 70-120 marines), attributed to Dionysius I about 399 BC. Probable arrangement is multiple men per oar. DEBATED. — [Wikipedia: Polyreme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyreme)
- Tessarakonteres of Ptolemy IV: 130 m, 4,000 rowers, 2,850 marines, mostly for show. — [Wikipedia: Polyreme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyreme)
- Hemiolia: light, fast, favoured by pirates. Rhodes favoured the trihemiolia. — [Wikipedia: Polyreme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyreme)
- Lembus: small, low-sided, fast, about 50 troops plus rowers. Illyrians (Agron, Teuta) used it for piracy, and Philip V had 100 built about 216 BC, later enlarged into rammed biremes. — [Wikipedia: Lembus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lembus)
- Demetrius beat Ptolemy at Salamis in 306 BC. — [Wikipedia: Demetrius I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demetrius_I_of_Macedon)

### Gaps
- Rams in detail, Rhodian marines, Cretan pirates, the Salamis "sevens", ship-mounted artillery: not retrieved. The Wikipedia Greek-pirates pages 404'd.

## Local militias, garrisons, mercenaries, pirates in small cities

### Takeaway
No source in this pass covers town guards or watchmen specifically. Only the general model is supported: citizen levies, hired Cretan and Thracian troops, and thureos-armed leagues.

### Cited Findings
- Rhodes 305 BC: 6,000 citizens, 1,000 foreigners and some freed slaves defended the city. — [Wikipedia: Siege of Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Rhodes_(305%E2%80%93304_BC))
- Leagues (Aetolian, Achaean, Boeotian) used thureophoroi in the 270s BC. — [Wikipedia: Thureophoros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thureophoros)

### Inferences
- Small-town guards plausibly look like thureophoroi or light javelin troops (tunic, thureos, spear, Phrygian-type helmet), with Cretan archers as hired professionals. Not directly sourced.

### Gaps
- Watchmen, ephebes' kit, pirate dress, bandit gear: nothing found. Check Aeneas Tacticus, Ager, Chaniotis, and Wikipedia on ephebes.
