Spreading by Word of Mouth

Can an update reach every machine in a large system simply by each machine passing it on to a few others at random, with no central coordinator in charge?

We find out by exploring how news spread among the scholars of Paxos by word of mouth in the colonnade, and how their yearly festival caught whatever the gossip missed.

After Alan Demers, Mark Gealy, Dan Greene, Carl Hauser, Wes Irish, John Larson, Sue Manning, Scott Shenker, Howard Sturgis, Dan Swinehart, Doug Terry and Don Woods, Epidemic Algorithms for Replicated Database Maintenance, 1987

First, in machine terms

One update, many replicas

Suppose a database is copied across hundreds of sites. When an update is made at one site, how can every other site come to hold it without any central coordinator keeping track of who has heard?

  • Direct mail sends the update straight to every other site. It is fast when it works, but messages get dropped and the sender may not even have a complete list of every site on the network.
  • Anti-entropy has each site periodically pick another site at random and compare their entire databases, copying over whatever is newer. It is guaranteed to reach everyone eventually, though comparing whole databases is slow and heavy.
  • Rumor mongering (Demers et al., 1987) spreads a fresh update like gossip: a site that learns of a new update tells randomly chosen peers until too many of them already know it, at which point it loses interest. It is fast and light, leaving only a tiny fraction of sites untold—which infrequent anti-entropy then sweeps up.

We tell this story along the scholars' coast of Paxos, with its scattered libraries, the scholars who talk in its colonnade, and the copyists who meet once a year at its festival.

The scholars' coast

Many libraries, no one in charge

Below the fork of Paxos, the island widens into the scholars' coast—a string of small libraries among the olive groves, each keeping its own copies of the same works. Nothing here waits for a vote in the Chamber or the Tholos: a librarian answers readers straight from the shelf, and everyone comes to agree in the end by comparing and merging rather than by assembly.*

What has to travel

One scrap, many shelves

Each library's copy of a work is called an edition. Every edition carries a tally: the day and hour by the sun when it was written, with the number of the library that wrote it added to break ties.

What travels from library to library is brief: a single entry under a title, written on a scrap of papyrus. Suppose Demerios, working at his own library, revises The Order of the Heavens to state that the Earth goes round the Sun. Because he writes it today, his new edition carries a tally larger than any earlier copy of that work on the coast.

Writing a new edition at one's own library is the only change a scholar makes directly. Every other change to a library's shelves happens when it receives an edition from another library and compares the tallies. If the incoming edition has a larger tally than the one on the shelf, it replaces it; if its tally is smaller, it is ignored. That single rule guarantees that any two libraries comparing a work will immediately agree on which edition to keep.

The whole question is how Demerios's scrap gets from his shelf to every other shelf on the coast—how quickly each habit spreads it, how much walking and carrying it costs, and who, if anyone, gets left behind.

The simplest habit

Letters to everyone

The most obvious way to spread a new edition is direct mail: whenever a scholar writes a new edition, he copies it onto a slip for every other library on the coast and dispatches a courier to each.

While the coast had only a handful of libraries, sending letters to everyone seemed good enough. As the coast grew to hundreds of libraries, three flaws made it unworkable on its own.

What goes wrong

A courier is lost

A courier may be delayed, turned back by a closed door, or drop his slip in a ditch. When that happens, the destination library never hears of the new edition.

What goes wrong

A library is not on the list

Because no one is in charge of the coast, Demerios's list of libraries may be incomplete. A newly founded library that is not yet on his list receives no courier at all.

What goes wrong

One library does all the work

Every single edition forces the library that wrote it to copy and send out hundreds of slips at once, crowding the roads around it.

Worst of all, when a slip goes astray, nobody notices. The library that missed the letter has no way of knowing a letter was ever sent, and Demerios's library has no record that it failed to arrive. On a coast of hundreds of libraries, no one can track down missing slips by hand.

The habit that misses no one

The festival

To make sure no edition is lost for good, the copyists of the coast gather once a year on the festival ground, where rows of long stone tables stand below the stoa.

There, each copyist draws by lot the name of one other library, walks over to its copyist, and sits down across a stone table with their entire collections unrolled between them. Because every choice is by lot, some copyists are drawn two or three times and some are not drawn at all, though every copyist initiates one table.

Side by side, the pair compare their scrolls work by work. Whenever they hold different editions of the same work, the copyist with the smaller tally replaces it with the larger—depending on which of three rules the festival follows.

Three ways to sit

Giving

The copyist who drew the lot gives (`push`) any newer editions he holds to the partner he chose, and takes nothing back for himself.

Three ways to sit

Asking

The copyist who drew the lot asks (`pull`) his chosen partner for any newer editions that partner holds, without handing over his own.

Three ways to sit

Both

Each copyist both gives and asks (`push-pull`), so when they rise from the table, both hold the newer edition of every work, regardless of who brought it.

No master list of deliveries is needed—and because the number of informed libraries roughly doubles each festival at first, doubling the size of the coast adds only a single extra festival or two to the wait.

The last few libraries

When few remain, asking beats giving

Notice what happens near the end of the spread, when almost every library on the coast already has the new edition and only a few stragglers still lack it.

Suppose nine out of ten libraries already have the edition, and one in ten (`0.10`) still lacks it. Under asking (`pull`), each straggler actively draws a partner and asks. It stays uninformed only if the partner it draws also happens to be one of the few stragglers—which happens with probability `0.10 × 0.10 = 0.01`, and at the next festival `0.01 × 0.01 = 0.0001`. Each festival squares the fraction of libraries still missing the news!

Under giving (`push`), by contrast, the stragglers sit passively waiting for one of the informed copyists to draw their name by lot. Even when nearly everyone is informed, each straggler has roughly a `1/e` (about `37%`) chance of not being picked by anyone in a given festival. So giving shrinks the remaining fraction by only a constant factor each year, while asking wipes out the tail in a couple of steps. Doing both (`push-pull`) gets the fast early growth of giving and the quadratic finish of asking.

Proof · that pull squares the susceptible fraction and push only scales it

Let pi be the probability that a site is still susceptible (has not received the update) after the ith cycle, n the number of sites, with partners chosen uniformly. Claim. With pull, pi+1 = pi²; with push, pi+1 = pi(1 − 1/n)n(1−pi).

  1. This is the paper's deterministic model of §1.3. Let pi be the probability that a site is still susceptible after the ith cycle, n the number of sites, and partners be chosen uniformly.
  2. Pull. A site is susceptible after cycle i + 1 exactly when it was susceptible after cycle i and the partner it contacted was also susceptible. The partner is susceptible with probability pi, independently, so pi+1 = pi².
  3. Push. A site is susceptible after cycle i + 1 exactly when it was susceptible after cycle i and none of the infective sites chose it. There are n(1 − pi) infective sites, each choosing it with probability 1/n, so pi+1 = pi(1 − 1/n)n(1−pi).
  4. For large n the last factor is close to e−(1−pi), and for small pi close to e−1. Push therefore multiplies a small pi by about 1/e each cycle, a geometric decrease, while pull squares it, which falls faster than any geometric decrease once pi is small.
  5. Push-pull makes a site informed if either holds, so it does at least as well as pull; the paper reports that it behaves essentially like pull.∎

The festival is completely reliable, but it has one glaring cost: at every table, the two copyists read through their entire collections from first scroll to last, even when almost every work already agrees. Nearly all their effort is spent verifying that nothing needs changing.

A cheaper table

Comparing sums first

To avoid reading thousands of identical scrolls, each library keeps a catalogue sum beside its catalogue: a single compact number (a checksum) computed from all its editions and tallies.

Whenever a library replaces an old edition with a newer one, it updates its catalogue sum on the spot. When two copyists sit down at the festival, they first compare their two catalogue sums. If the sums match, their collections are identical and they can rise from the table at once.

On an active coast, however, the two sums almost never match right away! Any edition written in the last few days has reached some libraries and not others, so two copyists drawn by lot will almost always differ on at least one recent work—forcing them right back into a full reading of the catalogue unless they clear away recent differences first.

The remedy

The recent list

Each library keeps a separate recent list of all editions whose tallies fall within a chosen recent time window (say, the past week). When two copyists sit down, they first exchange their recent lists, updating their shelves and catalogue sums, and only then compare their catalogue sums. Now the sums almost always match.

Its price

The span must be long enough

The time window of the recent list must be longer than the time an edition needs to cross the entire coast. If the coast grows until editions take longer than that window to finish spreading, old differences fall off the recent list before everyone has them, the catalogue sums disagree every time, and the copyists are forced back to reading their whole collections.

With no span

Newest first

To avoid picking a fixed time window at all, each library can keep an index of its catalogue ordered from newest tally to oldest. The two copyists compare editions newest first—what the paper calls peel back—updating their sums as they go and stopping the instant the two catalogue sums agree.

Between festivals

Word of mouth

Even with catalogue sums, the festival is a heavy undertaking held only from time to time. Between festivals, news travels much faster and lighter by word of mouth in the stoa—the long colonnade beside the festival ground where scholars from every library stroll and talk.

Whenever a scholar writes a new edition, or hears of one for the first time, he copies that scrap onto a small wax tablet he carries under his arm—his hot tablet. Each day in the stoa, he picks another scholar at random and shares the scraps on his hot tablet. Nobody coordinates who speaks to whom, and nobody keeps a list of which libraries have heard.

With respect to one scrap

Has not heard

The scholar's library does not hold this edition yet (in the paper's epidemic terms, susceptible).

With respect to one scrap

Still telling

The scrap is on the scholar's hot tablet (infective), and he tells it to the scholars he meets. Anyone who hears it for the first time puts it on their own hot tablet and starts telling it too.

With respect to one scrap

Has stopped

The edition sits on the library's shelf for readers to consult, but the scholar has wiped it off his hot tablet and no longer tells it in the stoa (removed).

Why a scholar stops

Losing interest

If scholars never wiped a scrap off their hot tablets, the stoa would soon drown in old news. A scholar needs a local rule for when a piece of news has grown stale—and the natural cue is meeting people who already know it.

Each scholar has an allowance k that governs his patience. When a scrap is brand new, almost everyone he meets in the stoa has not heard it yet, so the number of tellers multiplies rapidly. Once most of the coast knows the scrap, however, a teller keeps running into listeners who say, I already have that on my shelf, and he loses interest and wipes it from his hot tablet.

The paper compares two pairs of choices for how a scholar loses interest:

First, attentive (`feedback`) versus blind: an attentive scholar checks whether his listener already knew the scrap and only loses interest when the telling was wasted on someone who already knew; a blind scholar does not wait for a reply and may lose interest after any telling, even one that informed someone new.

Second, toss (`coin`) versus count (`counter`): with a toss, each wasted (or blind) telling has a `1` in k chance of making the scholar stop; with a count, the scholar stops after exactly k wasted tellings. (If an attentive counting scholar tells someone who needed the news, he resets his wasted-telling count back to zero.)

When the last teller stops

Who never hears

Word of mouth runs until the last scholar wipes the scrap from his hot tablet. At that moment the rumor dies out completely—and any library that has not heard by then will never hear by gossip alone. Those left-out libraries are the residue.

The paper tracks three quantities that trade off against one another: the residue (the fraction of libraries that never hear), the average number of tellings per library (the communication traffic), and the delay (both the average number of days until a library hears, and the day the very last library hears).

The paper's measures, on a thousand libraries

Counting, attentive

An allowance of `1` leaves about `18%` of libraries without the news, at `1.7` tellings per library. An allowance of `3` leaves only `1%` unheard at `4.5` tellings. An allowance of `5` leaves roughly `1 in 1,000` unheard at `6.7` tellings.

The paper's measures

A larger allowance, a smaller residue

With an attentive scholar who gives up on a toss, an allowance of `1` leaves about `20%` of a large coast unheard, and `2` leaves about `6%`. Increasing the allowance drives the residue down exponentially, though no finite allowance ever makes it strictly zero.

The paper's measures

Delay changes little

Raising the allowance barely slows the spread, and counting is noticeably brisker than tossing: a library hears in `11–13` days on average with counting (`17` for the last to hear), compared with `14–19` days with tosses (and over `30` for the last).

Proof · that the fraction who never hear satisfies s = e−(k+1)(1−s)

Let s, i, r be the fractions of the population susceptible (have not heard), infective (still telling) and removed (have stopped), with s + i + r = 1, and let an infective site lose interest with probability 1/k each time it calls a site that is not susceptible. Claim. When the epidemic ends, s = e−(k+1)(1−s).

  1. This is the paper's model of §1.4. Let s, i, r be the fractions of the population susceptible, infective and removed, so that s + i + r = 1. A susceptible site becomes infective in proportion to si; an infective site becomes removed at rate (1/k)(1 − s) per unit of i, since it loses interest with probability 1/k when it calls a site that is not susceptible, which happens with probability 1 − s. Hence
    ds/dt = −si,   di/dt = si − (1/k)(1 − s)i.
  2. Dividing the second by the first removes t: di/ds = −1 + (1 − s)/(ks) = −(k + 1)/k + 1/(ks).
  3. Integrating, i(s) = −((k + 1)/k)s + (1/k) ln s + c.
  4. At the start one site in n is infective, so i(1 − ε) = ε with ε = 1/n. For large n, ε → 0, so i(1) = 0 and c = (k + 1)/k. Hence i(s) = ((k + 1)/k)(1 − s) + (1/k) ln s.
  5. The epidemic ends when i = 0, that is when ln s = −(k + 1)(1 − s), so the residue satisfies s = e−(k+1)(1−s). For k = 1 the solution is about 0.20 and for k = 2 about 0.06, as the paper states.∎
One price in every version

The residue is always about e−m, where m is the average tellings per library

Whether scholars are attentive or blind, and whether they stop by count or by toss, a striking law holds across every push variant: if the epidemic sends an average of m tellings per library (`nm` random tellings in total), the probability that a given library is never picked by any of those `nm` random tellings is `(1 − 1/n)^(nm) ≈ e^(−m)`. Thus `1.7` tellings per library always leaves about `e^(−1.7) ≈ 18%` unheard, and `6.7` tellings leaves about `e^(−6.7) ≈ 0.12%` unheard. Counting and attentiveness do not beat that traffic-to-residue curve; what they improve is the delay.

Argument · that s = e−m in every version of rumour mongering

Let n be the number of sites, s the residue, and m the average number of updates sent from a site, so that the epidemic sends nm updates in all, each to a site chosen uniformly. Claim. s = e−m. The paper says the argument is not fully proved.

  1. The paper gives this argument in §1.4 and says it is not fully proved. Let m be the average number of updates sent from a site, so the epidemic sends nm updates in all.
  2. Each update is addressed to a site chosen uniformly, so a given site is not chosen by any one with probability 1 − 1/n.
  3. If the updates were independent, the probability that a given site is chosen by none of them is (1 − 1/n)nm, which tends to e−m as n grows. This probability is the residue s.
  4. The step that is not proved is that m may be treated as a constant: it varies from site to site, and the relation holds only if its spread about the mean goes to zero as n → ∞. The authors observe this empirically and have not proved it.
  5. Table 1 agrees with the relation: m = 1.7 gives e−1.7 ≈ 0.18 and m = 3.3 gives ≈ 0.037.∎
Two ways to meet

Telling, asking and crowded days

Just as at the festival, word of mouth in the stoa can work by telling (`push`) or by asking (`pull`). A telling scholar approaches a random peer and offers what is on his hot tablet; an asking scholar approaches a random peer and asks, What is on your hot tablet?

Once again, asking beats telling dramatically in the tail—shrinking the residue to roughly e−Θ(m³) instead of e−m. With attentive counting scholars, an allowance of `1` leaves only `3%` unheard (at `2.7` tellings per library); an allowance of `2` leaves `6 in 10,000`; and an allowance of `3` leaves `4 in a million`.

With asking, every library asks one other each day, and a scholar who still has the scrap on the hot tablet hands it over. A scholar asked by several in a day counts the day as wasted only if none of them needed the scrap, and starts the count afresh if any did.

Why telling is used at all

A quiet coast

If asking is so much sharper, why ever use telling? Because when no new editions are being written, telling scholars have empty hot tablets and say nothing at all. Asking scholars must keep walking up to peers every day to ask What is new?—wasting breath whenever the coast is quiet. Asking suits a busy coast; telling suits a quiet one.

Doing both

Only the lower count is charged

When two scholars both tell and ask (`push-pull`) and discover that both already hold the scrap on their hot tablets, who should count the meeting as wasted? The paper's minimization rule charges only the scholar with the smaller wasted-telling count (or both if tied), keeping hot tablets alive longer and yielding the smallest residue of all.

Crowded days

Turned away, and hunting

Suppose a scholar can speak with at most a fixed number of peers per day and turns extra callers away. Under telling, being turned away actually helps, because the busy peer was probably already told anyway! Under asking, being turned away hurts, unless a refused scholar hunts for another peer who is still free.

In fact, if each scholar accepts only one partner per day and anyone turned away hunts until they find an available partner, the stoa pairs everyone off into one-to-one tables. Telling and asking become identical, and the residue drops almost to nothing.

Two habits, each covering the other

The festival behind the gossip

Word of mouth in the stoa is fast and cheap, but leaves a small residue of libraries untold. The festival is slow and thorough, and leaves no one behind. Put together, each cures the other's weakness.

Nearly all editions reach nearly all libraries within days by gossip in the stoa. Then, every so often, copyists sit down for a festival table (`anti-entropy`) and sweep up whatever few scraps the gossip missed—or heal the coast after a stopped courier cuts it in two and lets the gossip die out on one side.

When two copyists at a festival table discover that one of them holds an edition the other missed, they copy it across. Should the newly informed library also start spreading that edition again, in case others missed it too? The paper considers three options:

The cautious reply

Nothing more

Simply copy the missing edition onto the shelf and leave it off the hot tablet. The festival itself will eventually carry it to any other stragglers.

The bolder reply, by letters

Tell everyone again, by courier

This can explode in cost: if a network partition left half the coast without an edition, then at the next festival roughly half the copyists will discover the missing edition at once and each mail it to everyone—sending $O(n^2)$ letters!

The bolder reply, by word of mouth

Treat it as news again

Putting the rediscovered edition back on the hot tablet for word of mouth is always cheap: if almost everyone already has it, the teller quickly meets informed peers and drops it; if half the coast lacks it, gossip spreads it cleanly without flooding the roads.

A combination that cannot fail

Swap from the head of the list until the sums agree

Even better, a library can merge gossip and festival into a single habit. Instead of wiping old scraps from a hot tablet, each library keeps its editions in a list ordered by how recently each scrap proved useful: whenever a scrap is new to a listener, it moves to the front of the list; whenever the listener already knew it, it slips back. When two scholars meet, they exchange scraps from the front of their lists in small batches until their catalogue sums match.

Spreading an absence

Withdrawing a work

Suppose Demerios decides that an entire work is spurious and wants to withdraw (delete) it from the coast. Simply taking the scroll off his own shelf and burning it will not work!

Why? Because at the very next festival table, Demerios's copyist will sit down across from a copyist who still holds the old scroll. Seeing that Demerios's library has nothing under that title while the partner has an edition from day 3, the festival rule will dutifully copy the old scroll back onto Demerios's shelf! Every habit we have seen spreads what is present; an empty space on a shelf has no tally and cannot beat an existing edition.

To make an absence travel, the library must turn the deletion into a positive record: a notice of withdrawal (in the paper's terms, a death certificate). The notice names the withdrawn work and carries the tally of the moment it was withdrawn, just like a new edition. Now it spreads by word of mouth and at the festival, and wherever it meets an edition with a smaller tally, the edition is struck and replaced by the notice.

What notices cost

They pile up

If libraries kept every notice of withdrawal forever, their shelves would gradually fill up with tombstones for long-dead works. A library would love to throw a notice away once every library has seen it—but knowing that everyone has heard requires hearing from everyone, which a single closed library blocks.

The plain answer

A fixed span

Each library can discard a notice once its tally is older than a fixed span τ₁ (say, a month). The risk is that if a long-cut-off library comes back after a month and a day, its old edition will resurrect the deleted work across the whole coast.

A far longer span

Dormant notices

To get a much longer memory without filling every library's shelves, each notice names a few wardens (`retention sites`) chosen by lot. After the first span τ₁, ordinary libraries throw the notice away, while its wardens keep it asleep (`dormant`)—taking up shelf space at only those few libraries and generating zero festival traffic unless an old edition resurfaces.

What a woken notice carries

Two tallies

Suppose an old copy from day 3 finally reaches a warden on day 10. The warden wakes its sleeping notice from day 5 so that it can travel again and strike down the resurrected day-3 edition. How should the woken notice be dated?

If the warden simply stamped the woken notice with a brand-new tally of day 10, disaster could strike: what if someone had legitimately rewritten and restored the work on day 8? A notice with a tally of day 10 would wipe out the legitimate day-8 edition too!

To prevent that, a notice of withdrawal carries two tallies: its original withdrawal tally t (day 5), which never changes and decides which editions the notice is allowed to strike (u < t), and a waking tally a (`activation timestamp`), which is updated to day 10 when woken and decides only whether the notice is awake and actively spreading.

Proof · that reactivating by the activation timestamp cancels no later update

A death certificate carries an ordinary timestamp t and an activation timestamp a, equal when it is created. It is dormant once a is older than the threshold τ₁, and deleted once a is older than τ₁ + τ₂. Claim. Reactivation, which sets a to the current time, cancels no item whose timestamp u exceeds t.

  1. This is the argument of §2.2, set out. A death certificate carries an ordinary timestamp t and an activation timestamp a, equal when created.
  2. The rule of cancellation uses only t: a certificate cancels an item with timestamp u exactly when t > u. The rule of propagation uses only a: the certificate is dormant, and so not propagated by anti-entropy, once a is older than τ₁, and is deleted once a is older than τ₁ + τ₂.
  3. Suppose a site has a legitimate item with timestamp u > t, an update that reinstated the item after its deletion. The certificate has t < u, so it does not cancel it.
  4. Reactivating a certificate sets a to the current time and leaves t unchanged. The cancellation test is unchanged, so the reactivated certificate still fails to cancel any item with u > t, while the new a lets it propagate.
  5. By contrast, had reactivation set t to the current time, a certificate with t′ > u would cancel the reinstating update, which is incorrect.∎

Dormant notices act like an immune system's memory cells: for the same shelf space per library, a coast of n libraries can remember deletions roughly n times longer (years instead of weeks). Still, the first span τ₁ must be kept longer than the time an edition needs to cross the coast, which grows slowly (as $O(\log n)$) with the number of libraries.

A coast is not an even plain

Near partners and the ferry

Up to now, every scholar and copyist has picked partners uniformly at random from the entire coast. On a real coast, however, some crossings are bottlenecks.

Suppose most libraries lie along the main coast road, while a few lie across an inlet that can only be crossed by a single ferry. If every library picks partners uniformly at random, the few libraries across the inlet almost always pick a partner on the main coast—and the many libraries on the main coast frequently pick one across the inlet. Every one of those pairings has to ride the single ferry!

To spare the ferry, the paper has each library prefer nearer partners: each library ranks all the others from nearest to farthest, and picks the d-th nearest library with probability proportional to $1/d^2$. Because the probability depends on a library's rank d in the list rather than raw road miles, it adapts automatically where libraries are clustered tightly and where they are spread far apart.

How much nearer

Along one road

If scholars spoke only to their immediate next-door neighbours, the ferry would be spared, but news would creep down the coast one library per day ($O(n)$ delay). Picking the d-th neighbour with probability $1/d^2$ keeps the road and ferry traffic low ($O(\log n)$) while still spreading news in $O(\log n)$ time.

Safe for the festival

Every pair still has some chance

Because every library on the list still has a non-zero probability of being drawn, anti-entropy at the festival remains guaranteed to reach every library on the coast.

Not safe for the telling

Telling the neighbour again

For rumor mongering alone, favouring nearby libraries can backfire on uneven coasts: two libraries close to each other and far from the rest may repeatedly pick each other, exhaust their allowance k, and stop telling before the rumor ever jumps the gap!

Combining telling and asking (`push-pull`) with a slightly higher allowance makes spatial gossip much more resilient, and the festival behind the gossip catches anything that stalls at a bottleneck. When the authors deployed spatial epidemic algorithms on Xerox's real Clearinghouse network, cross-gateway traffic dropped sharply and replica convergence improved.

The limits

What it does not say

The coast's epidemic habits eventually bring every library the news without anyone in charge. Here are five things they do not promise.

Does news arrive within a fixed number of days?

No. Because every pairing is chosen by lot, there is no hard deadline by which every library is guaranteed to have heard. Once writing stops, the probability that any library is still behind falls exponentially with time—a trade the authors gladly accept for algorithms so simple and decentralized.

Does telling alone reach everyone?

No. Rumor mongering in the stoa always leaves a non-zero residue of libraries that never hear, and on awkward network topologies it can die out early. Only the festival (`anti-entropy`) behind the gossip guarantees that 100% of libraries eventually converge.

Does the coast ever agree at one instant?

Only if everyone stops writing new editions long enough for the last scraps to finish spreading. While scholars keep writing, some shelves are always slightly behind, and a reader may be handed an older edition. How likely that is, and how far behind, is the subject of the next book.*

What if two scholars edit the same work at once?

The larger tally wins (`last-writer-wins`), and the edition with the smaller tally is silently overwritten rather than merged. Moreover, because tallies come from each library's reading of the sun, if one library's clock runs fast its editions will beat later editions written elsewhere.

What is left open?

The authors note several open questions: how to prevent spatial distributions from stalling rumor mongering on arbitrary network graphs, whether fixed time thresholds on death certificates can be avoided without global snapshots, and whether organizing sites into a hierarchy would beat flat random pairing.

The paper behind this book

Alan Demers, Mark Gealy, Dan Greene, Carl Hauser, Wes Irish, John Larson, Sue Manning, Scott Shenker, Howard Sturgis, Dan Swinehart, Doug Terry and Don Woods, Epidemic Algorithms for Replicated Database Maintenance. Proceedings of the Sixth Annual ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), Vancouver, August 1987, 1–12; the version used here is Xerox PARC CSL-89-1, January 1989. — Direct mail, anti-entropy with checksums and peel back, rumor mongering, death certificates and spatial distributions, from the Clearinghouse servers of the Xerox Corporate Internet.

The ideas, plainly.

Demers and co-authors keep a database copied at many sites and ask how an update made at one site can reach all the others with very little help from the network and no one in charge. Each entry carries a timestamp and the larger timestamp wins, so any two sites that meet can settle which entry is newer. Mailing the update to everyone is quick but unreliable, and the sender may not know every site. Anti-entropy, in which each site regularly picks another at random and the two reconcile, always reaches every site, but slowly and at the cost of comparing whole databases; checksums and lists of recent updates make it cheap.

Rumor mongering passes an update on while it is still news: a site that has it tells others and loses interest after meeting enough who already knew. That is quick and cheap, but it can leave a fraction of sites who never hear, a fraction that shrinks exponentially with the patience allowed and is tied to the traffic by one relation in every version. Pull does much better than push when few sites are left. Anti-entropy run rarely behind it makes certain that nothing is missed.

Deletions have to be spread too, or an old copy brings the item back, so a deletion is a certificate with a timestamp; certificates cannot be kept for ever, so a few sites keep them asleep and wake them if an old copy turns up, with a second timestamp so that waking does not cancel newer updates. Choosing partners nearer first, with a chance that falls with the number of closer sites, spares the few critical links, at a small cost in delay; but it can defeat rumor mongering in awkward networks, which is another reason for the anti-entropy behind it.

Demerios stands for Alan Demers, the first author. The paper names twelve authors, Alan Demers, Mark Gealy, Dan Greene, Carl Hauser, Wes Irish, John Larson, Sue Manning, Scott Shenker, Howard Sturgis, Dan Swinehart, Doug Terry and Don Woods, and all are credited here. Only Demerios is named; the scholars of the stoa and the copyists of the festival are not told apart, since no one is in charge of either.
