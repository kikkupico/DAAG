# Reality checks

What each place, building, character, prop and practice of the setting has in common with the real Greek world, and where the setting departs from it. Every entry is shown on the setting page it belongs to (`places/<slug>`), under *Reality Checks*, and this file holds them all. The wider period reference they draw on is `hellenistic-reference.html`.

**What this is for.** The series is allegory, and an allegory is easier to trust when a reader can see what is borrowed and what is made up. Each entry names a real precedent in geography, history or literature and says how close it is. It does not defend the setting: where the setting is wrong for the period, it says so.

**The four verdicts.**

- **Close**: the real thing is much like it.
- **Loose**: borrowed and changed.
- **Invented**: the setting made it up (for the model's sake, or Lamport's).
- **Anachronism**: the real thing is of another age. Kept on purpose, because the model needs it.

**The dagger.** † marks a citation given from memory and not checked against the text. Everything without a dagger was checked against an online source while this file was written (Pausanias 1.44.6 on the Scironian road; Aristotle, *Constitution of Athens* 44 and 67; Herodotus 4.98 and 7.213; Xenophon, *Hellenica* 2.4.2 and *Anabasis* 1.3.21; Aelian and Pliny on Taurosthenes' pigeon; Polybius 10.43–47; Alcaeus fr. 129 and the Messon; Amorgos's three cities; the first hourglass, 1338; Lamport's Paxos and the Ionian Paxoi; the Tower of the Winds). Check a dagger before quoting it.

**How it differs from the leak asides.** A *Where the allegory leaks* note on a setting page says where the allegory departs from its model (rats are not real messengers, so the rats stand for private bounded messages). A reality check says what the real world offered, whatever the model needed.

**Rules for entries.** No measurements, even when a real site is named. Cast by number and by colour, as on the setting pages. Never a motive for a liar. Prose only: no symbols from a paper. A new setting, or a new device in an existing one, adds its entries here and on its page in the same pass (see `process.md`).

## Arche · the ring road

### Places

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| Arche, the island | The name is the Greek word *archē*, “beginning, first principle, rule”. For a round island with one great massif, the nearest real match is Samothrace, whose mountain Homer makes Poseidon's lookout over Troy (Iliad 13.12–14)†. The geometry itself is invented. | Loose |
| the ring road, one lane each way, no overtaking | The real cliff road of the Greek coast is the Scironian road from Megara to Corinth. In classical times it was a ledge fit for walkers and pack animals; the emperor Hadrian widened it so that vehicles could pass one another in opposite directions (Pausanias 1.44.6). A lane each way is a Roman standard, so a Hellenistic cliff road would be single track. | Loose |
| three harbour towns on terraces round a harbour | Hellenistic trading harbours such as Delos and Rhodes: terraced towns of courtyard houses, with warehouses and quays at the water†. | Close |

### Buildings

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| a house: one long range, lime-plastered rubble walls, a Doric portico along the road, a gabled tile roof | Plastered rubble walls (the “masonry style” of Delos) and a Doric colonnade facing a public way, as on the stoas of Athens and Pergamon†. The office, gate and storehouse under one roof is the setting's own arrangement. | Close |
| a portico of plain Doric columns along the road | Greek Doric columns had no base: the shaft stands directly on the top step of the platform, the stylobate†. | Close |
| a gabled tile roof of flat pan tiles under narrow cover tiles, with a decorated tile at each eave end | The Corinthian roof system: flat terracotta pan tiles, ridged cover tiles over the joints, and antefixes at the eave†. Sparta's Laconian roofs used curved tiles throughout†, so a curved tile is Greek too, but the flat and cover pair is the standard. The timbers beneath were hewn by adze and saw-pit, not milled†. | Close |
| port-town houses facing inward, blank street walls, a door and a few small high windows | The courtyard houses of Olynthus and Delos present blank walls to the street and open onto a court†. | Close |
| the board of orders at House 3's gate, numbered slots and a peg | The *klērotērion*, a stone slab with columns of slots into which citizens' bronze tokens were set, used in Athens to allot jurors and officials. It sorted by lot, not by arrival, so the board is a loose borrowing. | Loose |
| the customs seat | Greek harbours levied a tax on goods landed; Athens took a fiftieth at the Piraeus†. | Close |
| the shared storehouse cut into the rock beside the road | Greek rock-cut cisterns and store-rooms exist, but a store shared between rival trading houses is the setting's own device. | Invented |

### Characters

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| the three houses, known by number | Hellenistic commerce ran through individual merchants and bankers rather than numbered firms; the nearest paper trail is the estate archive of Zenon, a Ptolemaic manager of the third century BC†. Numbering the houses is a series rule, so that none has a character of its own. | Loose |
| the bookkeeper and the clerks | Clerks kept dated accounts of receipts and shipments; the Zenon papyri show the work†. The bookkeeper's Greek name follows the series practice of Hellenising the authors. | Close |
| messengers on foot, bare-headed, in an ochre *exōmis* and strapped sandals, a hide pouch | The *hemerodromoi*, “day-runners”, such as Pheidippides (Herodotus 6.105)†. The *exōmis* is one woven rectangle, belted, with one shoulder bare†; sandals were simple strapped soles†; a traveller's bag was a soft hide *pēra*†. The traveller's hat, the *petasos*, is Hermes' emblem, but the setting leaves it off: on a runner it reads as a modern brimmed hat. A pinched-crown hat, a sleeved tailored shirt, a buckled sandal and a stitched flap bag are all of a later age. | Close |
| loaders at the storehouse door | Dock and warehouse labour was done by slaves and hired men†. | Close |
| the goatherd who crosses the mountain | A local who knew the hill path that no army could see: Ephialtes of Malis showed the Persians the Anopaia track round the pass of Thermopylae (Herodotus 7.213–218)†. | Close |
| bandits who take carts on the road | Brigandage and piracy were constant in the Hellenistic world, and cargoes were lost as they would be to a sale. | Close |

### Props

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| slips of papyrus | Papyrus was the ordinary Hellenistic writing material for letters and accounts. | Close |
| numbers written as Greek letters | The alphabetic numerals, Α for one, Β for two, were in use from the Hellenistic period†. | Close |
| water-clocks that disagree; a sundial dead under cloud | Athenian courts timed speeches with the *klepsydra* (Aristotle, Constitution of Athens 67), and the Tower of the Winds in Athens combined sundials with a water-clock. Greek hours also varied in length with the season†, so there really was no common hour. | Close |
| the red sash on the messenger who carries the marker | Heralds were sacrosanct under Hermes, and killing them was thought to bring a curse: the Spartans' killing of Persian heralds is the famous case (Herodotus 7.133–137)†. A red sash itself is the setting's own. | Loose |
| carts, mules, oil jars, merchant ships | The Kyrenia ship, a fourth-century BC merchantman found off Cyprus, carried wine jars, millstones and almonds†; mule and ox carts took goods on land. | Close |
| the houses' account scrolls | Hellenistic accounts were kept on papyrus rolls and sheets and on wax tablets; the Zenon papyri are the surviving archive of one such manager†. | Close |
| the tally kept in a counting room | Greek reckoning used pebbles on a counting board; the marble Salamis tablet is an example†. | Close |

### Practices

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| orders served in the sequence placed | Athens preferred the lot and the rota to a queue; first come, first served is the setting's own rule. | Invented |
| one house at a time in the shared storehouse | The president of the Athenian prytaneis, chosen by lot for a day and a night, kept the keys of the treasuries and the public seal (Aristotle, Constitution of Athens 44). A single key-holder is the real form of mutual exclusion. | Loose |
| counting the stock without stopping the trade | Temples and fleets were inventoried each year (the Delian temple accounts, the Athenian naval lists)†, but those were counted at rest. Counting a moving trade is the setting's problem. | Loose |
| trade in the cloudy season | Hesiod advises against sailing in winter (Works and Days 618–694)†, so the houses' trade is by road, which fits. | Close |

## Mount Phyle · the foot and the crown

### Places

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| Mount Phyle, a hill held by a small band | Phyle is a real border fortress on Mount Parnes in Attica. In 404 BC Thrasybulus seized it with a small band of exiles, and an attack by the Thirty failed in a snowstorm (Xenophon, Hellenica 2.4.2–3). The setting borrows the name and the situation of a small garrison on a hill that a larger force cannot take. | Close |
| four camps with no pair in sight of one another | Greek armies camped in separate contingents round a besieged place; the Peloponnesian lines at Plataea are the standard example (Thucydides 2 and 3)†. | Loose |
| a small ruined ring wall of rough cyclopean stone, solid all round | Later Greeks called the great walls of Mycenae and Tiryns “Cyclopean”, as if giants had built them (Pausanias 2.25.8)†, and Mycenae was re-occupied in Hellenistic times†. Those walls have gates, so a wall with no gate is the setting's own. | Loose |
| three lookout posts out on the slopes: a hollow with a dry-stone breastwork | Dry-stone lookouts and field forts on Greek hillsides are common, and Attica had small hilltop forts (*phrouria*) beside Phyle†. | Close |

### Characters

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| four mercenaries, one man to a tent, no leader | Xenophon's Ten Thousand were hired for pay (a daric a month, Anabasis 1.3.21). When their generals were killed the army elected new ones and voted its decisions in assembly (3.1.46–47)†. Each man being hired separately is the setting's own. | Close |
| the four told apart by tunic colour | Soldiers were not generally in uniform, but Sparta's crimson cloak is the famous case of a colour that identifies a body of men (Xenophon, Constitution of the Lacedaemonians 11.3). | Loose |
| four bandits holding loot on a hill | Brigands and pirates were a fixture of the Hellenistic world, and bandit chiefs are stock figures in the Greek novels: Heliodorus's Egyptian herdsmen and Xenophon of Ephesus's robbers†. Why any one of them lies is never given, in the setting or in the model. | Close |
| the chief, in purple | Purple dye from the murex was one of the costliest in the ancient world†, so a chief in stolen purple is plausible; the colour only tells the figures apart. | Close |
| the lying lieutenant among loyal ones | Betrayal inside a defended position is a staple of Greek siege narrative, from Ephialtes at Thermopylae (Herodotus 7.213) onward. The paper's “Byzantine generals” are Lamport's own picture of an army round a city. | Close |

### Props

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| a wind that drops | The Aegean's summer north wind (the *etesiai*, now the meltemi) blows by day and tends to die away at dusk†, and the Tower of the Winds in Athens names eight winds. | Close |
| a linen tent, a plain bronze-faced shield, a chest, a wicker basket | The *skēnē* (tent), the *aspis* (the round shield, bronze-faced in Sparta's case, Xenophon 11.3), the chest and the basket are all ordinary kit†. | Close |
| ravens carrying messages, a strip in a bronze tube | Real ravens do not carry messages, as the setting says. The real bird is the pigeon: Taurosthenes of Aegina is said to have sent news of his Olympic win home to his father by a pigeon on the same day (Aelian, Varia Historia 9.2; Pliny, Natural History 10.53). Apollo's raven is a mythical messenger (Hesiod fr. 60)†. | Loose |
| a glass, turned at last light | The hourglass is not attested before the fourteenth century AD (its earliest evidence is Lorenzetti's fresco of 1338), and the Greek instrument is the water-clock. The setting keeps the sandglass on purpose, a deliberate anachronism for narrative convenience: it can be turned over at a stroke, so that four men who cannot see one another start together. | Anachronism |
| the slate on which a man chalks the tents he thinks dead | Greeks wrote notes on wax tablets, whitened boards and potsherds; chalk on slate is not attested for them†. The slate is a convenience of the allegory. | Loose |
| a coin: owl for hold, goddess for attack | The Athenian silver tetradrachm showed the head of Athena on one side and her owl on the other, and was accepted as money across the Greek world. The pairing with the two answers is the setting's own. | Close |
| rats as messengers, a cord knotted with the sender's number | No Greek army used rats; the setting says so. Apollo was “Smintheus”, lord of mice (Iliad 1.39)†, but that is a cult title, not a postal service. A knotted cord as a message is real: Darius left the Ionians a thong with sixty knots to undo one a day (Herodotus 4.98). | Invented |
| the sandglasses turned together at dusk | The hourglass is not attested before the fourteenth century AD (its earliest evidence is Lorenzetti's fresco of 1338), and the Greek instrument is the water-clock. The setting keeps the sandglass on purpose, a deliberate anachronism for narrative convenience: it can be turned over at a stroke, so that four men who cannot see one another start together. | Anachronism |
| seal rings cut with each man's number | Signet rings were the Greek proof of sender and of an unopened message: Polycrates' ring (Herodotus 3.41)†, and the seals Aeneas Tacticus describes for messages (31)†. | Close |
| the loot scroll, a copy at each post | Papyrus rolls were the ordinary Hellenistic form for lists and accounts, written in columns with new entries added at the end†. | Close |
| a timber ladder drawn up after the chief | Ladders and walls: the Plataeans' breakout used ladders over the besiegers' wall (Thucydides 3.20–24)†. | Close |
| strongboxes on a stone shelf | Treasure was kept in chests (*kibōtoi*) and temple treasuries†. | Close |

### Practices

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| no fire and no signal, because the other side is listening | Fire signals were real: the beacon stations of the Cyclades were called *phryktoriai*, and Polybius describes a signalling code (10.43–47). The weakness of fire signals is that the enemy can read them too. | Close |
| a hill that cannot be left unwatched, attack or hold | Councils of commanders voted on whether to fight or withdraw: Herodotus's account of the council before Salamis is the model (8.49–63)†. | Close |
| a man who cannot settle tosses a coin | Greeks decided by lot (the *klērotērion*) and played *ostrakinda*, in which a shell black on one side and white on the other was tossed while a player called “night” or “day” (Pollux 9.111, a later account of an older game). The coin toss as a decision rule is Roman, “heads or ships”†. | Loose |
| the glasses turned together as the last light leaves the crown | The civil day began at sunset in Athens†, so a common moment fixed by the evening light is a real one. | Close |

## Paxos · the Chamber

### Places

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| the island of Paxos | Lamport's story puts a thriving mercantile Paxos in the Aegean and warns that it is not the Ionian island of Paxoi. Real Paxos is a small island in the Ionian Sea. | Invented |
| the Y shape: a stem that forks into two arms round a bay | A whole island in this shape is rare; the parts are common. Chalkidiki is a peninsula that splits into three prongs, Pallene, Sithonia and Athos†, and Lesbos is cut by two deep gulfs, Kalloni and Gera, so that its land falls into lobes†. Sicily was called *Trinakria*, “three-cape”†, and Strabo likened the Peloponnese to a plane leaf†. The Aegean coast is a drowned one: the sea rose over a mountain country and filled its valleys†, and a bay between two ridges that run out as arms is just what that makes. | Loose |

### Buildings

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| the Chamber, a round hall ringed by a Doric colonnade under a conical tiled roof | Greek round halls were tholoi under conical roofs. The Athenian Tholos, a round building on the west side of the agora, housed the prytaneis (Aristotle, Constitution of Athens 44), and its roof was held up by columns inside†; the tholos at Delphi and the Philippeion at Olympia add a colonnade round the drum†. The tholos at Epidaurus has a Doric colonnade outside and a ring of Corinthian columns inside the drum†, so the Chamber's inner columns are Corinthian, not Doric. A true dome belongs to Roman concrete. Like a real tholos, it has one door, which can be locked. | Loose |
| poor acoustics, so legislators send messengers | This is Lamport's own conceit, which the setting follows faithfully. The real Greek councils met in halls built for hearing, such as the bouleuteria of Priene and Miletus†. | Invented |

### Characters

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| part-time legislators who wander in and out | The Athenian Council of Five Hundred sat by rota: each tribe's fifty served in turn for a tenth of the year, with one third of them on duty in the Tholos at any hour (Aristotle, Constitution of Athens 43–44). | Close |
| messengers between legislators | Heralds and runners carried decrees and summonses in every Greek city†. | Close |

### Props

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| a legislator's scroll in indelible ink | Greek ink was soot in gum and could be wiped off with a sponge†, so indelible ink is the story's own; the scroll itself is the ordinary Hellenistic form of a record. | Loose |
| a decree and its number | Athenian decrees were dated by the year's chief magistrate and the number of the prytany, and cut in stone†. | Close |

### Practices

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| a decree passes by a majority | The Athenian Assembly voted by show of hands, and law-courts by pebbles; decisions went by simple majority†. | Close |

## Paxos · Schedia

### Places

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| Schedia, a city that keeps its own laws on the eastern arm | *Schedia* is the Greek word for a raft, as in Odysseus' raft (Odyssey 5)†. A city founded from a different mother city kept its own laws: Amorgos held three cities in Classical times, Minoa from Samos, Aigiale from Miletus, Arkesine from Naxos, though the Naxian settlement is not securely attested until later. Lesbos held five (Herodotus 1.151)†. | Close |

### Buildings

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| the Tholos, a round hall with a podium | The Athenian Tholos housed the committee on duty, with one member presiding for a day and a night chosen by lot (Aristotle, Constitution of Athens 44). The podium is the setting's version of that president's place; the real president was chosen by lot, not by a numbered board. | Close |

### Characters

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| a council of five, a majority of three | Sparta had five ephors, elected each year†. Councils and colleges of other sizes were common. | Loose |
| the five told apart by colour; plain Greek names for the seats | Kleon, Theron and Melissa are ordinary Greek names (Kleon was an Athenian politician, Theron a tyrant of Akragas)†. Colours only tell the figures apart. | Close |
| boys who run the lines from the podium | Heralds and runners again†. | Close |

### Props

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| wax tablets smoothed when their holder goes home | Wax tablets were the Greek notebook; the stylus's flat end smooths them. Plato uses a wax block as his image of memory (Theaetetus 191c–d)†. | Close |
| a bench's name cut into its stone | Seats in the Theatre of Dionysus at Athens were inscribed with the names of the priests and officials who held them†. | Close |

### Practices

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| a higher board raised when the podium falls silent | Athens renewed its presidency daily by lot. A new holder taking a higher board number is the setting's rule, not a Greek one. | Invented |

## Paxos · the scholars' coast

### Places

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| Homonoia, the scholars' coast | *Homonoia*, “concord”, was a Greek civic ideal and later a cult of the Hellenistic and Roman world†. | Close |

### Buildings

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| the libraries | The Library and Museum of Alexandria, and Pergamon's rival library, kept and collated copies of the works of Greek literature†. | Close |
| the stoa, where scholars pass on news | Zeno taught under the Stoa Poikile in Athens, which gave the Stoics their name†; philosophers and scholars gathered in colonnades. | Close |
| the festival ground, where copyists compare whole collections once a year | Panhellenic festivals drew poets, rhapsodes and scholars from across the Greek world; the yearly collation itself is the setting's own. | Loose |
| a stone table grooved in columns, with piles of pebbles | The Salamis Tablet, a marble counting board of the fourth century BC, was used for reckoning with pebbles†; *psēphoi* are pebbles used to count. | Close |
| white pebbles for copies made, black for copies lost | Jurors voted with pebbles of two kinds, as Athena's casting vote does in Aeschylus' *Eumenides* (734–753)†. Counting made against lost with the two colours is the setting's own. | Loose |
| a hillside cut into terraces, a scribe at each | Greek sanctuaries were built on terraces cut into slopes, as at Delphi†. | Close |
| the far terraces, a count kept in voting pebbles | Greek *psēphoi* were pebbles used to vote and to count, and the Greek for “to reckon” (*psēphizein*) comes from them†. | Close |
| the courier landing | Hellenistic kings ran their own couriers, and merchant ships carried private letters†. | Close |
| the ferry across the inlet, the one crossing between two parts of the coast | Short sea crossings were ordinary: the strait between the Attic shore and Salamis was crossed by boat†, and the Euripus was bridged only in 411 BC†. | Close |

### Characters

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| scholars and copyists, readers, couriers | Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus of Samothrace edited Homer at Alexandria, recording variant readings†; *hemerodromoi* were Greek couriers on foot. | Close |

### Props

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| a scrap of papyrus carrying a new learning: that the Earth goes round the Sun | This is a real example: Aristarchus of Samos proposed it in the third century BC, as Archimedes reports in the Sand Reckoner, and Plutarch says that Cleanthes thought he should be charged with impiety for it†. | Close |
| different editions of one work | Homer's text circulated in variant forms, among them the “city” editions that the Alexandrians collated†. | Close |
| the catalogue scroll, and the catalogue mark worked from all of it | Callimachus' *Pinakes* catalogued the Alexandrian library in some 120 scrolls†. The mark is the setting's own: sealed documents were common†, but no seal summed up a whole catalogue, and a digest is a modern device. | Invented |
| a notice of withdrawal, spread like an edition | Aristarchus and Zenodotus marked lines they judged spurious with the *obelos* and left them in the text†; Athenian decrees were sometimes struck from their stelai†. The setting's notice, which travels and cancels older copies, is its own. | Loose |
| the hot tablet, a wax tablet of the scraps a scholar is still telling | Wax tablets were the Greek everyday writing surface†. | Close |

### Practices

| In the setting | The real precedent | How close |
|---|---|---|
| collating copies line by line | The Alexandrian scholars collated manuscripts and marked disputed lines, as the setting says†. | Close |
| learnings spread by word of mouth, each carrier losing interest | Hesiod calls rumour a goddess, *Pheme*, who never wholly dies (Works and Days 763–764)†. Rhapsodes also spread the epics by recitation (Plato, Ion)†, and a rule credited to Solon or Hipparchus made them take over from one another in order†. | Loose |
| a work kept at several libraries, each holding its own copy | Athens kept an official copy of the tragedies and Ptolemy III is said to have borrowed it for Alexandria, giving a deposit and keeping the original (Galen on Hippocrates' *Epidemics* III)†. Several libraries holding copies of one work is ordinary; the three fixed keepers are the setting's own. | Loose |
| the couriers' logbook of journey times | Herodotus describes the stages and days of the Royal Road from Sardis to Susa (5.52–54)†, and *periploi* record days of sailing between ports†. A logbook of the variation in a journey, from which odds are worked, is the setting's own. | Loose |
| a promise with odds: nine in ten you are handed the newest | Greek rhetoric argued from the likely, *eikos* (Antiphon's *Tetralogies*; Aristotle, *Rhetoric* 2.24)†; odds worked out from counts are not Greek. | Loose |
| a library lends a copy to a reader for a term | Ptolemy III is said to have borrowed Athens' official copy of the tragedies against a large deposit and kept the original, returning a copy (Galen on Hippocrates' *Epidemics* III)†. A term of loan is the setting's own. | Loose |
| corrections to several works bound under one cord, shown all together or not at all | Scrolls were tied and sealed in bundles†; showing a bundle only when complete is the setting's own. | Loose |
| the reading room in every library, where a reader asks and corrects | Scholars read in the rooms and colonnades of the Museum and library at Alexandria†. | Close |
| keepers of a dormant notice named by lot | Athens filled most magistracies by lot, drawing names with an allotment machine, the *kleroterion*†. Drawing names to keep a notice is the setting's use of it. | Close |
| answering from whatever copy a library holds | The Alexandrian library is said to have seized books from ships in its harbour and returned copies†, so the copy a reader held really did depend on the shelf. | Loose |
| no assembly; nothing waits for a vote | The scholarly world had no governing body; the Museum was a royal foundation and its scholars answered to the king†. | Loose |
