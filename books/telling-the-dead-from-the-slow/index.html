Telling the Dead from the Slow

A machine that has crashed looks exactly like one that is merely slow, and that alone makes agreement impossible. How well must we tell the dead from the slow before agreement becomes possible again?

The four mercenaries at the foot of the hill must go in at one gate, and any of them may fall silent while no bird has a longest flight. Give each tent a slate on which a man chalks the tents he thinks are dead. The slate may be wrong, often. It turns out to need very little: if one living man is never chalked, the four can agree however many die; if that holds only in the end, they can agree while more than half live.

Chapter I

The Slate

Bandits hold the crown of Mount Phyle. At the foot, at the four quarters, four mercenaries keep four small tents, one man to a tent, under nobody's command, and no man can see another*: Fischeros at the bull, Lynchaia at the boar, Patersonos at the trident and Kallias at the stag. They must all go in at one gate, north or south. Each formed an opinion at dusk, his sighting. Word passes only by raven. A bird always arrives, but there is no longest flight. Nobody lies. Any man may be killed, and a dead man's tent simply falls silent.

The three demands First. No two of them go to different gates.
Second. A man goes only to a gate someone sighted.
Third. Every man who is not killed eventually goes.

On such a night no arrangement guarantees that anyone goes, because a man waiting on a silent tent cannot tell whether the man there is dead or his bird is still in the pines*. If he waits, he may wait for ever on a dead man. If he stops waiting, he may leave out a live man whose bird was about to arrive.

The one change in this book: each tent has a slate, with a row for every other tent. A man chalks a tent he thinks is dead and rubs the mark out when he thinks again. How he decides is not the question here. The slate may be wrong, and often is: a mark may stand against a live man whose bird is merely slow, and a dead man's row may stay clean for a while. The question is how good a slate must be for the four to agree.

Chapter II

How a Slate May Be Wrong

A slate can go wrong in two ways. It can fail to chalk the dead, or it can chalk the living. So two things are asked of it.

Chalking the dead. Every man who is killed is, in the end, chalked for good. Either by every living man, or at least by some living man.

Sparing the living. Four strengths, from strongest to weakest:

Four ways of sparing the living Never chalked: no man is chalked while he lives.
One never chalked: some man who lives through the night is never chalked by anyone.
In the end, never chalked: there comes a time after which no living man is chalked.
In the end, one never chalked: there comes a time after which some man who lives through the night is chalked by no one.
Completeness, strong and weak; accuracy, strong, weak, eventual strong and eventual weak, §2.3.

Two ways of chalking the dead and four of sparing the living make eight kinds of slate.

The last column is the weakest thing a slate can promise and still promise anything. Every living man may be chalked at one time or another, over and over, by everyone. All that is asked is that, from some moment nobody can see, one man who lives is left alone. No man ever knows that the moment has come, or who that man is.

Chalking the dead on its own is worth nothing. A slate that chalks every tent all night chalks every dead man, and tells nobody anything. It is the sparing of the living that makes a slate useful.

Chapter III

Copying Marks

The two rows of the table are really one. If only some living man chalks the dead, the men can make every living man do so by copying one another's marks.

Copying marks Every man keeps a second slate, his copy. Again and again he sends every tent the marks on his own slate. When a bird comes from a tent, he adds its marks to his copy, and rubs out the mark against the tent the bird came from.
The reduction TD→D′, Fig. 3, §3.
Copying Marks

If some living man in the end chalks every dead man for good, then with copying every living man does. And the copy spares the living exactly as well as the slate it was copied from.

Proof — that copying makes every living man chalk the dead, and spares the living as before
  1. The dead. A dead man sends no more birds, so after his last bird arrives nobody rubs him out. The living man who in the end chalks him for good goes on sending his marks, and every living man adds them to his copy. So in the end every living man's copy chalks him for good.
  2. Never chalked, or one never chalked. A man that nobody's slate has chalked is on nobody's list of marks, so nobody copies a mark against him.
  3. In the end. Suppose from some time no living man's slate chalks a certain living man. The dead stop sending, so in the end no bird carries a mark against him. He goes on sending, and each bird from him rubs him out of every copy. So in the end no copy chalks him.∎

So only the top row matters: perfect, strong, in the end perfect, and in the end strong. The next two chapters use the two middle ones, strong and in the end strong. A perfect slate is also strong, and one in the end perfect is also in the end strong, so whatever works with the weaker works with the stronger.

Chapter IV

One Man Never Chalked

Suppose the slates are strong: every living man in the end chalks the dead, and one man who lives, say Lynchaia, is never chalked by anyone. Nobody knows it is Lynchaia. Then the four can agree however many of them die, even three.

Each man keeps a tally of sightings: a row for each tent, holding that tent's sighting if he has learned it, and blank if not. At first it holds only his own.

The arrangement for a strong slate Spreading, three rounds. In each round, a man sends every tent the sightings he learned for the first time in the round before (in the first round, his own). He waits for that round's bird from every tent, except a tent he has chalked; if he chalks a tent while waiting, he stops waiting for it. He writes any new sightings into his tally.
Comparing. He sends every tent his whole tally, and waits in the same way. Any row that is blank in any tally he receives, he blanks in his own.
Going. He goes to the gate in the first row of his tally that is not blank, taking the rows in shield order.
The algorithm of Fig. 5, §6.1: n − 1 = 3 asynchronous rounds of Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3.

The whole thing turns on Lynchaia. Nobody ever chalks her, so everybody waits for every one of her birds. Whatever she has learned by a round, everyone who finishes the next round has learned too. By the end of spreading, every tally that finishes holds everything Lynchaia's holds. In comparing, everyone waits for her tally, and blanks every row that is blank in hers; and every row filled in hers is filled in every tally, so nobody blanks it. So everyone who reaches the end holds exactly Lynchaia's tally, which is not empty, since it has her own sighting. They all go to the same gate, a gate someone sighted.

Nobody waits for ever: a dead man is chalked in the end, and a live man's bird comes in the end. So everyone who lives goes.

One Man Never Chalked

With strong slates, the four meet all three demands however many of them are killed.

Proof — that with a strong slate everyone who finishes holds the same tally
  1. Every row of every tally is either blank or that tent's true sighting, since sightings are only ever copied.
  2. Let c be the man never chalked. Everyone who finishes a round received c's bird for that round, since he waited for it.
  3. After spreading, every finished tally holds all of c's. Take a sighting c first learned in round r. If r is before the last round, c sends it in round r + 1 and everyone who finishes that round has it. If r is the last round, three rounds among four men means the sighting reached c through every other man, each of whom wrote it into his tally before passing it on.
  4. After comparing, every finished tally equals c's. A row filled in c's tally is filled in every tally sent in comparing, by the last step, so nobody blanks it. A row blank in c's is blanked by everyone, since everyone waits for c's tally.
  5. c's own row is filled in c's tally and so in everyone's, so every finished tally has a first filled row, the same in all. Everyone who goes, goes to that tent's sighting.
  6. Nobody waits for ever, since a dead man is in the end chalked by every living man and a live man's birds all arrive. So every living man goes.∎

No man in this arrangement is in charge of anything. Everybody sends everything to everybody, and the man who holds it together is one nobody can name.

Chapter V

In the End, One Man Spared

Now suppose less: the slates are only in the end strong. For as long as the slates like, every man may be chalked by everyone. Only from some moment nobody sees is one living man left alone. Then the four can still agree, provided more than half of them live. With four men, one may die.

They take turns. Each round belongs to one tent, in shield order: bull, boar, trident, stag, bull again. The round number decides whose turn it is, not anyone's command, as on the windy nights*. Each man carries an estimate, a gate, which starts as his sighting, and beside it the round in which he last took it up.

A round, for slates in the end strong (1) Every man sends the round's owner his estimate and the round he took it up.
(2) The owner waits for three of these, himself included, and takes the estimate taken up most recently. He sends it to every tent.
(3) Every man waits for the owner's estimate, or until he chalks the owner. If the estimate comes, he takes it up, notes this round, and sends the owner agreed. If he chalks the owner first, he sends not agreed.
(4) The owner waits for three answers. If all three say agreed, he sends every tent a going bird naming his estimate.
Going. A man who takes in a going bird for the first time sends it on to every tent, then goes to its gate.
The rotating-coordinator algorithm of Fig. 6, §6.2; three is ⌈(n + 1)/2⌉ for n = 4. The going bird is Reliable Broadcast of the decision, Fig. 4.

Why they never go to two gates. Take the first round in which three men sent agreed, say on north. Those three took up north in that round. Any later owner waits for three estimates, and any three out of four share a man with those three, so the owner sees at least one north taken up that recently or later. Take the estimate taken up most recently, and by the same argument round after round, it is north. So every later owner sends north, and every going bird names north.

Why they go. Nobody waits for ever: a dead owner is in the end chalked, and three men live to answer. From some moment one living man is never chalked. When his turn next comes, he gathers three estimates, everyone waits for his, nobody chalks him, all say agreed, and his going bird goes out.

In the End, One Man Spared

With slates that are only in the end strong, the four meet all three demands provided more than half of them live.

Proof — that the turns never send two gates, and in the end send one
  1. No two gates. Let r be the first round in which three men sent agreed to its owner, on estimate e. Each of them took up e in round r. Show by induction that every owner of a round k ≥ r who sends an estimate sends e. For k = r it is so. For later k, the owner heard from three men; one of them is among the three of round r, and reported an estimate taken up in round r or later. So the most recent round t among the three satisfies r ≤ t < k, and the estimate taken up in round t was sent by that round's owner, which by induction was e.
  2. Every going bird was first sent by an owner who had three agreed in a round no earlier than r, so every going bird names e, and everyone who goes, goes to e. Every estimate is someone's sighting, so e was sighted.
  3. Going. If anyone living takes in a going bird, he sends it on before going, so every living man takes it in and goes. Suppose instead nobody living ever goes. No living man is stuck for ever in any round: three men live to send estimates and answers, and a man waiting on an owner either gets his estimate or, if the owner is dead, in the end chalks him.
  4. From some time, one living man q is chalked by nobody, and the dead are all dead. Take a later round whose owner is q. He gathers three estimates and sends one; everyone waits for it, since nobody chalks him; three answer agreed; he sends a going bird. That contradicts the supposition.∎

While the slates are still chalking the living, rounds fail: a man chalks a live owner whose bird is slow, says not agreed, and the round comes to nothing. Failing is always safe. The chalk can cost time; it can never cost agreement.

Chapter VI

Why More Than Half, Then

With a strong slate the four bear any number of deaths. With one that is only in the end strong, they need more than half alive. The need is real, and it holds even for slates in the end perfect, which in the end chalk the dead and nobody else.

More Than Half

If two of the four may die, no arrangement meets the three demands, even with slates in the end perfect.

Proof — that slates in the end perfect cannot carry the four past two deaths
  1. Suppose some arrangement does. Divide the four into pairs: Fischeros and Lynchaia, Patersonos and Kallias.
  2. The first night. All sighted north. Patersonos and Kallias are dead from dusk, and the other two chalk them all night. That slate is in the end perfect. Fischeros and Lynchaia go, within some time A, and to north, since only north was sighted.
  3. The second night. The same with pairs and gates swapped: Patersonos and Kallias go south within some time B.
  4. The third night. Fischeros and Lynchaia sighted north, Patersonos and Kallias south. Nobody dies. Every bird between the pairs sits in the pines until after A and B, and until then each pair chalks the other; after that, all birds arrive and all chalk is rubbed out. That slate is in the end perfect too.
  5. Until then each pair sees exactly what it saw on its own night, and goes: one pair north, the other south. That contradicts the first demand.∎

The difference from Chapter IV is when the slate is right. A strong slate never chalks one living man, from dusk; that man holds the four together from the start. A slate that is right only in the end may be wrong about everyone for as long as the arrangement needs it to be right, and while it is, the four are two pairs, each able to believe the other dead.

Chapter VII

What the Slate Does Not Say

It does not say how to keep one. The slate's promises are granted, not earned. On a night when no bird has a longest flight, and nothing more is known, no man can keep a slate that is even in the end weak: if he could, the four could agree on that night, and they cannot*. The slates of this book are an assumption about the hill, stated as a promise about chalk.

On a windy night, a slate can be kept. Where a wind blows for a while and then drops, and in still air every bird comes within a glass*, a man can keep a slate in the end perfect. Every man sends a bird to every tent, again and again, to say he is alive. A man chalks a tent when a glass has run out with no bird from it; when a bird from it comes after all, he rubs out the mark and waits longer for that tent next time. A dead man's birds stop, so he is chalked for good. Once the wind has dropped, a live man's birds come in time once the waiting is long enough, so in the end nobody living is chalked. The slate is a way of saying what the wind gives, without saying anything about wind.

It does not have to be right to be safe. If the slates break every promise, chalking the living for ever and never chalking the dead, the arrangements of Chapters IV and V may never send anyone. They never send two men to different gates. Only going is lost.

The weakest slate that will do. In the end weak, with more than half alive, is enough. It is also necessary: any slate with which the four could agree can be made into one in the end weak. That was proved separately, and is not proved here.

Agreeing once is agreeing on an order. If the four had many things to decide, one after another, and had to take them in the same order, the same slates would do, and nothing weaker: each can be turned into the other.

Nothing follows from going. The four go in at one gate. What happens there is not the subject of this book.

The ideas, plainly

Consensus is impossible in an asynchronous system with even one crash, because a crashed process cannot be told from a slow one. Chandra and Toueg add an unreliable failure detector: a module at each process that lists the processes it suspects, and may be wrong. They classify detectors by completeness (crashed processes are eventually suspected, by every or by some correct process) and accuracy (correct processes are not suspected: never, or eventually; all of them, or at least one).

Weak completeness can be turned into strong by gossiping suspicions, so four classes matter. With S, where some correct process is never suspected, consensus tolerates any number of crashes, by relaying proposals for n − 1 rounds. With ◇S, where some correct process is eventually never suspected, consensus needs a majority of correct processes and uses a rotating coordinator; safety holds however badly the detector behaves, and progress returns once it behaves. The majority is necessary even with ◇P, by a partition argument.

A failure detector packages timing assumptions as a statement about suspicions. In partially synchronous systems, growing timeouts implement ◇P; the detector lets algorithms be written without mentioning time at all. ◇W is the weakest detector for consensus, and consensus is equivalent to atomic broadcast.
