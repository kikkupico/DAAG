Pilot · Questions · Arche · The Ring Road

Ordering Without Clocks

If no two machines agree on the time, how can they agree on what happened first? And when two things happen far apart, how can anyone tell whether one led to the other or neither knew of the other?

Three threads run through this book and take turns: the orders on House 3's grain, a clerk's doubt about her own book, and a goatherd who crosses the mountain. Each chapter leaves a question that a later chapter of its own thread answers.

Chapter I · The Orders

Two Messengers

Three trading houses, House 1, House 2 and House 3, stand round the coast of Arche with Mount Phyle between them. Word goes between them only on written slips carried by messengers along the ring road, where no messenger can pass another, so slips from one house to another arrive in the order they were sent, after a time nobody can bound. In the trading season cloud silences the sundials, and the water-clocks of the three counting rooms keep no common hour*.

Say House 3 has grain in its storehouse, enough for one of the others, and both need it. House 2 sends a messenger with an order for it. House 1, knowing nothing of this, sends a messenger with an order of its own. The houses have agreed that the order placed first is filled first. So which was placed first?

Can House 3 tell by which reaches its gate first? No: then a house with a quicker messenger would win, whoever placed its order first. It must know when each was written, and for that it needs a clock the three houses share, and they share none. Could House 3 ask each house the hour its order was written? It would be told two readings of two water-clocks that were never set together, and comparing them would decide nothing.

Every way of settling it asks for a clock the island does not have. Must the houses then give up filling the first-placed order first? Or have they been asking the wrong question?

Chapter II · The Clerk's Doubt

The Clerk's Doubt

At House 2 the clerk who keeps the book has a doubt of her own, and it is a different one. Her book holds two entries a few lines apart: an order House 2 placed for oil, and, above it, a slip that came in from House 1 about the price of jars. Was the order placed because of the slip? Or would House 2 have placed it anyway, knowing nothing of House 1's business?

The question matters to her. If the order followed from the slip, then whatever House 1 does next with its jars may bear on it; if the two are unconnected, she can treat them apart. But what in her book could tell her? The lines are in order, one above the other, and every line is House 2's own. The book records what happened at House 2. It does not record what House 2 knew, still less what House 1 knew when it wrote its slip.

Put it more sharply. Two entries in two different books, one at House 1 and one at House 3: were they made in ignorance of each other? Nothing written at either house seems able to say.

Is there any way of keeping a book so that a clerk can read off, from two entries alone, whether one could have known of the other?

Chapter III · The Orders

What Came Before

Lamportios, who kept the books of House 1, answered the question of the first chapter by changing it. The houses had been asking which of two orders came first in absolute time, which no house can see. What their business needs is narrower. An order should never be served before one that could have led to it. And whether one thing could have led to another is something the houses can see, because the only way news moves on Arche is by slip.

What, then, does it mean for one event to come before another? He set down three rules, and they are the whole foundation. One event came before another (written a → b) when:

The three rules of coming-before (1) They happened in the same house, and the book of that house has the first written above the second.
(2) The first is a house dispatching a slip, and the second is another house receiving that same slip.
(3) Or there is some third event that the first came before, and which came before the second.

If none of the three rules joins two events, they are unrelated (a ∥ b). Does unrelated mean they happened at the same moment? No: it means neither could have known of the other, since no chain of slips joins them. The sun may have seen one before the other, but nothing the houses hold can tell them which.

How is a clerk to calculate with this? Lamportios gave every house a tally: a number kept in the counting room, written on every slip sent out, and raised by two mechanical rules.

The two rules of the tally (1) A house advances its tally by at least one between any two entries in its own book.
(2) When dispatching a slip, a house writes its current tally on it. When receiving a slip, a house sets its own tally higher than the number on the slip, and never lowers it, before entering the arrival in its book.
The Clock Condition

Whenever event a came before event b, the tally of a is strictly smaller than the tally of b:
a → b ⟹ C(a) < C(b)

Walking forward along any chain of slips or book lines always means larger numbers. But is the converse true: does a smaller tally mean an earlier event? It does not. Two houses that have heard nothing from each other all morning may give tallies 2 and 3 to two unrelated events, and a clerk reading only the numbers would take the 2 for the earlier. The tally puts a sequence where nature left none.

So the tally respects every chain of slips, and says nothing true about orders that no chain joins. Yet the two orders for House 3's grain are exactly such orders. How can a number that means nothing between them decide which is served first?

Chapter IV · The Mountain

Over the Mountain

There is a way across Arche that is not the road. A goatherd who knows the smugglers' tracks can cross Mount Phyle, if he is lucky, in a couple of hours, though he may take two days or never come down, which is why no house sends its business that way*.

Suppose a trader at House 3 concludes a sale, then pays a goatherd to cross to House 1. This time the goatherd is lucky. House 1 hears the news and at once issues an order based on it. House 1 has had a quiet morning, and its tally stands at 3. House 3's busy counting room had reached 9 when the sale was entered. The order, which the sale caused, carries a smaller tally than the sale itself, and hours later House 3's messenger arrives by the road with the official letter, too late to correct anything.

Is that a fault in the tally? By its own rules, no. The rules of the tally touch only slips, and the goatherd carried no slip. As far as the houses' books can tell, the sale and the order are unrelated. Yet the order was caused by the sale, as surely as if a messenger had carried it.

The houses can make their numbers respect every slip. Could any clock respect the goatherd too, a path that carries no slip at all?

Chapter V · The Orders

Breaking Ties

Return to the grain, and the question the tally left open. Two orders no chain of slips joins: the tally may give them different numbers, or the same one. A partial order cannot decide who gets the grain; “these two orders are unrelated” does not tell House 3 whose order to fill. What does it need? A sequence that decides between every pair, that every house works out the same way, and that never contradicts a chain of slips.

Lamportios got it by the plainest means available. Compare tallies; where they are equal, compare the houses' numbers, fixed once and for all: House 1 before House 2 before House 3.

The rule of precedence An event a at house i is earlier than event b at house j (written a ⇒ b) if:
(1) C(a) < C(b);
(2) or C(a) = C(b) and i is less than j.
Proof — that precedence is a total order extending coming-before
  1. Total comparability. For any two distinct events a and b: if their tallies differ, clause (1) decides. If their tallies are equal and they occurred at the same house, rule (1) of Chapter III already gave the later one a higher tally, so they must belong to different houses, whose numbers differ, and clause (2) decides.
  2. Antisymmetry and transitivity. Compare by the pair (C(e), house). This is the standard lexicographical ordering of pairs drawn from two total orders, which is strictly total and transitive.
  3. Consistency with causality. If a → b, then by the Clock Condition C(a) < C(b) strictly. Precedence places a earlier by clause (1) alone; house numbers are never consulted. Hence a → b ⟹ a ⇒ b.∎

Does this find out which unrelated order was really first? No, and nothing the houses hold could. It replaces the question with an answer every house reads the same way. Is that fair? In the one sense the houses can check: where one order led to another, they are filled in that sequence, and no house can win by owning a quicker messenger. Judged by the sun, placed first is a different promise, and the tallies do not keep it.

So the houses can agree on a sequence without a clock. Is that enough to run something that truly needs one house at a time, with no master to hand out turns?

Chapter VI · The Clerk's Doubt

The Column

The clerk's doubt of the second chapter is still open, and the tally does not settle it. Tallies 4 and 5 on two entries say only that the 5 was not made before the 4 could have reached it. Were they joined, or made in ignorance of each other? A single number cannot say.

Why not? Because a single number mixes together everything a house has heard. When House 2 raises its tally past a slip from House 1, it forgets whose number it was raised past. What would it take to remember? One number for each house.

Fidgeos of House 2 and Matternos of House 3 found this at the same time. Each house keeps a column of three rows, one for each house on the island; row i is the latest count of house i's own entries that this house has heard of.

The rules of the column (1) When entering any event of its own, the dispatch of a slip included, house i increments its own row: Vi[i] := Vi[i] + 1.
(2) When sending a slip, house i stamps its full column Vi on the slip, as it stands after that increment.
(3) On receiving slip with column U, house j merges each row by taking the maximum, then increments its own row: Vj[k] := max(Vj[k], U[k]), and Vj[j] := Vj[j] + 1.

Now the clerk has more than a number to look at. Her order carries a column; so does the slip from House 1. If the slip's row for House 1 is no greater than her order's row for House 1, then by the time of the order House 2 had heard of the slip's entry, and perhaps of more.

The column plainly sees more than the tally. Does it see everything: can a clerk now tell, from two columns alone, exactly whether one entry came before the other or neither knew of the other?

Chapter VII · The Orders

The Shared Storehouse

The island keeps one common dry storehouse for grain, cut into the rock beside the road. Only one house may load from it at a time. Could a keeper at the door simply let in whoever asks first? That is the trouble of the first chapter again: a request can reach him ahead of one sent earlier, because it had a shorter road to run.

The order of the fifth chapter answers the question that chapter left, though not by itself. Each house keeps a private queue of requests, ordered by precedence, and follows five rules.

The five rules of the storehouse (1) To request: House i writes a request under its current tally, enters it in its queue, and sends a messenger carrying it to every other house.
(2) On receiving a request: House j enters the request in its queue, advances its tally, and dispatches an acknowledgement slip back to i.
(3) To release: When done, house i removes its request from its queue and dispatches a release slip to every other house.
(4) On receiving a release: House j removes that house's request from its queue.
(5) To enter: House i enters the storehouse when its own request stands first in its queue, and it has received from every other house a slip carrying a tally greater than its request.

Why the second half of the fifth rule? It is not enough that your request is the earliest in your own queue, because an older request may be on the road right now in a messenger's pouch. What proves it is not? A slip from that house carrying a larger tally: the house had moved past your request's number, and on a road where no messenger passes another, nothing older can be trailing behind that slip.

Proof — that two houses never enter the storehouse at once
  1. Suppose for contradiction that House 1 and House 3 both hold the storehouse at once, on requests R₁ and R₃. Precedence is total, so one is earlier; assume R₁ ⇒ R₃.
  2. By Rule 5, when House 3 entered, it had received from House 1 some message carrying a tally T > C(R₃).
  3. House 1 dispatched R₁ to House 3 under tally C(R₁) before dispatching any message with tally T. Because the coastal road is single-file, slips from House 1 to House 3 arrive in FIFO order.
  4. Therefore, House 3 received R₁ before receiving the message with tally T. So R₁ was already in House 3's queue when House 3 entered.
  5. Since R₁ ⇒ R₃, R₃ did not stand first in House 3's queue. Rule 5 was violated, and House 3 could not have entered.∎

The houses now share a storehouse by a sequence they all agree on, with no clock and no master. But whose order is it? It is not the sun's. If a merchant who saw both houses' messengers set out swore that House 2 asked first, could the houses ever prove him wrong, or right?

Chapter VIII · The Clerk's Doubt

What the Column Sees

The sixth chapter asked whether the column sees everything. It does: comparing two columns row by row settles causality completely.

Vector Equivalence

For any two events a and b:
a → b ⟺ V(a) < V(b)
And two events are unrelated if and only if neither column dominates:
a ∥ b ⟺ ¬(V(a) ≤ V(b)) ∧ ¬(V(b) ≤ V(a))

Proof — that vector timestamps detect causality and concurrency exactly
  1. If a occurs at house i with timestamp V(a) and a → b, there is a causal chain of local events and messages from a to b. By Rules 1–3, every step non-strictly increases all components and strictly increases the component of the executing process. Thus V(a)[i] ≤ V(b)[i]. Since at least one step strictly increments a component, V(a) < V(b).
  2. Conversely, suppose a occurs at house i and does not precede b (a ↛ b). Then no slip carrying information about a has reached b. By Rule 3, the value of row i at b reflects only events at i that causally precede b. Since a is not among them, V(b)[i] < V(a)[i]. Thus V(a) ≰ V(b).
  3. Therefore, a → b if and only if V(a) < V(b). Concurrency a ∥ b holds if and only if neither column dominates the other.∎

So the clerk's doubt has its answer. Her order was placed because of House 1's slip exactly when the slip's column is no greater than her order's in every row. And two entries at two houses were made in ignorance of each other exactly when neither column is at least the other. Where the tally could only fail to rule out a connection, the column finds it or rules it out.

Is anything left that the column cannot see? Only what never went by slip. The column is built from slips and nothing else.

Which brings back the goatherd, who carried none.

Chapter IX · The Mountain

Clocks That Run With the World

Two questions have waited for this chapter. Could any clock respect the goatherd's path? And whose order do the houses keep, if not the sun's?

To respect the goatherd, it would not be enough to respect slips. What would be needed is this: if one event precedes another in the world, by any path at all, its number is smaller. No rule about slips can promise that, since the path that matters carried no slip. Only clocks that run with the world can.

Lamportios described such clocks. Each house keeps a physical clock that runs true to within a small fraction. Every slip carries the sender's reading, and a house that receives it sets its own clock forward, if it is behind, to that reading plus the least time the road could have taken. If messengers go down every stretch at regular hours, and the road has a longest crossing, the three clocks stay within some small bound of one another. Then the goatherd is caught, provided that bound is smaller than his fastest crossing: a man who arrives sooner than the clocks can disagree could still arrive, by House 1's clock, before he left.

Can the houses keep such clocks? They cannot. The remedy needs a longest crossing and messengers at regular hours, and the trading season gives neither: a messenger takes as long as he takes, and nobody on the island knows the hour. Arche's clocks remain the water-clocks of the counting rooms, and the tallies are all the order the houses have.

That answers the merchant of the seventh chapter too. The sequence the houses keep is theirs, not the sun's. It respects every slip; between orders that no slip joins it decides by a rule every house reads alike, and it can neither prove the merchant wrong nor prove him right. A sequence judged by the sun would be a different promise, and a harder one.
