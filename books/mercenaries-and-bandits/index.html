Mercenaries and Bandits

The limits of agreement when processes crash or lie

One hill, two parties, one question apiece. Below, four mercenaries who must go in at the same gate and cannot tell a dead man from a slow bird: no arrangement guarantees that any of them ever goes. Above, four bandits who must hold or scatter together and cannot tell a lying chief from a lying peer: they can agree, but only if more than two thirds are honest — or, under seal, whatever the number of liars.

Chapter I

The Hill

Bandits hold the top of the hill, where the loot is. Their chief sits on the peak with it, and a little below him, spaced round the slope, three of his men hold three posts, each watching a different approach. There are two ways through the wall on the top: a narrow slot on the north side and a wider timber-framed gap on the south.

The crown is not a bare dome. It is a jumble of limestone crags and clefts, and the posts sit in three of them, each tucked below the rim of its own crag. No post can see another, and none can see the peak. Nor will the bandits shout, raise a banner or light a brand. It is night, the hill is quiet, and every tent at the foot is listening. A voice carries down a gully far better than it carries round a crag, and a light on the crown is seen from the whole island. Whatever the bandits say to one another, they say where no one else can hear.

Four mercenaries have been hired to clear them out. Each has pitched a small tent at the foot of a gully, at the four quarters — one man to a tent, hired separately, paid separately, under nobody's command and least of all each other's. They cannot see one another: between any two tents stand two wooded spurs. They light no fires and sound no horns, which would tell the hill where they are and that they are talking. Nor can a man carry word himself, or send anyone with it. There is nobody else in his tent, and to walk round the foot to a neighbour is to leave his own quarter open. That is the very thing going does, and he would not be back before dawn.

Both parties send word by raven, and each man keeps a few birds belonging to the others. Each party has one question to settle, and each question has two answers.

Where the allegory leaks

Real ravens do not carry messages home; homing doves do, and a homing bird flies one way only, to its own loft. So each man really does need birds belonging to the tent he writes to, and his stock of them is limited. This makes the men's lot harder than the model's, which allows as many messages as a protocol cares to send. It does not help them, and nothing below turns on it. The birds here are called ravens and fly as many times as they are needed.

The two questions

Below. Four men cannot come at both gates — two at each is no assault, and a good way to be killed in pairs. So all four must go in at the same gate: north or south.

Above. The bandits must hold the hill and keep the loot, or scatter and abandon it, and must do the same thing. Half holding while half scatter is worse than either.

Which answer is the better one, in either case, is not the subject of this book and is settled nowhere in it. Nor is any night being narrated: what is described is an arrangement, not an evening, and nothing here comes out.

The two halves differ in exactly one respect each, and those two differences are the whole book. Below the hill, no man ever misreports anything, but any man may die, and there is no telling how long a bird will take. Above the wall, nobody dies and the flights are short and bounded, but some of the men do not report what they were told. Neither half pretends the other's trouble is impossible; each asks what can be arranged against one kind of failure.

Part One · Below

The Mercenaries

Fischeros at the bull, Lynchaia at the boar, Patersonos at the trident, and Kallias at the stag — one to a tent, at the four quarters of the foot. Nobody lies. Any one of them may be killed. No bird has a longest flight.

Chapter II

The Three Demands

Each man formed an opinion at dusk from what he could see of his own quarter — north gate or south gate, one of two. Three things are wanted of what they do with those opinions, and this half of the book is about the third.

The three demands

First. No two of them go to different gates.

Second. Both gates must stay genuinely possible. Agreeing beforehand to go north whatever anyone saw would satisfy the first demand perfectly and make the evening pointless. What they do must depend on what they saw.

Third. Somebody must eventually go.

The first is never in danger anywhere below. The second is easy. The third is the one that cannot be had.

Going is a definite act, not a state of mind. A man goes by striking his tent, taking up his shield and going round the slope to one gate. Once he has done it his quarter is unwatched, the bandits may slip out of it behind him, and he will not be back before dawn. So he may think what he likes for as long as he likes, and change his mind as often as he likes, and none of it counts; then he does one thing and it counts for good.

And they may not draw lots. Four men could have agreed to put a hand in a bag; they did not. Each acts on what has reached him and nothing else, so that given the same sightings and the same arrivals in the same order, each does the same thing every time.

Chapter III

The Ravens Below

A man writes on a strip, rolls it into a bronze tube, stops it with wax, ties it to the leg of a bird belonging to the tent he is writing to, and lets it go. Four things are true of the birds down here, and all four are needed.

They arrive. No bird of that hill has ever been lost. Nothing below ever vanishes.

They take as long as they take. The gullies are wooded and full of the hill's own crows, and a bird may join them for an hour or for the night before going on. There is no longest flight. No man can say if nothing has come from the bull by now, nothing is coming, because no such time exists and all four know it. A bird eleven hours in the folds is not more overdue than a bird one hour in the folds, only later.

They come in no particular order. Two birds sent an hour apart may arrive in either order, and often do.

Nothing is forged. A tube that arrives from the boar's tent was tied there and says what that man wrote. Whatever else these four do not know, they never doubt the word they actually hold.

One more fact, small and disproportionately important: a man may go to his perch and find nothing there. That is not news. He comes back empty-handed and the world is as it was, and he can do it all night. Looking and finding nothing is always available and never tells anybody anything.

Chapter IV

A Tent That Does Not Answer

Any one of the four may be killed at any moment — at his perch, on the path, in the middle of writing a strip. It has not happened; there is no assassin in this account. What matters is that it may, and that the four have arranged matters on that footing.

A dead mercenary's tent simply falls silent and stays silent. Nobody succeeds him: he was hired alone and paid alone, and there is no second man in that tent to take up his correspondence or even to know there was any. No bird goes out to report it. A dead man produces exactly one effect in the world, and it is the absence of anything.

The difficulty, entire

A man waiting on a tent that has not answered is in one of two situations. Either the man there is dead and no bird will ever come; or he is perfectly well, the bird went out hours ago, and it is sitting in a pine two spurs away.

Nothing distinguishes them — no observation, no instrument, no length of waiting. Both look like an empty perch. And since there is no longest flight, waiting longer never turns one into the other. It only makes the wait longer.

One point of arithmetic. At most one of the four may fall silent. Nobody on the hill could promise it; it is the assumption the argument grants, and it is a generous one to grant. What follows needs only the possibility of a single death, so allowing more could only make matters worse.

Chapter V

Standings and Arrivals

The argument needs five words that ordinary language lacks. The terms the mathematicians use are given beside them.

In this bookWhat it means
a standingHow the night stands, entire: what each of the four men holds in his head, together with every tube aloft and not yet taken in. A configuration.
an arrivalOne named man taking in one named tube. It is the only kind of thing that happens; a man thinks only when something arrives. An event.
an empty-handed arrivalGoing to the perch and finding nothing. Always possible, changes nothing, counts as that man's turn. A receive returning the null marker.
a course; a fair courseA list of arrivals in order. It is fair if it could really happen: no tube sent to a man still answering stays aloft for ever, and at most one man falls silent. A schedule and its run; an admissible run.
two-gated / one-gatedA standing is two-gated if a fair course from it still ends at the north gate and another still ends at the south. One-gated if only one remains. Bivalent / univalent.

Two-gated is not a state of mind. Every one of the four may be entirely resolved and the standing be two-gated all the same: what the word reports is that the night is not yet closed, and which gate it ends at still depends on the order in which birds happen to arrive. Nor does it mean nothing has happened yet. A standing late in the night, with a great deal settled, may be as two-gated as the standing at dusk.

Once any one of them has gone, of course, the standing is one-gated, and by the first demand every other man who ever goes must go the same way. That is what makes the first departure interesting.

Chapter VI

Separate Folds

The first thing to establish is the dullest and the most useful. It is about two men who have nothing to do with each other for a while.

The lemma of separate folds

Take any standing and two courses that could run from it, no man appearing in both. Then they do not interfere: run either first and the night arrives at the same standing.

An arrival consists of one man taking in one tube, reading it, and perhaps writing one or two of his own. Everything in that belongs to him or to the air, and nothing to anybody else; he cannot consult another tent even if he wants to. So if one course is the business of the bull and the boar and the other of the trident and the stag, each is weather to the other.

The one thing to check is the air. A tube is addressed, so the first course cannot swallow one the second needs, and no bird was ever in another bird's way.

Proof — that courses touching no man in common commute
  1. A standing is what is in each man's head together with the tubes aloft. An arrival changes two parts of it: the one man who takes the tube in, and the air — one tube removed, perhaps others added.
  2. Let σ₁ touch a set of men A and σ₂ a set B, disjoint. Running σ₁ alters only men in A, which σ₂ never reads; and conversely.
  3. Neither disables the other through the air: tubes are addressed, so those σ₂ consumes are addressed into B, and σ₁ removes only tubes addressed into A. Both orders are therefore applicable.
  4. In either order, the men of A have taken exactly the arrivals of σ₁ and those of B exactly the arrivals of σ₂, each in its own order, and the air has had the same tubes removed and added. So the standings reached are the same.∎
Chapter VII

The Dusk That Settles Nothing

The second thing to establish is that the night can begin open: that there is a way for the four sightings to fall which leaves both gates reachable, with nothing in the sightings to settle it.

Line up the sixteen possible beginnings so that each differs from the next in one man's sighting only, all-north at one end and all-south at the other. Suppose every beginning were already settled. The second demand forces the ends to settle opposite ways, so somewhere along the row two neighbours are settled opposite ways while differing in one man's sighting. Call that man p.

Take a fair course from the north-settled neighbour in which p never stirs — he is allowed to be the one silent man — and by the third demand it must end at the north gate. Now run that very same course from the other beginning. The two differ only in what p saw, and p has said nothing to anybody, so no other man can tell them apart; each sees the same arrivals in the same order and does the same thing. It ends at the north gate too. But that beginning was supposed to be settled south.

Note what did the work. Not a death: nobody died. What did it was that the other three could not tell a silent man from a slow one, and so could not tell two different worlds apart.

The dusk that settles nothing

However the four have arranged their affairs, there is a way for the sightings to fall that leaves the night two-gated.

Proof — that some beginning is two-gated
  1. Suppose every beginning were one-gated, each already fated by the sightings alone.
  2. Both fates occur: the second demand forces north where all four saw north, and south where all four saw south.
  3. Call two beginnings neighbours when they differ in one man's sighting. Walking from a north-fated beginning to a south-fated one by single changes, there must be neighbours B₀ and B₁ fated north and south, differing only in the sighting of one man p.
  4. Take a fair course from B₀ in which p never stirs; this is permitted, since at most one man may be silent. Being fair, it ends at the north gate.
  5. Run the same course from B₁. Since p never acts and his sighting is the only difference, every other man sees exactly the same arrivals in the same order, so the course ends at the north gate here too.
  6. But B₁ was fated south, and a fated beginning admits no course to the other gate. Hence some beginning is two-gated.∎
Chapter VIII

Deferring the Telling Arrival

The night can start open. The third thing to establish is that it can be kept open: whichever arrival would have settled the matter, there is a way to take it and be open still.

Fix a two-gated standing and an arrival e that can happen at it. We are not going to prevent e — the birds are reliable and prevention is not on offer. The question is only whether it must settle anything when it comes.

Consider every standing reachable while e is held back, and then e allowed at each of them. Suppose every result were settled. Since the standing we began from was two-gated, some results are settled north and some south; so somewhere two of them, one arrival f apart, are settled opposite ways. Two cases, neither of which survives.

If f is another man's business, then by the lemma of separate folds e and f commute, and the two routes end in the same standing — which would then be settled north and settled south at once.

If f is the same man's business, let that man go quiet — not die; go quiet, as a man does whose birds are in the pines — and let the other three run on to a finish, which they must. Now run that same finish with e done first, and again with f then e done first: by the lemma the other three notice nothing, so they finish the same way in the first case and, since f was chosen to flip the matter, the other way in the second. So both gates were reachable from a standing that had already finished, which is impossible.

Deferring the telling arrival

From any two-gated standing, and for any arrival that can happen at it, the night can go on — without that arrival for a while, then with it — and arrive two-gated still.

No single tube is ever the one that has to settle the matter.

Proof — that deferring an arrival always leaves the night two-gated
  1. Let C be two-gated and e an arrival applicable there. Write 𝒞 for the standings reachable from C without allowing e, and 𝒟 for those got by allowing e at each standing of 𝒞. Suppose no member of 𝒟 is two-gated.
  2. Both settlements occur in 𝒟. C is two-gated, so some course from it ends north and another ends south. If such a course never allows e, its standings lie in 𝒞 and allowing e at the end gives a member of 𝒟 settled the same way; if it does allow e, the standing just after is already in 𝒟.
  3. So 𝒞 holds neighbours that split. 𝒞 is connected, so walking from the standing whose image is settled north to the one whose image is settled south gives C₀ and C₁ = f(C₀), one arrival apart, whose images D₀ and D₁ are settled opposite ways.
  4. Case one: f is another man's arrival. By the lemma of separate folds e and f commute, so D₁ = f(D₀). But everything reachable from the north-settled D₀ is settled north, and D₁ is settled south.
  5. Case two: f is the same man's arrival, say p's. Take a course σ from C₀ in which p never stirs and which does finish; permitted, since p may be the one silent man. Call the standing it reaches A.
  6. σ has no man in common with e or f, so by the lemma it may be run from D₀ and from D₁ as well, reaching e(A) and f then e applied to A — settled north and settled south respectively. So A is two-gated, though σ finished there.
  7. Both cases are impossible, so 𝒟 holds a two-gated standing.∎
Chapter IX

What Cannot Be Promised

The pieces fit together into a night. Keep the four men in a queue. Go to the man at its head; if a tube is waiting for him let him take in the one longest aloft, and if none is waiting let him walk to the perch and come back empty-handed, which is always allowed. Choose the stretch of night before that arrival by the deferral, so that the standing afterwards is two-gated again. Send him to the back of the queue and go to the next.

Run that for ever. No man is starved of his turn; every tube sent is eventually the oldest waiting and so is eventually taken in. The night is entirely fair — it could really happen on that hill — and at every stage of it the standing is two-gated, which is to say that nothing has been settled.

What cannot be promised

There is no arrangement the four could have made — none at all, however ingenious — which guarantees that anybody ever goes to a gate.

Four ways of misreading that sentence are worth closing off.

Nobody dies in it. All four are alive at dawn. The one permitted death was spent on the argument, not on the hill. What limits them is not a death but the possibility of one: since a death would look exactly like a slow bird, no man may ever safely treat silence as death, and so no man may ever safely stop waiting.

Nothing goes wrong. No two men go to different gates, because nobody goes anywhere. Only the third demand fails. They are never wrong; they are only never done.

One would have been enough. The demand is not that all four commit together. One man striking his tent would satisfy it, and the fair night just constructed does not manage even that.

It is not about this hill. Nothing used the number four, or the shape of the ground, or the gates being north and south. Any number of men from two upward, any two answers: same argument, same conclusion.

What would have to change is worth naming exactly. That the birds are slow is not the trouble, nor that they come out of order. The trouble is two things together: there is no longest flight, and a dead man looks exactly like a slow bird. Give them a longest flight — let it be known truly that no bird has ever taken more than five hours — and silence of six hours means something; it need not mean death, but it is no longer nothing, and the deferral cannot be run for ever. Or give them some report of who has died, not a reliable one, merely something that eventually stops naming the living wrongly; that is a thin thing to be given and it is enough. Or let the deaths all happen before the evening begins, so that every man who can answer answers all night: then, with more than half of them in that class, they can manage, and nothing they do has to tell a dead man from a slow bird.

None of these is on offer at the foot of this hill. It is worth noticing, before going up it, that the first of them is on offer at the top.

Part Two · Above

The Bandits

Lamportos the chief on the peak with the loot, and three of his men on the slope below him — Shostakos, Peasios and Dolevios — each watching one approach. None of the four may leave his place: the loot cannot be left, and an unwatched approach is an open one. Nobody dies. The flights are short. Some of them lie.

Chapter X

The Two Conditions

Each man sees one part of the hill and forms his own account of what should be done — hold, or scatter. The four accounts must get into four heads unaltered, and that is the whole difficulty, because a liar can send one thing to one man and something else to another.

So take the accounts one at a time. One man — the chief, the man whose account is going round — sends his order to the other three, the guards, and what is wanted of that sending is this.

The two conditions

The first. Every honest guard obeys the same order.

The second. If the chief is honest, every honest guard obeys the order he sent.

If the chief is honest, the second gives the first for nothing. But the chief may be the liar, and then the second says nothing at all — it only ever spoke of an honest chief — while the first still stands. Honest men must end up together even when the man they were listening to told them different things. That asymmetry is the problem.

A liar may do anything: send different orders to different men, send nothing, keep a lie up consistently, or arrange his lies in advance with another liar. Who the liars are is never learned, by anybody on the hill or by the reader, and why they do it is not explained; an arrangement that works only when you can see into men's reasons is not an arrangement.

The chief is a job, not a rank. Run the thing four times, once for each man's own account, and every honest man ends holding the same four accounts, with each honest man's own among them — after which any rule they all apply gives them all the same answer.

Chapter XI

The Ravens Above

The birds are the same birds, but the hops up here are short, direct and clear of the lower gullies, and no bird has ever taken longer than a jar takes to empty. Each post has such a jar. At dusk, before the posts were manned, the four men stood together by the loot and filled their jars at one word; then three went down to their crags carrying theirs, still running. That meeting is the last time they will see one another before dawn. From then on each man simply refills his jar the moment it runs dry. One emptying is a filling: within it, every man who is going to send, sends.

What the birds are assumed to give, above the wall The first. A slip sent arrives, and arrives as written.
The second. The bird shows whose post released it, so no man can be impersonated.
The third. Silence is detectable: if nothing comes from a post within the filling, nothing was sent.

The third is the one that costs something, and it is bought with the short flight and the four jars — which is exactly what the mercenaries below do not have, and why nothing down there can ever be concluded. Keeping jars together among men who lie is itself a problem of this same difficulty. Here it is dodged, not solved: the jars were started together at a meeting that took place before anyone had anything to lie about. The night is short enough that they drift apart by less than a flight, and the flights are short enough that a single emptying bounds them.

Silence is useless unless it means something, or a man could block everything by sending nothing. So it is given a meaning: no slip within the filling counts as hold. Hold is the default — not the wiser answer, the definite one. All the arrangement needs is that the default be fixed and known.

What none of this gives is any check on content. A plain slip says whatever its writer chose to write, and a guard reporting what the chief sent him may write anything he likes. That is the case dealt with first.

Chapter XII

Three Cannot Be Brought to Agree

Take a chief and two guards, one of the three a liar, nobody knowing which. Nothing can be arranged.

Put an honest guard in two situations. In the first, the chief is honest and sends hold to both; the other guard is the liar and reports he sent me scatter. The second condition binds our man: the chief is honest, so he must hold.

In the second, the chief is the liar and sends hold to our man and scatter to the other; the other guard is honest and reports, truthfully, he sent me scatter. Our man has received exactly the same two slips as before. He cannot tell the situations apart, so he holds here too. But the same argument run on the other guard has him scattering — and in that second situation both guards are honest and they have gone two ways.

That argument is persuasive and is not a proof; reasoning of just this shape has settled things that turned out to be false. The result is correct, and the rigorous proof is in Pease, Shostak and Lamport 1980.

Proof — that three cannot be brought to agree, as far as such an argument goes
  1. Suppose an arrangement works for three men with one liar: all honest men obey the same order, and an honest chief is obeyed.
  2. A. Chief honest, sending hold to both guards; the second guard lies, reporting he sent me scatter.
  3. B. Chief the liar, sending hold to the first guard and scatter to the second; the second guard is honest and reports the same words.
  4. The first guard receives the same two things in A and B and nothing else, so he does the same in both. In A the chief is honest, so he must hold; hence he holds in B.
  5. C, the mirror of A: chief honest, sending scatter to both; the first guard lies. By the same argument the second guard scatters in C, and B looks like C to him, so he scatters in B.
  6. In B both guards are honest and have gone two ways, against the first condition.∎
Chapter XIII

Four Men to a Liar

The general rule: to bear a given number of liars there must be more than three times that number of men. One liar wants four men, two want seven, three want ten. The peak has four, so it bears one liar and no more — and a second liar would want three more men than there are.

Why: suppose some method brought three men or fewer to a liar safely through. Then three men, one of them a liar, could each privately play a third of that method's men, no one of them playing more parts than the method can bear liars. The method would keep its promise among the parts, and each real man would simply do what his own parts did — giving three real men agreement against one liar, which Chapter XII says cannot exist.

Nor does it help to ask for less. A party that only needed to act at about the same time, rather than to agree on an answer, would be no better off: rough agreement can be turned back into exact agreement, so the one is exactly as hard as the other. The difficulty was never the precision.

Proof — that fewer than four men to a liar cannot be made to work
  1. Suppose a method brings n men safely through m liars with n ≤ 3m. Call its men parts.
  2. Set three real men, one a liar, to play the parts: the real chief plays the parts' chief and at most m − 1 of its guards, and each real guard plays at most m of its guards. Since n ≤ 3m, they divide so.
  3. Word between two parts played by one man passes in his head; word between parts played by different men goes by bird, and by Chapter XI arrives as sent and correctly attributed.
  4. At most one real man lies, and he plays at most m parts, so at most m parts lie — which the method was assumed to bear. It therefore keeps both conditions among the parts.
  5. By the first condition, all the parts played by one honest real man obey one order, which he obeys; by the second, if the real chief is honest then every honest part obeys his order. So the three real men satisfy both conditions against one liar, which Chapter XII denies.∎
Chapter XIV

The Repeating

Above that bound there is a method, the Repeating. It needs one auxiliary, the greater count: any rule that takes the accounts a guard holds and returns one, such that if most of them say the same thing, that is what it returns. The commonest account, with hold when there is no commonest, will do; so will the middle one, if they can be put in order.

The Repeating, bearing m liars (1) The chief sends his order to every guard.
(2) Each guard takes what he received, or hold if nothing came, and — unless m is zero — acts as chief himself in the Repeating bearing m − 1 liars, putting that order about to the other guards.
(3) Each guard now holds his own account and one from each other guard, and obeys the greater count of them.

One filling for the chief, one more for each liar borne. On this peak, bearing one liar, that is two fillings.

Chief honest. He sends hold to all three guards. In the second filling each repeats what he got: two say hold, the liar says scatter or says nothing. Every honest guard holds hold, hold, scatter — greater count, hold. Both obey the chief. Note that nobody worked out who lied. He was outvoted, which is cheaper.

Chief the liar. He may send three different things: hold, scatter, nothing. All three guards are honest, so each repeats faithfully and all three end holding the same three accounts — and the same rule on the same accounts gives the same answer. They go together. The party has not found the truth; there was none to find. It has been wrong together rather than divided, which is all that was ever available.

The cost is the catch. Each further liar borne adds a filling in which every report is itself put about, so the traffic multiplies as fast as the party grows, and a late report is a report about a report about a report — kept straight only by naming the whole line of men it came down.

What the Repeating gives

Bearing m liars, it meets both conditions whenever there are more than 3m men and at most m of them lie.

Proof — that the Repeating meets both conditions above the bound
  1. Lemma. Bearing m liars, the method meets the second condition if there are more than 2k + m men and at most k liars. By induction on m: for m = 0 an honest chief's order arrives as sent. Assume it for m − 1.
  2. An honest chief sends v to all n − 1 guards. Each honest guard puts v about among n − 1 men bearing m − 1 liars, and n − 1 > 2k + (m − 1), so by the hypothesis every honest guard receives v from every honest guard.
  3. Since n − 1 > 2k, most guards are honest, so v is most of what each honest guard holds and the greater count returns it.
  4. The theorem, by induction on m. If the chief is honest, the lemma with k = m gives the second condition, and the first follows from it.
  5. If the chief lies, at most m − 1 guards lie, and there are more than 3m − 1 > 3(m − 1) guards, so the hypothesis applies to their repeatings: for each guard, any two honest guards end holding the same account of what he put about.
  6. So any two honest guards hold the same accounts in the same places, and the same greater count of them. The first condition holds; the second is vacuous, the chief being a liar.∎
Chapter XV

The Seal Rings

Each man on the peak has a signet with his own device — Lamportos the owl, Shostakos the wolf, Peasios the ship, Dolevios the key. A slip sent under seal is written in wax and stamped; a man passing it on stamps his own beside, so the slip carries the line it came down. hold : owl : wolf is the chief's order as relayed by the wolf.

What a seal is assumed to do (a) An honest man's seal cannot be counterfeited, and tampering shows.
(b) Anyone can tell whose device is whose.

Nothing is assumed about a liar's seal: liars may lend each other their rings. So a line of seals proves what the honest men in it did and nothing about the rest — which is the point, since it means colluding liars are allowed for.

And a seal cannot compel. A man can refuse to pass a slip on; he simply cannot alter it. Withholding survives the seal, and the next method is built around that rather than against it.

Chapter XVI

The Sealed Repeating

Each guard keeps a sheaf: the orders he has properly received, counted as orders and not as slips, since the same order may come down several lines. And the method needs a standing rule — any rule, fixed beforehand, that turns a sheaf into one order, with a sheaf of one giving that one and an empty sheaf giving hold. The middle order will serve.

The Sealed Repeating, bearing m liars (1) The chief seals his order and sends it to every guard.
(2) A guard given a properly sealed order not yet in his sheaf puts it in; and if fewer than m guards' seals stand on it — the chief's not counted — he adds his own and sends it to every guard not already named on it.
(3) When no further slip can come, he obeys the standing rule applied to his sheaf.

Step (3) is a fact and not a judgement: a slip carrying k guards' seals cannot arrive later than the k + 1st filling, so the thing is over at the m + 1st, and silence inside a filling is silence.

Three men are now enough. Chief and two guards, the chief lying, sealing hold to one and scatter to the other: each guard passes his across, so both sheaves hold the same two orders and the same standing rule sends both men the same way. Plain slips could not do this at any price.

And each guard is now holding two orders under the same owl. An honest man's seal cannot be counterfeited, so both could not exist if the chief were honest. Each guard knows the chief lied — and that is all he knows. It tells him nothing about the other guard, and it happened only because the chief sealed two contradictory orders, which no liar is obliged to do. Silence would have cost him nothing.

And any number of liars can be borne. There is no threshold and no proportion to keep: the Sealed Repeating bears as many liars as there are, whatever the size of the party. Only the question gives out — with fewer than two men more than there are liars, there are not enough honest men left for agreement among them to mean anything. Where plain slips bought each further liar with three more men, seals buy him with one more filling.

Proof — that the Sealed Repeating works against any number of liars
  1. Second condition. An honest chief seals one order and sends it to all, and no one can counterfeit his seal, so every honest guard's sheaf holds exactly that order and the standing rule returns it.
  2. First condition. Take the chief to be a liar. It is enough that if an honest guard i has an order in his sheaf, every honest guard j has it too; equal sheaves give equal answers.
  3. If i received it under the chief's seal alone, he sealed it and sent it to every other guard, so j has it.
  4. If it reached i carrying k guards' seals and j is among them, j had it already.
  5. If j is not among them and k < m, then i seals it and sends it to every guard not named on it, j included.
  6. If j is not among them and k = m: the chief is a liar, so at most m − 1 guards lie, so one of the m named on the slip is honest — and he, when he first received the order, sent it to every guard not then named on it, j among them.
  7. Nowhere were the men counted. The bound of Chapter XIII belongs to unsealed slips, not to lying, and the seals dissolve it.∎
Chapter XVII

The Two Halves

Set the two ends of the hill side by side. Above the wall, agreement is had in a few fillings: four men bear one liar by plain slip, three bear any number under seal, and either way the honest men do the same thing. Below, no arrangement whatever guarantees that anybody ever moves. The difference is not that the bandits are cleverer. It is that they have jars to bound the flights between them and the mercenaries cannot.

And each end is short of exactly one distinction, which is the same shape of trouble twice over.

The two things that cannot be settled

Below. A man with an empty perch cannot tell whether the man in that tent is dead or whether the bird is in a pine. So he can never safely stop waiting, and nothing is ever concluded.

Above. A guard holding two accounts of one order that disagree cannot tell whether the chief lied to one of them or the peer is lying to him. So no single man's word can ever be trusted, and nobody ever learns who lied.

Neither distinction is there to be had, and in both cases what is lost is smaller than it looks. Below, nothing goes wrong: the men are never in disagreement, only never finished. Above, nothing is discovered: the men are agreed, and wrong together as readily as right together. The arrangements were not built to tell anybody who failed. They were built so that nobody needs to be told.

The one exception on the whole hill is the chief who seals two contradictory orders and convicts himself, and he need not be so careless.

The ideas, below. No deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system in which even one process may crash. The proof turns on bivalence: a configuration from which both decisions are still reachable. There is always a bivalent initial configuration, and from any bivalent configuration the step that would resolve the indecision can be deferred and then taken without resolving it — so a scheduler can build an admissible run that never decides. Three details carry the weight: the run the proof constructs contains no failure at all, the single permitted fault being spent on the argument; agreement and validity are never violated, only termination is lost; and the result bites even though the requirement is merely that some process decide. The obstruction is not slowness but the inability to distinguish a crashed process from a slow one. Relax it — bound the delays, even only eventually, or supply an imperfect oracle about who has failed — and consensus becomes solvable again; the paper's own §4 relaxes it by letting processes die only before the protocol begins, and gives a protocol that works whenever a majority are sound.

The ideas, above. A commander must get an order to n − 1 lieutenants so that IC1 all loyal lieutenants obey the same order and IC2 they obey his if he is loyal. IC2 gives IC1 only when the commander is loyal, and he may not be: that is the problem. With oral messages no solution exists for three generals against one traitor, because a loyal lieutenant cannot distinguish a lying commander from a lying peer; more generally nothing works below 3m + 1 generals, shown by having three generals each simulate a third of a hypothetical smaller solution, and settling for approximate agreement does not help, since it can be turned back into exact agreement. Above the bound, OM(m) works: the commander sends his value, each lieutenant relays what he got by OM(m − 1), and each takes the majority — one extra round per traitor tolerated. Unforgeable signatures change everything: SM(m) tolerates any number of traitors with no requirement at all on the number of generals, and three generals suffice against one. No assumption is made about a traitor's signature, so signatures prove what the loyal did and nothing about the rest, and a traitor can still withhold a message. What no algorithm gives is identification, the sole exception being a commander who signs two contradictory orders. And all of it rests on A3 — detecting absence — which in practice means bounded message time and clocks kept synchronised, itself a problem as hard as this one.
