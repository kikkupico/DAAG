Schedia and the Odeon

A city with laws of its own, governed from one theatre in which one voice reaches every seat.

Read this first, and the book set in Schedia can be read on its own. It describes the island of Paxos, the city on its eastern arm, and the Odeon where its law is made.

I

The Island of Paxos

Paxos is shaped like a Y. Two arms reach north and face each other across a bay: the western arm holds the Chamber of the Paxon Parliament, the eastern arm the city of Schedia and its Odeon. The arms join at a fork, where the hall of two doors stands, and below the fork the stem widens into the body of the island, the scholars' coast. The island road runs up the stem, forks, and runs out along both arms.

Paxos can read the sun. Unlike Arche in its trading season, the island tells time by day, and the Chamber has a meridian line. Word goes by messenger and by courier along the island's roads, and takes as long as it takes.

II

A City of Its Own

Schedia is a small city on the eastern arm, above a sheltered cove on the bay. Its name means raft. It was founded by settlers from a different mother city, who came by sea and brought their founders' laws with them, and the Parliament's decrees have never run on the eastern arm. That was ordinary Greek practice: the small island of Amorgos held three independent cities, each settled from elsewhere and each with its own laws.

So Paxos has two cities and two law books. Each covers its own city, and the two never contradict each other. There is no quarrel between them for either to settle; Schedia simply keeps its law another way.

III

The Odeon

Schedia is governed from its Odeon, which stands above the town and faces the Chamber across the water. It is the Chamber's opposite. Where the rotunda makes oratory impossible, the Odeon is roofed, raked and aimed at one stage, so that a single voice reaches every seat.

Schedia's legislators keep their law books in the front row of the Odeon, one throne each. One of them at a time speaks from the stage; the others write down what the speaker says; the public, in the seats behind, deal only with the speaker and learn the law from him. The speaker is set apart by the building itself, and that is the one role that distinguishes a legislator here.

When the speaker falls silent, business stops. It starts again only when another legislator takes the stage under a higher number and first gathers what a majority of the front row holds, so that nothing a majority knew is lost. If two claimed the stage at once, nothing could proceed, and that is why every turn on the stage is numbered.
