Probably Up to Datehow likely a reader's copy is the newest, and by how much it may lag

When a reader asks only a few of the libraries that keep a work, how likely is the copy handed over to be out of date, and by how many editions?

On the scholars' coast a library can say probably current, with the odds worked out. It cannot say current.

First, in machine terms

Write to some, read from some

A data item is kept at N replicas. A write is acknowledged once W of them have it, and a read returns the newest of the first R answers.

  • If R + W is more than N, every read meets the latest acknowledged write.
  • Operators often choose smaller numbers, for speed. A read may then return stale data.
  • Probabilistically Bounded Staleness (Bailis et al., 2012) works out how likely that is, how many versions behind, and for how long after the write. It gives a probability, never a promise.

This edition tells it with one work kept at three libraries, a writer, a reader, and couriers who take as long as they take.

Who holds the work

A work and its keepers

A work kept at three libraries has three keepers, and every copy of it on the coast is held by one of them. Each copy is an edition with a tally, the day and hour it was written, and of two editions the larger tally is the newer.

A writer sends a courier to each keeper with a new edition. A keeper that receives it enters it and sends a courier back to say so. When enough keepers have answered, the writer announces the edition and regards it as done. The couriers still on the road keep walking.

A reader writes to all three keepers and takes the newest copy among the first few replies, without waiting for the rest.

So there are two numbers: how many keepers a writer waits for, and how many replies a reader takes. Nothing in the replies says whether the keepers heard from are ones the edition has reached.

Certainty, and its price

How many to wait for

Waiting for all three keepers and asking all three makes the slowest courier decide how soon anything is done. One slow road or one stopped courier holds up everyone.

Waiting for fewer is quicker. The risk is that the keepers the writer waited for and the keepers the reader heard from share no library.

Proof · that R + W > N forces every read quorum to meet every write quorum

Let there be N replicas, a write quorum QW of size W and a read quorum QR of size R, both subsets of the same N replicas. Claim. If R + W > N, the two quorums share a replica.

  1. This is the standard property of strict quorums, which the paper recalls in §2. Let there be N replicas, a write quorum QW of size W and a read quorum QR of size R, both subsets of the same N replicas.
  2. |QW ∪ QR| ≤ N, since both are subsets of a set of size N.
  3. |QW ∩ QR| = |QW| + |QR| − |QW ∪ QR| ≥ W + R − N.
  4. If R + W > N, the right side is at least 1, so the two quorums share a replica. That replica holds the committed write, and a read that returns the value with the greatest version among its responses returns that write or a later one.∎

Why would anyone choose smaller numbers? For speed. The paper observes that real systems with three copies often wait for one and ask one, on purpose: the quicker answers matter more to them than an old copy now and then. On the coast that is the quick way.

The plainest case

When the groups miss each other

Suppose the keepers that answer the writer, and the keepers the reader hears from, fall as if by lot. With three keepers and the quick way, the edition is at one keeper and the reader's keeper is one of the three. The reader misses it two times in three.

With more keepers the odds improve quickly, if the two numbers grow with them. Two groups drawn from a large crowd are very unlikely to share no one.

Proof · that the probability of a read missing the last write is C(N − W, R) / C(N, R)

Let N be the number of replicas, W the write quorum size and R the read quorum size, the W replicas written and the R replicas read each chosen uniformly at random; C(n, r) is the number of subsets of size r of a set of size n. Claim. The probability ps that the read quorum misses the write quorum is C(N − W, R) / C(N, R).

  1. This is Equation 1 of §2.1, with the paper's assumption that the W replicas written and the R replicas read are each chosen uniformly at random.
  2. The read quorum is one of the C(N, R) subsets of size R, each equally likely.
  3. The read misses the write quorum exactly when every one of its R members lies among the N − W replicas not written to. There are C(N − W, R) such subsets.
  4. So ps = C(N − W, R) / C(N, R). For N = 3, R = W = 1 this is 2/3; for N = 100, R = W = 30 it is about 1.88 × 10−6, as the paper states.∎

The odds are excellent for a coast with a hundred keepers of each work. A work on this coast has three, and the paper notes that real systems keep one to three copies. Nothing about the large case helps the small one. But a reader usually asks something narrower than is this the newest?

Leeway in editions

How many editions behind

A reader who will take any of the newest few editions is let down only if the keepers heard from lack every one of them.

Each announced edition went to its own group of keepers, and the reader's group has the same chance of missing each. So the chance of missing the newest two is the chance of missing one, multiplied by itself, and so on. The chance of an old copy falls away fast with every edition of leeway.

Proof · that PBS k-staleness is psk = psk

Let ps = C(N − W, R) / C(N, R) be the probability that a read quorum misses one write quorum, and let k be the number of versions a reader will accept. Claim. A read returns a value more than k versions old with probability psk = psk.

  1. This is §3.1 for quorums that do not expand. A read returns a value within k versions of the latest committed version exactly when its read quorum intersects at least one of the write quorums of the last k committed versions.
  2. Treat the k write quorums as independent, each missed by the read quorum with probability ps = C(N − W, R) / C(N, R).
  3. The read quorum misses all k with probability psk, so psk = psk and the read is within k versions with probability 1 − psk.
  4. With N = 3, R = W = 1, ps = 2/3, so 1 − (2/3)k is 5/9 for k = 2, 0.704 for k = 3, 0.868 for k = 5 and 0.983 for k = 10. With W = 2, ps = 1/3 and the values are 2/3, 8/9 and 0.996 for k = 1, 2 and 5, as the paper's examples give.
  5. For expanding quorums the paper notes this is an upper bound on the probability of staleness, since writes only reach more replicas with time.∎

There is a second gain. A library that need not return the newest edition need not ask as many keepers, so the busiest keeper is asked less often, and the same libraries can serve more readers.

These odds ignore time. They would be the same a moment after the announcement or a week after, and yet the couriers still on the road are still arriving.

Leeway in time

How long until it shows

The edition was sent to all three keepers at the start, so after the announcement it goes on reaching those that had not answered. A reader who comes a while later asks keepers of which more have it.

The visibility of an edition after a while is the chance that a reader who comes that long after the announcement is handed the edition or a newer one.

What decides it is how many keepers hold the edition at that moment. If all three do, no reader can miss it. If only the first to answer does, the odds are those of the quick way. In between, the chance of a miss is the chance that the reader's keepers are all among those that lack it.

Two things to note

A newer edition ends the question

If a second edition is announced before the first has finished arriving, the first no longer matters: a reader handed the second is handed something newer.

Two things to note

Not yet announced is not stale

A reader handed an edition that is written but not yet announced is handed something newer than the announced one, not older.

Proof · that the probability of a stale read t after commit is a sum over how many replicas hold the write

Let qc(t) be the probability that exactly c replicas hold version v at time t after v commits, for c from W to N. Claim. The probability of a stale read at time t is pst = Σc=WN qc(t) · C(N − c, R) / C(N, R).

  1. This is §3.4. Let qc(t) be the probability that exactly c replicas hold version v at time t after v commits, for c in [W, N]; at commit exactly W do with certainty, so qW(0) = 1.
  2. Conditional on exactly c replicas holding v, a read quorum chosen uniformly misses all of them with probability C(N − c, R) / C(N, R), by the argument of Equation 1.
  3. Summing over the possible c: pst = Σc=WN qc(t) · C(N − c, R) / C(N, R). The paper writes this with its distribution Pw(c, t) of the number of replicas reached; its printed Equations 4 and 5 differ from one another in the range of the sum and the sign of the difference, and the sum above is the quantity both mean.
  4. The paper notes that this assumes instant reads and instant commitment after the Wth copy arrives, so it is a conservative upper bound on the true probability of staleness. When the last k writes all happened at the same time the same argument gives the chance of missing all k as the kth power, the paper's ⟨k, t⟩-staleness.∎

For one of the newest few editions, a while after the announcement, a cautious rule of thumb is the same answer multiplied by itself once for each edition. A reader may trade one kind of leeway for the other. What is left to understand is how many keepers hold the edition at a given moment.

The couriers' journeys

The four legs

Four journeys are in every writing and reading, and each takes a time that varies from courier to courier.

The edition's way out from the writer to a keeper. The acknowledgment's way back. The request's way out from a reader to a keeper. The copy's way back to the reader.

A keeper's reply is stale when the reader's request reaches the keeper before the edition does. The keeper then has only the old edition to give. The reader is handed an old copy only if that is so for every one of the first replies taken.

A request that outruns the edition the writer, one keeper and the reader

  • 2 Enough other keepers answer, and the writer announces it.
  • 3 A while later the reader sends a request. Its way out is quick.
  • 4 The request arrives first. The keeper sends back the old copy.
  • 5 The edition arrives, too late for this reader.

The chance cannot be written down in one line, as the chance for editions was, and the paper says so. The moment of announcement depends on the first few journeys out and back, the reader's replies on the first few requests and copies, and the two overlap. So the odds are found by playing the situation over many times, drawing each journey's time from the couriers' logbook, and counting how often the reader is behind.

Proof · that under WARS no read is stale when R + W > N

WARS names the four delays: W, from the coordinator to a replica for a write; A, the acknowledgment back; R, a read request to a replica; S, the response back. Let the write be sent at time 0, reach replica i at wi and be acknowledged at wi + ai; it commits at wt, the Wth smallest acknowledgment time. Claim. If R + W > N, a read begun at any t ≥ 0 after commit is not stale.

  1. This is the remark of §4.1. Let the write be sent at time 0, reach replica i at wi (a draw from W) and be acknowledged at wi + ai (a draw from A). The write commits at wt, the Wth smallest of these acknowledgment times.
  2. The W replicas whose acknowledgments came first each received the write at wi ≤ wt.
  3. A read begins t ≥ 0 after commit, so its request to replica i arrives at wt + t + ri (a draw from R), which is at least wi for each of those W replicas. Such a replica returns the write.
  4. The read takes the first R responses, which are R distinct replicas. If R + W > N they cannot all lie among the N − W other replicas, so at least one is among the W that hold the write, and the read is not stale.∎

The method of §5.1 for the general case: draw N samples from each of W, A, R and S; compute wt, the Wth smallest of the sums W[i] + A[i]; order the replicas by R[i] + S[i]; and count the read stale when each of the first R satisfies wt + R[i] + t ≤ W[i]. Repeating this many times gives the probability for each t.

Is the replay to be trusted? The authors checked it against a working system they set up, with chosen journey times for each leg. Across fifty thousand writings, the predicted chance of seeing the edition after each moment differed from the measured by well under one part in a hundred on average.

The replay, run on real roads

What the logbooks say

The first finding is which journey matters: the edition's way out. The slower and more uneven it is, the longer a reader must wait for near certainty, and its unevenness matters more than its average.

With three keepers and the quick way, when the way out was very quick and even, a reader was handed the edition nine times in ten at the moment of the announcement. When it was ten times slower than the other legs, only four times in ten.

The paper then turned to the logbooks of two large systems, and took four coasts from them.

On the long-tailed coast, taking a second reply cut the wait for near certainty to about a seventh, and writer and reader together were still about four-fifths faster than the fastest way with no chance of a miss.

A reader who returns

Never going backwards

A reader who comes back to a work may not mind a copy that is not the newest. Finding a copy older than the one read last time is another matter.

The editions that will do are the one seen last time and every edition announced since. So this is leeway in editions again: one, plus the number announced between visits. A reader who visits seldom has the widest range and the best odds. A reader who visits all the time has the narrowest.

One sure way

Keep to the same keeper

A keeper's copy only ever moves forward, so a reader who always asks the same keeper is never handed anything older than before, unless that keeper is lost. A library may also limit how often a reader comes, to bring the odds where it wants them.

Proof · that PBS monotonic reads is k-staleness with k = 1 + γgw / γcr

Let a client read a data item at rate γcr and let the item be written, by all clients, at rate γgw. Claim. The probability of violating monotonic reads is psMR = (C(N − W, R) / C(N, R))1 + γgw / γcr.

  1. This is §3.2. Let a client read a data item at rate γcr and let the item be written, by all clients, at rate γgw. Between two successive reads by this client the expected number of versions committed is γgw / γcr.
  2. A read satisfies monotonic reads if it returns the version the client last read, or any newer one. If the last read returned version vi−k and the latest committed is vi, with γgw / γcr versions in between, the acceptable versions are the newest 1 + γgw / γcr.
  3. That is PBS k-staleness with k = 1 + γgw / γcr, so the probability of violating monotonic reads is psMR = (C(N − W, R) / C(N, R))1 + γgw / γcr, Equation 3.
  4. For strict monotonic reads, where the client must read strictly newer data if any exists, the exponent is γgw / γcr. The paper uses expected values of the rates, which in practice are measured as distributions.∎
The limits

What it does not say

A library can tell a reader the odds. Four things it cannot say.

Is the reader's copy current?

The library cannot say. It can say nine times in ten you are handed the newest, and nearly always one of the newest few. With the quick way a reader can be handed an old copy of any age: nothing in the numbers bounds how old, and the paper says so.

What do the odds assume?

That keepers answer as if by lot; that the couriers' journeys are alike and drawn afresh each time, as the logbook records them; that no library is closed and no courier stopped. They also count no festival, no word of mouth and no reader who puts a lagging keeper right, so the real odds are better.

Can a reader tell that a copy is stale?

Not when it is handed over. Afterwards, a late reply with a newer tally shows the copy was behind. Even that can mislead harmlessly: the later reply may carry an edition written after the request, or one not yet announced.

What of readers who need more than a recent copy?

A reader who must see a letter before its reply needs another kind of promise, which the paper leaves aside. It also leaves as a suggestion that a coast could set its two numbers by what it measures, and offer the quickest way that still meets stated odds.

The paper behind this book

Peter Bailis, Shivaram Venkataraman, Michael J. Franklin, Joseph M. Hellerstein and Ion Stoica, Probabilistically Bounded Staleness for Practical Partial Quorums. Proceedings of the VLDB Endowment, Vol. 5, No. 8 (38th International Conference on Very Large Data Bases, Istanbul, August 2012), 776–787. — Partial quorums, PBS k-staleness, t-visibility and monotonic reads, the WARS model, and a Monte Carlo evaluation on production latency data.

The ideas, plainly.

Bailis and his co-authors study data kept on several machines, where a write is acknowledged after W of N machines have it and a read returns after R machines have answered. If R + W exceeds N, a read always meets the latest write. Operators often choose smaller numbers, for speed, so that a read may miss it. The paper asks how often, and shows that a read within k versions of the latest has probability 1 minus p to the k, where p is the chance of a plain miss: the chance of being stale falls exponentially with the leeway allowed. Monotonic reads is the same idea with k set by how often a reader returns compared with how often the data is written.

Staleness in time is harder, because writes keep reaching more machines after they are acknowledged. The paper models the four message delays of a write and a read, W, A, R and S, shows that a read is stale when its request reaches a replica before the write does, and works out the odds by simulation using measured delays. The results are that the delay of the write message, and above all its variance, matters most, and that typically the chance of reading the latest write is high within a short time of its commit, at a latency far below that of a strict quorum.

What the paper offers is therefore a probability and a way to tune it, not a guarantee: the worst case stays unbounded, and the odds rest on assumptions about independence and about the delays staying as they were measured.

The paper has five authors: Peter Bailis, Shivaram Venkataraman, Michael J. Franklin, Joseph M. Hellerstein and Ion Stoica, and all are credited here. Nobody in this telling is named; the writer, the keepers and the reader are told apart by what they do.
