Answering While Cut Off

When one part of a system is cut off from the rest, should it keep answering and risk being wrong, or stay silent until it can be sure? Can it ever have both?

We find out by exploring what the same Greek mercenaries could promise one another about their standing plan when ravens were lost and one tent was cut off.

After Eric A. Brewer, Towards Robust Distributed Systems, 2000 · Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch, Brewer's Conjecture and the Feasibility of Consistent, Available, Partition-Tolerant Web Services, 2002

First, in machine terms

Many copies, one service

A service keeps copies of its data on several machines, and clients may ask any of them.

  • Three wishes. Every read sees the latest completed write, as if there were one copy. Every request to a working machine is answered. And all this holds although the network may lose messages, even every message between two groups of machines.
  • Brewer (2000) conjectured, and Gilbert and Lynch (2002) proved: no service can have all three. When the network splits, a part must refuse some requests or answer from a copy that may be stale.
  • Gilbert and Lynch (2012): this is one case of a wider fact. A promise that nothing goes wrong and a promise that something happens cannot always both be kept.

This edition tells it with four mercenaries, their ravens, and a plan they keep between them.

Four mercenaries are camped at the foot of the hill, one to a tent, under nobody's command.* They must all attack or all hold. Bandits hold the top.

A plan to keep

The standing plan

The four keep a standing plan: the answer they mean to commit to, as things stand. At dusk it is hold.

Any man who sees something on their side of the hill may move the plan to the other answer, and any man may ask which plan stands. Each tent keeps its own note of it. A move is done when the man who made it is told it stands. A question is done when the man who asked is told attack or hold. Nobody commits in this book, and nothing follows from which plan stands.

What is different about these nights? A hawk may take a raven in the folds, and it never arrives. A man waiting on a bird learns it was lost only when longer than any flight has passed and it has not come, and even then learns nothing of what it carried. On some nights every bird between one tent and the other three is lost, for as long as the hawks keep to that fold. That tent is cut off.

On the night this book is about, tent 4 is cut off. Number 4 is alive and well and in the tent. They want to know which plan stands, and they want to know now. What should they be told? And what, exactly, do the four owe a man who asks?

Two debts, and a condition

What the four owe each other

The four owe a man who asks two things, and they must pay while birds can be lost.

First debt

Answering as one

Each tent keeps its own note, but the plan behaves as one plan. Every move and every question, at all four tents, fits one order in which a single record would have given every answer, each taking effect at a moment within its own span, and anything finished before another began coming first.* If Number 2 has moved the plan to attack and been told it stands, a man who asks afterwards must hear attack, or a later move.

Second debt

Every man is answered

A man who asks, or moves the plan, is answered in the end, from what their tent holds. No limit is set on how soon, only that the answer come.

The condition

Birds may be lost

A hawk may take any bird, on any night. The two debts must be paid all the same.

Two ways to answer badly Number 3 asks

Number 2 has moved the plan to attack and been told it stands. Later Number 3 asks which plan stands.

On these nights each tent has a sandglass. The glasses run alike, so a man can measure how long they have waited, but no man can tell what another's glass shows. No wind blows, so a bird that arrives does so within a known flight, as on a windy night once the wind has dropped,* here from the start. The wind comes back at the end of the book.

Answering as one, and answering every man, while birds can be lost. Can the four promise all three?

The camps of this book, stated formally
  1. On these nights there is no wind. A bird that arrives does so within a known span, a flight; a man answers a bird within a known span of his own.
  2. Any bird may be lost, and between a cut-off tent and the rest every bird is lost, for as long as that lasts.

The first result

The cut-off tent

The four cannot promise all three: answering as one, answering every man, and carrying on while birds can be lost. They may have any two. The impossibility was first put as a claim, and only later proved.

The Cut-Off Tent. When birds can be lost, no arrangement of the four can both answer every man and answer as one, even with a known flight and glasses in every tent. Scroll through two nights.

Tent 4 is cut off

2 · The first night

Number 2 moves the plan

Number 2 moves the plan to attack. Every man is answered, so Number 2 is told it stands. Whatever birds go to tent 4 are lost. Nothing else happens anywhere.

3 · The second night

Nobody moves the plan

The same dusk, and nothing happens anywhere for at least as long as the first night's move took. Then Number 4 asks which plan stands, and is answered. Nobody moved it, so a single record says hold.

4 · Put together

Both nights at once

Let the first night happen among the three and the second at tent 4. Nothing reaches tent 4 in either. Number 4 has the same note, the same glass and no bird, and asks after Number 2's move was finished.

5 · So

Number 4 says hold

Number 4 cannot tell this from the second night, so says what they said there: hold. But Number 2's move was finished before they asked, and a single record would say attack. The arrangement does not answer as one.

Would a known flight help, or glasses that run alike? The proof gave the four both. What Number 4 lacks is news, and no amount of waiting brings a lost bird.

Did the proof need tent 4 alone? No. With any division of the four into two sides the argument is the same.

Did Number 4 answer badly? No. On the second night hold is exactly right. Number 4 must say the same on both nights, and the two nights want different answers.

Proof · that no arrangement both answers every man and answers as one

In the paper's terms the tents are nodes, a lost bird is a lost message, a cut-off tent is a partition, answering every man is availability, and answering as one is atomic consistency. Theorem 2. In the partially synchronous model, with a known bound on delivery of messages that are not lost and local timers at the same rate, no algorithm guarantees both availability and atomic consistency in all executions, including those in which messages are lost. The two nights are the paper's executions α and α′₂, and T below is a time measured by the glass.

  1. Suppose some arrangement did both. Put tent 4 on one side and the other three on the other, and lose every bird between them for the whole of what follows.
  2. The first night. The plan stands at hold. Number 2 moves it to attack. Since every man is answered, he is told it stands. Nothing else happens anywhere. Call the time this takes T, by the glass.
  3. The second night. The plan stands at hold. Nothing happens anywhere for at least T. Then Number 4 asks which plan stands, and is answered. Nobody moved it, so answering as one gives hold.
  4. Put them together. Let the first night happen among the three and the second at tent 4, at the same time. The three cannot tell this from the first night, since nothing reaches them from tent 4 in either, so Number 2's move is made and answered as before. Because of the quiet span, Number 4 asks after his move was finished.
  5. Number 4 cannot tell this from the second night. He began with the same note, his glass has run the same, and no bird has reached him in either. So he answers hold.
  6. But Number 2's move was finished before Number 4 asked, so it comes first in any order that answers as one, and a single record would have said attack. That contradicts the supposition.∎

Proof · that the keeper's arrangement answers as one

In the paper's terms the keeper is the designated node of the centralized algorithm, and answering as one is atomic consistency. Claim. Ordering all moves and questions by the moment the keeper dealt with them, every answer is what a single record would say, each takes effect within its own span, and anything finished before another began comes first.

  1. Order all moves and questions by the moment the keeper dealt with them. This is one order, the same whichever tent the man was at.
  2. Every answer is the keeper's, from his one note in that order, so each is what a single record would say.
  3. The keeper deals with a man's business after it began, since the bird was sent then, and before it ends, since the answer waits for the keeper. So each takes effect within its span, and anything finished before another began was dealt with first.∎
A glass to wait

Waiting a glass

The four need not give up answering as one on every night. On a night with no hawks the keeper's arrangement gives answering as one and an answer for every man.

The four want to lose answering as one only while birds are actually being lost, and to answer every man throughout. Because a bird that arrives does so within a known flight, they can.

Number 4's glass

Why does it work? When no bird is lost, no glass runs out, and this is the keeper's arrangement, which answers as one. When a bird is lost, the glass runs out and the man answers anyway, so every man is always answered. On any night on which no bird is lost, every answer is as one. This does not break the Cut-Off Tent: on its first night Number 4's glass runs out and they say hold, as that result says any arrangement answering every man must. What waiting a glass buys is that this happens only on nights when birds are actually lost.

Does it buy anything afterwards? Nothing. If Number 4 moved the plan while cut off, the keeper never heard of it, and nothing here tells them. Long after the hawks have gone, the others are told a plan Number 4 has moved away from. The promise is about whole nights, not about the glasses after a loss.

The hawks left long ago, and a man's note may still be hours behind. Can the four promise that the damage ends?

  1. A man who moves the plan or asks sends a bird to the keeper and turns a glass that runs for two flights and the keeper's time to answer.
  2. If the keeper's answer comes before the glass runs out, he takes it.
  3. If the glass runs out first, he answers from his own note: a question gets the latest plan the keeper has sent him, unless he has himself moved the plan since, in which case it gets his own move; a move is told it stands.

The damage ends

How stale, and for how long

The four can promise the damage ends, and the promise has a span of time in it, fixed in advance and measured by the glass. A loss does its damage only while it lasts and for the span after. How long do the hawks stay? Nobody can promise that. The four promise only how long they take to catch up.

At the camps birds come in any order, so a man cannot count on an earlier bird arriving first. Every bird a man sends the keeper carries, before anything else, all the moves they have made that the keeper has not yet acknowledged, in the order they made them. The promise of the span, stated formally

All the moves and questions can be set in one order P, with t the span, fixed in advance and measured by the glass, in which:

  1. all moves are ordered among themselves, and every question is placed relative to every move;
  2. every question is answered with the plan of the last move before it in P, or hold if there is none;
  3. each man's own business keeps its order in P;
  4. if no bird was ever lost, P answers as one;
  5. and whatever happens, if something was finished, and then a span longer than t passed with no bird lost, and only then something else began, the first comes before the second.

Delayed-t consistency, Definition 3.

The keeper's arrangement with a span, stated formally

At every tent, always: every bird from the keeper carries the plan and its number, and a man keeps the highest-numbered one he has had. Every bird a man sends the keeper carries all his unacknowledged moves, in order.

A question: send to the keeper and wait a glass. If the keeper answers in time, give that answer. If not, give the latest of his own unacknowledged moves, or if there is none, the highest-numbered plan he has.

A move: send to the keeper and wait a glass. It stands when the keeper acknowledges it or the glass runs out, whichever is first. Until acknowledged, send it again, often enough that with no bird lost it reaches the keeper within half of t.

At the keeper: each move that reaches him, taken in its sender's order, changes his note and takes the next number. He sends the plan and number to every tent, again and again to any that has not acknowledged it, often enough that with no bird lost they arrive within half of t.

The modified centralized algorithm, Theorem 4.

Proof · that the keeper's arrangement with a span keeps the promise

In the paper's terms (Definition 3 and Theorem 4), t is the span, fixed in advance and measured by the local timers, and P is an order on all the moves and questions of a night. Claim. On every night the keeper's arrangement with a span answers every man, and admits an order P satisfying clauses (1) to (5) of the promise of the span, stated above.

  1. The order. Order the moves by the keeper's numbers; a move that never reaches him goes after all the numbered ones, in the order it was made. Place each question immediately after the move whose plan it was given, or at the start if it was given hold from dusk. Then every question is answered with the last move before it: clauses (1) and (2).
  2. Each man's own order. A man's moves reach the keeper in the order he made them, since each bird carries all the earlier unacknowledged ones first; so the keeper numbers them in that order, and has numbered them before he answers that man's question. A man never answers with a lower number than one he holds, and his own unacknowledged moves are newer than anything the keeper has sent him. So each man's questions land after his earlier business: clause (3).
  3. No bird lost. Then no glass runs out, every answer is the keeper's, and this is the plain keeper's arrangement, which answers as one: clause (4).
  4. A move, then a span. Suppose a move by Number 4 was finished, then more than t passed with no bird lost. In that span his move reaches the keeper and the keeper's number for it reaches every tent, each within half of t. So any move begun after the span gets a higher number, and any question begun after it is answered with this move or a later one.
  5. A question, then a span. The plan a finished question was given came from the keeper or from a move of the asker's own. Either way, within the span that move is numbered and sent to every tent, and the last step places everything begun after the span later in P: clause (5).∎

The paper's own proof of this is a few lines long, and says nothing about how a man should treat his own moves the keeper has not yet acknowledged; the second step supplies it.

Proof · that in a wind, answering as one cannot even be promised when nothing is lost

In the paper's terms a night is an execution, and in the asynchronous model there is no bound on message delay. Corollary 1.1. No algorithm that guarantees availability in every execution guarantees atomic consistency in every execution in which no message is lost.

  1. Suppose an arrangement answers every man on every night, and answers as one on every night on which no bird is lost.
  2. By the Cut-Off Tent, which holds in a wind as well, there is a night on which a bird is lost and a wrong answer is given. It is given after only so much has happened.
  3. Take that night up to the wrong answer. Let every bird that was lost instead be still in the pines, arriving afterwards; in a wind nobody can tell the difference. Continue the night so that every bird arrives.
  4. On this night no bird is lost, and up to the wrong answer it is the same night, so the wrong answer is still given. That contradicts the supposition.∎
Four questions

What being cut off does not say

Is this the camps' impossibility?

No. With one man who may fall silent and no longest flight, the four cannot be sure of ever committing.* That has the same shape as this result: something that must never go wrong, and something that must in the end happen, which cannot both be promised. But neither result gives the other. There, no bird is lost and one man may stop. Here, no man stops and birds are lost instead.

Is it about a dead man?

No. A man who has stopped owes no one an answer. Number 4 is alive and wants to know. That is why the question has to be put to tent 4 at all.

Does it cover everything the four know?

No: a thing that needs no news is outside it. Which number each tent bears never changes, and any man, cut off or not, can say so and be right. The result is about a plan that can be moved, where one tent needs news of the others.

Must the four choose once for all?

No. They may answer as one for the standing plan and answer at once for something that matters less, or answer questions from their own notes while moves wait for the keeper. Each such division gives up a little of both, and each is still bound by the Cut-Off Tent wherever a man answers from a note that may be behind.

A service cannot be consistent, available and partition-tolerant at once.

Since networks do partition, the real choice comes when one does: refuse some requests to keep every answer consistent, or answer every request and let the copies diverge.

The ideas, plainly. In 2000 Eric Brewer conjectured that a shared-data system can have at most two of three properties: consistency, availability, and tolerance of network partitions. Gilbert and Lynch made the conjecture precise and proved it. Consistency is atomicity, or linearizability: the system behaves as if there were one copy of the data. Availability means every request received by a working node eventually gets a response. Partition tolerance means these hold even though the network may lose any messages, including every message between two groups of nodes.

The proof is a single indistinguishability argument. Cut the nodes into two groups and lose every message between them. A write completes in one group; later, a read arrives in the other. That group cannot tell this execution from one with no write, so if it answers at all, it is wrong. Timers and a known delivery bound do not help, because the missing information cannot arrive.

Any two of the three can be had. A single designated node gives consistency always and availability whenever there is no partition. With timers and a delivery bound, a node can time out and answer locally only when messages are actually lost, and Delayed-t consistency bounds how long stale answers persist once the partition heals. Without a delivery bound, even that is impossible.

CAP is one case of a wider fact about unreliable systems: a safety property and a liveness property often cannot both be guaranteed. Real systems divide the trade-off by data or by operation.
