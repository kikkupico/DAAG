Ordering Without Clocks

If no two computers agree on the exact time, how can they agree on what happened first? And when two events happen far apart, how can anyone tell whether one caused the other, or if they happened completely independently?

To find out, we travel to an ancient Greek island where three trading houses must keep their trades in order—even when heavy clouds make their sundials useless.

First, in machine terms

Many computers, no shared clock

When computers work together over a network, they often need to agree on the exact order in which things happened—like which of two database updates came first, or whose request should be handled next.

If every computer could look at the same clock on the wall, settling the order would be easy. Instead, each machine has its own internal clock. Those clocks slowly drift apart, and messages travelling across a network take unpredictable amounts of time to arrive. Two classic ideas let machines agree on an order without a shared clock:

  • Logical clocks (Lamport, 1978): Each machine keeps a simple counter and attaches that number to every message it sends. By adjusting their counters whenever messages arrive, machines guarantee that a cause always gets a smaller number than its effect.
  • Vector clocks (Fidge and Mattern, 1988): Instead of a single number, each machine keeps a list of counters—one for every machine in the network. This extra detail reveals exactly which events could have influenced one another, and which happened completely independently.

Logical clocks give every machine the same shared order of events. Vector clocks also reveal whether two events actually knew about each other. We explore both through the story of three trading houses on an island whose sundials have gone dark.

The ring road of Arche

Three houses, one narrow road

House 1, House 2, and House 3 sit along the coast of the island of Arche, separated by a steep central mountain. News travels between them only on written slips of paper, carried by messengers along a single coastal ring road.

No overtakingBy the law of the narrow road, no messenger may pass another. Slips sent from one house to another always arrive in the exact order they were sent.
Unpredictable travel timeEvery slip eventually arrives, but a journey might take an hour or a day—nobody can put an upper limit on how long a messenger will take.
No shared clockDuring the cloudy trading season, shadows vanish from the sundials, and each counting room's indoor water-clock keeps its own separate time.*
The question

Which request was placed first?

House 3 has grain in its storehouse—just enough for one of the other houses, though both need it. House 2 writes a request for the grain and sends a messenger down the road. Shortly after, knowing nothing of House 2's order, House 1 sends a messenger with a request of its own.

The houses have agreed on a fair rule: whichever request was placed first must be filled first. So House 3 has to figure out which request was written earlier. The most obvious way is simply to see whose messenger reaches the gate first.

The gate of House 3 who gets there first?

In this scene, House 2 always writes its request first. Choose whose messenger has the quicker legs.

Judging by who reaches the gate first simply rewards whichever house hired the faster runner, regardless of who actually wrote their request first. What if House 3 asked each house to write the time on its slip? That fails too: it would only compare readings from two indoor water-clocks that were never synchronized. Every obvious way of settling who came first relies on a shared clock that the island does not have.

Yet the houses do not need to give up on fairness. They have been asking a question that nobody on Arche can answer—what happened first under the sun?—while right beside it lies a simpler, more practical question that every house can answer.

A simpler question

What could have influenced what

That simpler question came from the chief accountant at House 1. Without the sun, no house can tell which of two independent requests happened first in absolute time. Fortunately, fair trade doesn't need absolute time—it only requires that a request never be filled before an earlier one that could have led to or influenced it.

And on Arche, tracking what could have led to what is completely straightforward, because news only travels by written slip. The accountant boiled this down to three clear rules. One event came before another in exactly these three cases:

Rule one

Same house, higher on the scroll

If two events happen in the same house, the one written higher up on its account scroll came before the one written below it.

Rule two

Sending comes before receiving

When one house sends a slip and another house receives that exact slip, sending the slip always came before receiving it.

Rule three

Chains of events count

If a first event came before some middle event, and that middle event came before a second, then the first came before the second.

If none of the three rules connects two events, they are unrelated (or concurrent). That does not mean they happened at the exact same instant. It simply means neither event could have known about or influenced the other, because no chain of messengers connects them. Even if the sun saw one happen before the other, nothing in the houses' records can prove which came first.

These three rules give us a solid definition of order, but clerks in a busy counting room cannot see all three scrolls at once. They need a simple way to calculate this order on the fly—so the accountant gave every counting room a single rising number.

A counter that respects every chain

The tally

That number is called the tally. Every counting room keeps a tally of its own, writes its current tally on every slip it sends out, and raises the number using two simple rules. Scroll slowly to watch the tallies rise across the morning's work.

1 · Start

The same morning, before numbering

Here are ten entries across the three scrolls, connected by three messenger slips on the road. None of the entries have a tally number yet.

2 · Rule one

Every new entry raises the tally

Whenever a house records an entry on its own scroll—including sending a slip—it increases its tally by at least 1.

3 · Rule two

The tally rides on every slip

When House 1 sends a slip to House 2, it stamps the slip with its current tally: 2.

4 · Rule two

The receiver jumps past the slip

House 2's tally was at 1 when the slip marked 2 arrived. To make sure receiving gets a higher number than sending, House 2 jumps its tally past the slip to 3 before logging the arrival. A tally never goes backward.

5 · On it goes

Each slip pulls the next house forward

House 3 stood at 2 when it received a slip marked 4, so its entry becomes 5. Later, House 1 stood at 2 and received a slip marked 6, jumping its tally straight to 7.

6 · The promise

Along any chain, the numbers strictly rise

Follow the red chain of cause and effect from house to house: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. Because of the two rules, any event that could have led to another is guaranteed to have a smaller tally.

7 · The catch

A smaller number does not prove a connection

Look at the two circled entries. No chain of slips connects them. Even though House 3's entry happened later in the morning by the sun, it carries 2 while House 2's carries 3. The tally assigns a sequence even where two events had nothing to do with each other.

So the tally respects every real chain of slips, but looking at two tally numbers alone cannot tell you whether the events were actually connected or completely unrelated. Our two competing requests for House 3's grain are just such an unrelated pair—and House 3 still needs a fair way to decide which one to fill first.

One sequence for everyone

Breaking ties

Even if House 3 knows that two requests are unrelated, it still has to pick one to fill first. To avoid arguments, the houses need a single global sequence that decides between every pair of events, that every house calculates the exact same way, and that never contradicts a chain of slips.

House 1's accountant created one with a simple tie-breaker. The rule of precedence: first compare the tallies, putting the smaller tally first; if the tallies are tied, the lower house number goes first—House 1 before House 2, and House 2 before House 3. Precedence does not magically discover which of two unrelated requests was written first under the sun—nothing in the houses' scrolls could. Instead, it replaces an unanswerable question with a consistent rule that every house reads the exact same way. And it is fair in the only way the houses can verify: whenever one request could have led to another, they are filled in that exact order, and no house wins simply by hiring a faster runner.

Now that the houses can agree on a single sequence of events without a shared clock, they can tackle a harder test: sharing a storehouse that only one house may use at a time, with no gatekeeper in charge to hand out turns.

Proof · precedence is a total order that preserves cause and effect

Write a → b when event a came before event b, and C(a) for the tally of a. The two rules of the tally guarantee the Clock Condition: if a → b, then C(a) < C(b). For an event a at house i and an event b at house j, we say a precedes b (a ⇒ b) if either (1) C(a) < C(b), or (2) C(a) = C(b) and i < j. Claim. ⇒ is a strict total order, and a → b implies a ⇒ b.

  1. Every pair can be compared (Total comparability). Take any two distinct events a and b. If their tallies differ, clause (1) decides which goes first. If their tallies are equal, they cannot have occurred at the same house (since Rule 1 gives every subsequent event at the same house a strictly higher tally). Thus they belong to different houses with different house numbers, and clause (2) decides between them.
  2. No loops (Antisymmetry and transitivity). Ordering events by the pair (C(e), house) is a standard dictionary (lexicographical) order of two numbers, which is always strictly total and transitive.
  3. Respects cause and effect (Consistency with causality). If a → b, the Clock Condition guarantees that C(a) < C(b). Precedence places a before b by clause (1) alone, without needing the tie-breaker. Hence a → b always implies a ⇒ b.∎
Putting the sequence to work

One storehouse, no gatekeeper

The island's common storehouse is carved into the rock beside the ring road, and its loading bay is narrow: only one house may load goods inside it at a time.

A gatekeeper at the door who simply let in whoever arrived first would be fooled just like House 3's gate was, because a nearby request can beat an earlier one travelling a longer stretch of road. So there is no gatekeeper. Instead, each house keeps its own waiting list (or queue) of requests, sorted by precedence, and follows five simple rules. Scroll to watch what happens when two houses ask to use the storehouse at once.

1 · Quiet

Three waiting lists, all empty

Every house keeps a waiting list of its own. Right now, nobody is asking to use the storehouse.

2 · To request

Two houses ask at the same time

House 1 logs a request under its tally of 2, puts it on its own waiting list, and sends copies to both other houses. At the same moment, knowing nothing of this, House 3 does the exact same thing under its own tally, which is also 2.

3 · Not yet

Being first on your own list is not enough

House 3's request currently sits first on House 3's own waiting list—yet House 3 still may not enter the storehouse. An earlier request might still be on the road right now, inside a messenger's pouch.

4 · On receiving a request

Every waiting list comes out the same

Each house adds the incoming request to its waiting list. Because both tallies tie at 2, House 1's request goes ahead of House 3's by house number—on all three lists.

5 · The answer

Each house sends back a reply

Whenever a house receives a request, it advances its tally and sends back an acknowledgment. Every reply is guaranteed to carry a tally higher than 2.

6 · To enter

House 1 loads its goods

A house may enter the storehouse only when its request stands first on its list and it has received a message with a higher tally from every other house. House 1 meets both rules and goes inside. House 3 has heard from everyone too, but stands second on its list, so it waits.

7 · To release

House 1 finishes up

When House 1 finishes loading, it removes its request from its own waiting list and sends a release slip to both other houses.

8 · On receiving a release

House 3's turn

Each house crosses House 1's request off its list. House 3's request now stands first, and since it has already heard from every other house, it can safely enter.

9 · Why it is safe

Never two houses at once

Why couldn't House 3 enter too early? To enter, House 3 needed a slip from House 1 carrying a tally above 2. On a road where no messenger passes another, that slip could only arrive after House 1's tally-2 request—so House 1's request was already sitting on House 3's list, ahead of its own.

With these rules, the houses safely share a storehouse using an order they all agree on, with no shared clock and no gatekeeper. Still, that order is built entirely from road slips—and a shortcut across the island shows how far it can drift from what the sun actually saw.

Proof · two houses never use the storehouse at the same time

Let Ri be the request of house i, and C(Ri) the tally under which it was written. Rule 5 states that house i may enter the storehouse only when Ri stands first in its local queue (ordered by ⇒) and it has received from every other house at least one message carrying a tally strictly greater than C(Ri). Messages between any pair of houses arrive in the exact order sent (FIFO). Claim. No two houses ever hold the storehouse at the same time.

  1. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that House 1 and House 3 are both inside the storehouse at the same time on requests R₁ and R₃. Since precedence is a total order, one request must precede the other; assume R₁ ⇒ R₃.
  2. By Rule 5, before House 3 could enter, it must have received from House 1 some message carrying a tally T > C(R₃) ≥ C(R₁).
  3. Because a house's tally only increases over time, House 1 must have sent its request R₁ (at tally C(R₁)) to House 3 before sending any message with the higher tally T. And because no messenger overtakes another on the coastal road, slips from House 1 to House 3 arrive in FIFO order.
  4. Therefore, House 3 must have received R₁ before receiving the message with tally T. Since House 1 is still in the storehouse and has not yet sent a release, R₁ was sitting in House 3's queue when House 3 entered.
  5. Because R₁ ⇒ R₃, R₃ could not have been first in House 3's queue. This contradicts Rule 5, proving that both houses cannot be inside at once.∎
What the tally cannot see

Shortcut over the mountain

That shortcut goes straight over the mountain. A goatherd who knows the smugglers' tracks can scramble across in a couple of hours, with a bit of luck. Of course, the same goatherd might get lost for two days—or never come down at all—which is why no house trusts official business to the mountain path.*

Still, word of mouth can travel with a goatherd, who carries no official slip. Suppose a trader at House 3 closes a sale after a busy morning and pays a goatherd to carry the news across the mountain to House 1, while the official slip takes the long way around the coastal road.

A sale at House 3 and a lucky goatherd

House 3 has had a busy morning (high tally). House 1 has had a quiet one (low tally).

By its own rules, the tally system has done nothing wrong. Its rules only track official slips, and the goatherd carried none. As far as the houses' scrolls can tell, the sale at House 3 and the follow-up request at House 1 are completely unrelated—even though the sale directly caused the request, and ends up with a larger tally number than its own effect!

Outside shortcuts like the goatherd are one blind spot of the tally. But the clerk at House 2 has noticed another blind spot—and this one happens right on the coastal road itself.

Connected, or made in ignorance?

One number for each house

The clerk of House 2 has a practical doubt that a single tally number cannot settle. She looks at two entries marked 4 and 5. Did the event at 4 lead to the event at 5, or was each written in complete ignorance of the other? With single numbers, both possibilities look identical.

A single counter cannot tell them apart because it squashes everything a house has heard into one number. When House 2 raises its tally past a slip's number, it forgets whose events it just learned about. Remembering that history takes one counter per house. So each house keeps a column of three rows (a vector clock): row by row, it records the latest count of entries it has heard about from House 1, House 2, and House 3. Three rules keep the column up to date:

Rule one

Count your own entries

Whenever a house logs an entry of its own—including sending a slip—it adds 1 to its own row in the column.

Rule two

Send the whole column

Every slip is stamped with the sender's complete three-number column as it stands right after that entry.

Rule three

Keep the larger of each row

On receiving a slip, a house compares its column with the slip's column, keeps the larger number in each row, and then adds 1 to its own row.

The same morning, with columns click two entries

The dots show single tallies. Click any two entries to compare their full columns row by row. Try comparing the 2 on House 3's line with the 3 on House 2's.

Comparing two columns row by row settles the clerk's doubt completely. One entry came before another if and only if every number in its column is less than or equal to the corresponding row in the other's column (and the two columns aren't identical). Two entries are unrelated if and only if one column is larger in one row while the other column is larger in another. Where a single tally could not tell coincidence from cause and effect, the column proves which is which.

With columns, every connection that travels by slip is laid bare. Only what never went by slip remains unseen—and that brings us back one last time to the goatherd.

Proof · the column detects cause-and-effect and unrelated events exactly

House i keeps a vector Vi with one slot for each house. Rule 1: on every local event (including a send), it increments Vi[i]. Rule 2: every message carries the sender's vector right after that increment. Rule 3: upon receiving a vector U, house j sets Vj[k] = max(Vj[k], U[k]) for every row k, and then increments Vj[j]. Let V(a) be the vector recorded at event a. We write V(a) ≤ V(b) when every row of V(a) is at most the corresponding row of V(b), and V(a) < V(b) when V(a) ≤ V(b) and the two vectors are not equal. Claim. a → b if and only if V(a) < V(b); consequently, a and b are unrelated (a ∥ b) if and only if neither V(a) ≤ V(b) nor V(b) ≤ V(a).

  1. Forward direction (If a → b, then V(a) < V(b)). If a → b, there is a chain of local events and messages leading from a to b. By Rules 1–3, every step along this chain never decreases any row, and the final step at b strictly increases b's own row. Thus V(a) < V(b).
  2. Reverse direction (If a ↛ b, then V(a) ≰ V(b)). Suppose a occurs at house i and did not come before b (a ↛ b). Then no chain of slips carrying news of a has reached b. Because row i only increases at house i (by Rule 1) and is only copied to other houses via messages (by Rules 2–3), the value of V(b)[i] can only reflect events at house i that came before b. Since a is not among them, V(b)[i] < V(a)[i], which means V(a) ≰ V(b).
  3. Conclusion. Combining both directions shows that a → b if and only if V(a) < V(b), and two events are unrelated (a ∥ b) if and only if neither column is less than or equal to the other.∎
The goatherd again

Whose order do the houses keep?

Because the three-number column is built solely from official slips, the goatherd escapes it too. Catching outside shortcuts like the goatherd requires numbers that rise along every path in the physical world—slip or no slip—and only physical clocks that tick in step with real time can promise that.

In principle, such synchronized physical clocks can be built: each house keeps a steady clock that barely drifts, every slip carries the sender's clock reading, and any house that falls behind nudges its clock forward. As long as the houses' clocks never disagree by more than the goatherd's fastest sprint across the mountain, the goatherd can never arrive at a house at an earlier timestamp than when he set out.

On cloudy Arche, however, water-clocks drift and no one can guarantee a maximum travel time for messengers along the road. So the houses rely on the tally and the column—and it is worth stating plainly what each one can and cannot see:

The tally

Respects every chain of slips

Gives every house the exact same global sequence using just one number per event, but cannot tell by itself whether two events were connected or unrelated.

The column

Reveals every chain of slips

Distinguishes connected events from unrelated ones with complete precision, at the cost of keeping one number for each house.

Neither

Sees what never went by slip

Both tools track only the messages inside the system. Matching the order of the outside world requires synchronized physical clocks.
