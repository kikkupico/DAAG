The Foot of Mount Phyle

Four mercenaries camped at the foot of a besieged hill, out of sight of one another, with word between them only by raven.

Read this first, and any book set at the foot can be read next, in any order. It describes the hill, the four camps and the ravens between them, which every book here takes for granted, and it says what each book changes.

I

The Hill

Mount Phyle is the limestone massif at the middle of Arche, broad enough to block every line of sight across the island. Eight wooded spurs run down from its top, with gullies between them, and the hill's own crows roost in the pines of the folds. On the very top is the crown: a small ruined ring wall, solid all round, with no way through.

The hill is under siege. The crown above is held by bandits with their loot, and the four at the foot have been hired to clear them out. The siege has no end in any book: no assault is made, no wall is carried, and nobody surrenders. The books describe arrangements, not events, and nothing follows from any decision anyone reaches.

II

The Camps

Four small tents stand at the foot of four gullies, at the four quarters of the hill, one mercenary to a tent. The tents are numbered 1 to 4, and each mercenary goes by his tent's number: Number 1 to Number 4. They are alike. Each was hired separately and is paid separately; none commands another, and none of them lies.

Between any two tents stand two wooded spurs, so no man can see another. They light no fires and sound no horns, which would tell the hill where they are and that they are talking. And no man leaves his quarter to carry word, because leaving it is the very thing they must agree on.

Their question has two answers: which approach, north or south, the two ways up the hill to the crown's wall. All four must go up by the same one, since two at each is no assault. The mercenaries do not know where the bandits' posts lie. They know only that the bandits are few and scattered, too far apart to see or help one another, so the way to win is to beat them one at a time, all four together on whatever they meet first. Two pairs, one on each approach, would each risk meeting more than a pair can handle. With nothing to tell the two approaches apart, the four must still pick the same one, or not go at all. Going is a definite act. A man goes by striking his tent and taking the path round the slope to his approach, and once he has done it his quarter is open behind him and he will not be back before dawn. What he thinks before that does not count.

Any of them may be killed. A dead man's tent simply falls silent. Nobody succeeds him and no bird goes out to report it, so to the others a dead man looks exactly like a live one whose bird has not yet come.

III

Ravens

Word passes on the mountain only by raven. A man writes on a strip, rolls it into a bronze tube, ties it to the leg of a bird belonging to the tent or post he is writing to, and lets it go. What a bird carries is exactly what was written, and it is known whose it is.

How long a bird takes is where the books at the foot differ. On the first nights a bird may sit among the crows of the folds for an hour or for the night: there is no longest flight, and there is no timer at any tent.

A goatherd crossing the mountain by the smugglers' tracks may pass between the tents, but he takes as long as he takes and may never come down at all. A strip given to him would only be one more slow bird, less sure than a raven, and nothing on the hill either needs him or is changed by him. The camps carry nothing from one house of the ring road to another.

Where the allegory leaks

Real ravens do not carry messages home; homing doves do, and a homing bird flies one way only, to its own loft. So each man really does need birds belonging to the tent or post he writes to, and his stock of them is limited. This makes the men's lot harder than the model's, which allows as many messages as a protocol cares to send. It does not help them, and nothing in any book turns on it. The birds here are called ravens and fly as many times as they are needed.

IV

One Change a Night

Every book at the foot keeps the same hill and the same four at the same tents. What changes from book to book is one condition of the night, never the place.

The first nights: birds with no longest flight, and one man may die. Nothing else.
Windy nights: while a wind blows, a bird may sit it out for any time; the wind always drops in the end, and in still air every bird arrives within a glass. On these nights each tent has a glass, turned as the last light left the crown.
Nights of lost birds: no wind, and a bird that arrives does so within a known flight, but a hawk may take any bird. The four keep a standing approach that any of them may move or ask after.
Nights of the coin: each man carries Attic silver and, when he cannot settle, tosses a coin alone in his tent.
Nights of the slate: each tent keeps a slate, chalking the tents its man believes dead, often wrongly.

Whatever the night, nothing is ever settled by force, and the arrangements are judged only by what they guarantee.
