What You Can Promise Alone

When a machine cannot stop to check with other replicas on every transaction, what guarantees can it still keep on its own? Can it hide half-made changes? Show a reply only after the message it answers? Prevent two people from taking the last item?

We find out by exploring what the librarians of the scholars' coast of Paxos could still promise their readers on days when the couriers between libraries stopped running.

After Peter Bailis, Aaron Davidson, Alan Fekete, Ali Ghodsi, Joseph M. Hellerstein and Ion Stoica, Highly Available Transactions: Virtues and Limitations, 2014

First, in machine terms

Replicas that must always answer

On Mount Phyle,* we saw that a partitioned replica cannot both answer every request and guarantee linearizability (strong consistency). Does giving up linearizability mean giving up transactions and isolation altogether?

  • Highly Available Transactions (HATs) (Bailis et al., 2014) sort the classic database isolation levels and distributed session guarantees by whether a replica can keep them while cut off from other replicas for an arbitrary length of time.
  • Some guarantees can be kept by any replica: `Read Committed`, `Monotonic Atomic View` (showing all or none of a transaction's writes), `Item/Predicate Cut Isolation`, `Monotonic Reads`, and `Writes Follow Reads`.
  • Some require sticky availability (the client always talks to the same replica): `Read Your Writes`, `PRAM`, and `Causal Consistency`.
  • Some are provably impossible without coordination: preventing `Lost Updates` and `Write Skew` (which rules out `Snapshot Isolation` and `Serializability`), and any recency bound on reads.

We tell this story in the reading rooms of the scholars' coast, on a day when the couriers between libraries have stopped.

The reading rooms

A librarian, a reader, and no courier

Every library on the scholars' coast has a reading room where readers come to consult works, leave corrections, or borrow copies. News of what other libraries have done arrives by courier—and couriers can be stopped at any time, cutting one library off from the rest for as long as the stoppage lasts.*

Answered from the shelfA librarian must answer every reader who walks through the door using only what is on the library's own shelves.
For as long as they likeCouriers between libraries may be stopped for hours, days, or months.

The standard

The couriers stop

What does it mean for a librarian to make a promise alone (`high availability`)? It means that every reader who can reach an open library gets their business completed, no matter which couriers are stopped or how long they stay stopped.

A librarian who has to wait for a majority of the coast (as the Chamber and the Tholos do) cannot answer when cut off. Nor does it count to say I am probably right, as in Probably Up to Date. Here we ask which hard, deterministic promises hold even on the day every courier on the road is stopped.

Furthermore, simply replying I abort your request does not count as availability! If refusing every visitor counted as answering, a locked door would be “available.” Under transactional availability, every visit that reaches an open library must genuinely complete (`commit`), unless the reader himself chooses to cancel it.

What a reader might want

A reader's visit

A reader's business at a library is a visit (`a transaction`): during a single visit, a reader may consult several works and leave corrections to several works before stepping out the door.

When we test every classic transactional and session guarantee against a courier stoppage, they sort cleanly onto three shelves:

Kept alone, by any library

Whatever the couriers do (`HAT`)

No correction is shown half-made (`Read Committed`). Corrections bound together are seen all or none (`Monotonic Atomic View`). Reading the same work twice in a visit gives the same answer (`Cut Isolation`). No answer is older than a previous visit's (`Monotonic Reads`), and no reply is shown before its letter (`Writes Follow Reads`).

Kept if the reader stays with one library

The same door every time (`Sticky`)

A returning reader sees the corrections they themselves left (`Read Your Writes`), in the order they left them (`PRAM`), and combined with everybody else's letters and replies (`Causal Consistency`).

Kept by no librarian alone

Needs news from elsewhere (`Unavailable`)

Two readers never borrow the last copy (`Lost Update`), two readers never leave a reference shelf bare (`Write Skew`), and the copy handed over is guaranteed to be the newest—or within any fixed time of the newest (`Recency / Linearizability`).

Let us walk through each shelf in turn and see how a librarian keeps the promises on the first two shelves without waiting for a courier—and why no librarian can keep the promises on the third.

The most basic promise

No half-made corrections

Suppose a scholar sits in a reading room revising three works during a single visit. While he is still writing the second revision—and before he has decided whether to finish the visit or tear up his notes—another reader walks in and asks for the first work.

The librarian must never show another reader an unfinished or abandoned revision (`dirty read`). Keeping that promise (`Read Committed`) requires zero help from other libraries: the librarian simply holds a visitor's revisions aside on a desk until the visitor declares the visit complete, and only then places them onto the shelves (or discards them if the visitor withdraws).

A second, smaller promise

Every correction in a visit carries the same tally

What if two scholars at two cut-off libraries both revise Work X and Work Y in a single visit? If one scholar's revision won on X while the other's won on Y, the shelves would end up with a Frankenstein mixture (`dirty write` / `G0`) that neither scholar intended. By stamping every correction in a visit with a single shared tally (the visit's timestamp plus the visitor's ID) and resolving clashes by largest tally,* whichever visit has the larger tally wins on both X and Y.

Proof · that Read Uncommitted and Read Committed are achievable with high availability

High availability: every client that can reach a non-failing replica gets a response, without that replica contacting any other. Read Uncommitted (Adya's PL-1) forbids G0: a cycle in the graph with an edge from Ti to Tj when Tj overwrites a write of Ti. Read Committed (PL-2) also forbids reading aborted or intermediate writes (G1a–c). Claim. Both can be provided with high availability.

  1. This is §5.1.1. Read Uncommitted (Adya's PL-1) requires that writes to each object are totally ordered and that the order is consistent across objects: the graph of transactions with an edge from Ti to Tj when Tj overwrites Ti's write must have no cycle (no G0). Mark every write of a transaction with one timestamp unique across transactions (a client's identifier combined with a sequence number) and let each replica apply last-writer-wins by timestamp.
  2. Then for any two transactions T1 and T2 that write to the same two items x and y, all of T1's writes carry the same timestamp, and so does all of T2's; whichever has the larger timestamp wins on both. It cannot happen that wx(1) precedes wx(2) while wy(2) precedes wy(1). No replica contacts any other to decide, so every replica can respond.
  3. Read Committed (PL-2) adds that no transaction reads uncommitted or intermediate versions (Adya's G1a–c). A client buffers its writes until it commits, or sends them to a server that does not reveal them to other readers until it is notified of the commit. Then a reader is never shown a write of a transaction that later aborts, nor an intermediate write.
  4. This provides no recency or monotonicity, and the paper notes it satisfies the implementation-agnostic definition rather than the lock-based behaviour of centralised implementations.∎
All or none

Corrections bound together

Suppose a scholar revises two works during one visit: he updates Work X (a map of the strait) and also updates Work Y (a sailing guide that refers to X). When couriers carry those two revisions across the coast, the courier carrying X might reach a library before the courier carrying Y.

If a reader saw the new X and then read the old Y, he would see a broken half-state (`fractured read`). To prevent that (`Monotonic Atomic View`), the scholar binds the revisions of a visit under one cord: each revision carries the visit's tally and the manifest of all works revised in that visit. A library does not place an incoming revision onto its public shelf the moment it arrives; it holds the revision in the back room until it knows that every revision under that cord has reached all the libraries (`pending stable`), and until then it continues to hand readers the older editions!

A reader asks for X, then for Y the correction to X has arrived at Library 1; the correction to Y is still on the road to Library 2

The reader has asked for nothing yet.

Holding back is not waiting

The librarian never makes a reader wait; he only delays unveiling new arrivals

Notice the trick that makes this highly available: when a reader asks for X or Y while the couriers are stopped, the librarian answers immediately using the older, pre-cord edition that is already complete on the shelf. Once every library has received the full cord, the new editions become eligible to show; and once a reader sees any work from a cord, his reading note records that cord's tally so any other library he asks in the same visit also shows him at least that cord's edition.

Sketch · that Monotonic Atomic View is achievable with high availability

Monotonic Atomic View (MAV): once some effect of a transaction Ti is observed by a transaction Tj, all later reads of Tj observe all of Ti's effects, or later versions. A write is pending stable once every replica for the transaction's final writes has received them. Claim. MAV can be provided with high availability.

  1. This is the algorithm sketched in §5.1.2. MAV requires that once some effect of a transaction Ti is observed by Tj, all later reads of Tj observe all of Ti's effects (or later versions): if Tj reads a version written by Ti, a later read cannot return an earlier version of an item Ti wrote.
  2. Begin with the Read Committed algorithm. Replicas withhold new writes from readers until all replicas for the final writes of the transaction have received them (the writes are pending stable). Each write carries the transaction's single timestamp and the list of items written.
  3. When a client reads an item, the returned timestamp and item list give a lower bound on the versions the client must read for the other items in the list. The client attaches the current bound to each later request.
  4. A replica responds with a write matching the bound or a pending-stable write with a higher timestamp. Each replica holds, per item, the highest pending-stable write and the set of writes not yet pending stable. Since the bound refers only to writes already pending stable, which every replica holds, a replica can always respond, so the algorithm is master-less and operations never block on replica coordination.
  5. The paper gives the full algorithm in its technical report; this is its outline.∎
Within a visit, and across visits

The same answer twice

What if a reader asks for the same work twice during a single visit and wants to see the exact same edition both times (`Item Cut Isolation` / `Repeatable Read`)?

That is effortless: the reader (or the reading room) simply jots down the edition shown on the first reading, and if the reader asks for that work again during the same visit, hands back the cached copy (unless the reader himself modified it during the visit). By keeping older editions on a back shelf, the librarian can do the same for a whole list of works matching a topic (`Predicate Cut Isolation`).

What about across visits, when a reader may visit Library 1 today and Library 2 tomorrow, and never wants to see an older edition than one he read before (`Monotonic Reads`)? Even without sticking to one library, the coast can guarantee this by having every library show readers only its floor: the highest edition that the library knows every library on the coast has already received!

Because no library reveals an edition until every library holds it, a reader who walks from any library to any other can never step backwards. The price is staleness: while couriers are stopped, the floor stops rising, so every library keeps showing the old pre-stoppage floor—even to the very scholar who just wrote a new edition!

The second shelf

Staying with one library

Suppose a reader leaves a correction at Library 1, steps out while the couriers are stopped, and returns the next morning to read the work (`Read Your Writes`).

If he walks into Library 2—which is cut off from Library 1 by the stopped couriers—Library 2 has never heard of his correction! Under high availability, Library 2 cannot wait for the couriers to resume; it must answer from its own shelves, which hold only the old edition. Thus no system can guarantee Read Your Writes if a client switches between partitioned replicas.

To get `Read Your Writes`, the reader must stay with one library (`sticky availability`), returning to the same door each visit—or else carry copies of his own corrections under his arm to show any new library he visits (which amounts to acting as his own sticky replica).

What is promised

A reader who stays with one library always sees their own corrections

When a reader sticks to one library, that library already holds every correction the reader ever wrote, in the exact order they wrote them (`PRAM` consistency). The only catch is that if that one library burns down or closes its doors, the reader's unpropagated corrections are trapped inside until it reopens.

Proof · that Read Your Writes is not achievable with high availability, and is with sticky availability

Read your writes: whenever a client reads an item after updating it, the read returns the updated value or one that overwrote it. Sticky availability: a client always reaches the same replica, and gets a response whenever it can. wx(1) is a write of 1 to item x, and rx(a) a read of x returning a. Claim. Read your writes cannot be provided with high availability, and is provided with sticky availability.

  1. This is §5.1.3. Read your writes requires that whenever a client reads an item after updating it, the read returns the updated value or one that overwrote it. Consider a client that executes T1 : wx(1) and then T2 : rx(a).
  2. Suppose the client executes T1 against a server partitioned from all others. For transactional availability the server must allow T1 to commit.
  3. If the network topology then changes so that the client can execute T2 only on a different replica, partitioned from the one that executed T1, that replica has no way to learn of wx(1). By transactional availability it must respond, so it either stalls indefinitely, violating availability, or returns a value other than 1 or one that overwrote it, violating read your writes.
  4. If the client remains sticky with the server that executed T1, that server holds the write and the scenario is excluded. So read your writes, and with it PRAM (monotonic reads, monotonic writes and read your writes) and causal consistency, require stickiness and are provided by default in a sticky system.∎
Other people's writing

Letters and replies

Suppose a scholar at Library 1 writes a letter on a question, and it is catalogued as a work. A second scholar at Library 2 reads that letter and writes a reply. What if the courier carrying the reply reaches Library 3 before the slow courier carrying the original letter?

To promise that no reply is ever shown before the letter it answers (`Writes Follow Reads` / `Causal Consistency`), each new work carries a note listing the editions its author had read before writing it (`causal dependencies`). When the reply arrives at Library 3 ahead of the letter, Library 3 holds the reply in the back room and does not place it on the public shelf until the letter it depends on has also arrived. All the while, Library 3 continues answering readers immediately from the works already on its shelf.

The ceiling of what can be promised without waiting

Causal consistency plus atomic views is as strong as a sticky library can go

Combining the first two shelves—holding back half-made or incomplete cords (`MAV`), holding back replies until their letters arrive (`Writes Follow Reads`), and keeping each reader at one library (`Read Your Writes` + `Monotonic Reads/Writes`)—gives transactional Causal Consistency. Anything stronger crosses onto the third shelf, where no librarian can keep the promise alone.

The third shelf

The last copy

Now we reach the third shelf: promises that no librarian can keep while the couriers are stopped. Suppose the libraries lend works out for a term,* and only one copy of a work remains available on the coast.

Before the couriers stop, both Library 1 and Library 2 have a note on their shelves saying 1 copy available. Now the couriers stop, and a reader walks into Library 1 to borrow the copy at the exact same time a second reader walks into Library 2 to borrow it.

Each librarian checks his shelf, sees `1 copy available`, lends it out, and writes `0 copies available` (`Lost Update`). Because the couriers are stopped, neither librarian can learn that the other just lent the copy! Any rule that prevented this—such as locking the work or waiting for a majority of libraries—would force at least one librarian to stall or turn his reader away while the couriers are stopped.

The same failure with two works

A shelf left bare (`Write Skew`)

Suppose two reference works X and Y are kept under a rule that at least one of the two must stay on the shelf at all times. While couriers are stopped, a reader at Library 1 checks that Y is on the shelf and borrows X; concurrently, a reader at Library 2 checks that X is on the shelf and borrows Y. Each visit obeyed the rule locally, yet together they left the reference shelf completely bare!

Mended outside

Settled on the road (`Compensation`)

How do real systems live without preventing `Lost Update` or `Write Skew`? Often by allowing both transactions to succeed while partitioned and issuing an apology or compensating action afterward—just as the two borrowers can settle who gets the scroll when they meet on the road.

Dodged by changing the rule

Shelf-marks and spare copies

Often a clash can be avoided by redesigning the rule: if shelf-marks only need to be globally unique rather than strictly sequential (`1, 2, 3...`), each library can prefix its own library number with zero coordination. And if a popular work would be over-lent, scribes can copy a fresh scroll on demand (`restocking`).

Proof · that Lost Update and Write Skew cannot be prevented with high availability

rx(v) is a read of item x returning v, and wx(v) a write of v to x. Lost Update: T1 reads an item, T2 updates it, and T1 then writes from its original read. Write Skew: the same across several items bound by a constraint. Transactional availability: a transaction that reaches a replica for each of its items eventually commits, or aborts by its own choice. Claim. Neither anomaly can be prevented with high availability.

  1. This is §5.2.1. Lost Update: T1 reads an item, T2 updates the same item, and T1 writes based on its original read, losing T2's update. Take T1 : rx(100) wx(100 + 20 = 120) and T2 : rx(100) wx(100 + 30 = 130), submitted on opposite sides of a network partition.
  2. By transactional availability both must be allowed to commit. Whichever of 120 and 130 a replica then chooses, the state could not have arisen from a serial execution of T1 and T2, since a serial execution gives 150.
  3. To prevent it, one of the transactions must not commit. Each side's server could try to detect that another write occurred, but this requires knowing the version of the latest write to x, which reduces to a requirement for linearizability, provably at odds with high availability by Gilbert and Lynch's proof of the CAP theorem.
  4. Write Skew generalises Lost Update to several items: T1 : ry(0) wx(1) and T2 : rx(0) wy(1), with a constraint that only one of x and y may be 1. Run on opposite sides of a partition, both commit and the constraint is violated, though each would have preserved it in isolation; any prevention needs the same knowledge of the other side and is likewise unavailable.
  5. Hence Snapshot Isolation, Cursor Stability and consistent read (which require preventing Lost Update), and Repeatable Read and one-copy serializability (which require preventing both) are not achievable with high availability.∎
What readers ask for first

The latest edition

Finally, no librarian who must always answer can promise that the copy handed over is the latest edition (`linearizability`)—nor even a looser time bound such as no more than a day behind.

Why? Because if couriers can be stopped for longer than whatever time bound the librarian promises, a scholar at Library 1 can write a new edition just as the stoppage begins, and once the stoppage outlasts the bound, Library 2 is forced either to stop answering (breaking availability) or to hand over its old copy (breaking the recency bound). This is the exact CAP impossibility of Answering While Cut Off, extended to every bounded-staleness guarantee.

Proof · that no recency guarantee, including linearizability, is achievable with high availability

A recency guarantee bounds how out of date a read may be: linearizability (a read returns the last completed write), safe and regular register semantics, or any bound in time. High availability: every client that reaches a non-failing replica gets a response. Claim. No recency guarantee can be provided with high availability.

  1. This is §5.2.2. A recency guarantee bounds how out of date a read may be: linearizability (reads return the last completed write), safe and regular register semantics, and any bound such as "no more than five seconds out of date".
  2. Let a write complete at a replica on one side of a partition and let a client then read the item at a replica on the other side. The partition may last indefinitely, with no message crossing.
  3. By availability the reading replica must respond, and it can respond only from information it received before the partition began, which does not include the write. So the response is out of date by at least the time the partition has lasted.
  4. Since the partition can last longer than any fixed bound, no recency bound is enforceable. This is the argument of Gilbert and Lynch for the CAP theorem, applied to every recency guarantee.∎

What do the libraries win in exchange for giving up the third shelf? Speed—even on sunny days when every courier is running! In the authors' geo-distributed benchmarks across continents, transactions that coordinated through a single master or quorum took `10` to `100` times longer than Highly Available Transactions (`HATs`) answered locally. Better yet, surveying eighteen commercial databases revealed that fifteen of them already defaulted to weak isolation levels (`Read Committed` or weaker) that can be made highly available.

The limits

What it does not say

The paper sorts transactional guarantees onto three clear shelves. Here are four things it does not claim.

Is “answering alone” the only useful definition of availability?

No—it is deliberately the strictest one (requiring `100%` of non-failing replicas to answer under arbitrary partitions). A system that only needs to answer in the connected majority of the coast (like the Chamber or Tholos), or only with high probability (like PBS), can provide stronger third-shelf guarantees to the clients it serves.

Are the proof-of-concept devices meant for production as-is?

Not always. For instance, keeping a global floor of editions across all libraries can stall visibility whenever one library is slow. The paper's buffered `Monotonic Atomic View` (`MAV`) design, however, is genuinely practical and achieved high throughput and low latency in the authors' experiments.

What happens when you combine several guarantees?

A combination of guarantees is only as available as its least available part: combining any first-shelf `HAT` guarantee with `Read Your Writes` makes the combination `Sticky`, and adding `Prevent Lost Update` makes it `Unavailable`. Across the combinations studied, the paper maps out `144` distinct consistency and isolation levels.

Can every application live without the third shelf?

No. Some operations genuinely require preventing `Lost Update` or `Write Skew`. The lesson of `HATs`—and of the next book, Knowing When to Wait—is to reserve slow cross-coast coordination for the few operations that truly need the third shelf, while running the rest at local reading-room speed.

Setting The Scholars' Coast

The paper behind this book

Peter Bailis, Aaron Davidson, Alan Fekete, Ali Ghodsi, Joseph M. Hellerstein and Ion Stoica, Highly Available Transactions: Virtues and Limitations. Proceedings of the VLDB Endowment, Vol. 7, No. 3 (40th International Conference on Very Large Data Bases, Hangzhou, September 2014), 181–192. — A taxonomy of highly available, sticky available and unavailable isolation and consistency models, with proofs of unavailability and proof-of-concept algorithms.

The ideas, plainly.

Bailis and co-authors ask which of the guarantees that databases and distributed systems offer can be kept by a replica that must always answer, even when it is cut off from every other replica for as long as the cut lasts. They call such systems highly available, and with transactions, highly available transactions. They go through the known guarantees and sort them into those that can be kept, those that can be kept if each client stays with one replica, and those that cannot.

The first group includes read committed, which stops a client reading writes that are not finished, monotonic atomic view, which makes the writes of one transaction visible all together or not at all, repeatable reads within a transaction, and monotonic reads and writes and writes-follow-reads. The second adds read-your-writes, PRAM and causal consistency. Each is built by holding writes back from readers until it is safe to show them, never by making a reader wait.

The third group is made of the guarantees that need a replica to know about concurrent operations elsewhere: preventing lost update and write skew, which rules out snapshot isolation, repeatable read in its other senses and serializability, and any bound on how out of date a read may be. These are fundamental, not weaknesses of particular systems. The paper concludes that many applications can live with the first two groups, that some operations will need the third and should use it sparingly, and that high availability buys the lowest delay.

The paper has six authors, Peter Bailis, Aaron Davidson, Alan Fekete, Ali Ghodsi, Joseph M. Hellerstein and Ion Stoica, and all are credited here. Nobody in this telling is named; the librarians and readers are told apart by what they do.
