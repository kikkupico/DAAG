The Census of the Ring Road

Being an account of how the goods of Arche were counted without stopping a single cart

In which the scribes produce a total that is certainly correct, that answers the question the archon asked, and that describes a morning nobody in Arche ever lived through.

Chapter I

The Archon's Question

Kleitos, archon of Arche in a winter when trade across the island multiplied, asked the trading houses a question that sounded simple and was not. He wanted to know how much grain and oil the distribution network held. Not how much had been landed at the Anchor's quay from foreign ships, which the toll-posts recorded, nor how much had been sold into the three port towns, which the house ledgers recorded, but how much was there, at one moment, everywhere at once.

The difficulty is the geography of the island. Arche rises around Mount Phyle, whose sheer limestone cone cuts off all line of sight between the shores. Goods reside in two forms: inside the storehouses of the three coastal houses — the Dolphin on the rocky western shelf, the Vine on the northern terraced slopes, and the Anchor on the eastern bay — and in transit between them, loaded in carts and pack-trains moving along the narrow coastal ring road cut into the cliffs.

Between these three houses, goods move continually from dawn until dusk. A cart loaded with oil jars at the Anchor at first light may reach the Dolphin by mid-afternoon or, if a mule throws a shoe on the cliff path, by nightfall.

The scribes' first proposal was to send an auditor to each house to count its storehouse, and then add the three tallies together. Chandyos, who kept the island's customs rolls, sent the men out, walked down to the cliff road to watch the carts pass for an hour, and returned to declare the proposal worthless. He showed why with two mornings' worth of arithmetic.

Suppose the Anchor's auditor counts at dawn, and the Vine's auditor counts at noon. In between, a cart carries two hundred jars of oil from the Anchor to the Vine. The Anchor counted them; the Vine counted them again upon arrival. The archon's total is two hundred jars too high: the island has manufactured oil merely by inquiring after it.

Reverse it. The Vine counts at dawn and the Anchor at noon, and in between a cart carries two hundred jars from the Vine to the Anchor. Now nobody counted them at all: they had left the Vine's storehouse before the Vine's auditor looked, and had not yet reached the Anchor when the Anchor's auditor finished his slate. Two hundred jars have vanished from the world, and there is no thief.

The error, stated once

Counting the houses one after another produces a total that is wrong by exactly the goods that moved along the ring road between one counting and the next. The error can run in either direction, cannot be bounded in advance, and nothing in the three local ledgers reveals that it has occurred.

Chapter II

Why the Road Is Not Stopped

There is an obvious remedy, and the archon proposed it at once: stop the road. Let no cart move from the second hour, count every shed while everything sits motionless, and let the trains move again. Then there is nothing in transit, and the three storehouse counts add up.

Chandyos objected on two grounds, and the second is the one that matters.

The first is commercial. Arche's distribution network feeds the three port towns, and a road that stops moving food ceases to serve the island. A census that halts all trade every time the archon takes stock will be tolerated once and never repeated.

The second is subtler, because stopping the road can be done, and done soundly. The order to stop travels by runner along the same cliff road, and the runner takes as long as the road takes. The Vine may receive it an hour before the Anchor, and in that hour the Anchor, which has heard nothing, will quite properly load a cart and send it down the path. That does no harm. Each house stops dispatching when the order reaches it, keeps taking in whatever arrives, and counts when its incoming roads are empty. Houses that stop at different hours still give a true total, so long as each one waits until nothing more is coming.

But how is a house to know that nothing more is coming? It cannot see the road, and a cart may be hours out, round the next headland, behind a lame mule. The only way to know is for each house, the moment it stops, to send a last runner down each of its roads who means nothing behind me. The roads are single-file, so when that runner comes in, the road behind him is empty. So stopping the island needs a marker on every road anyway. And once the houses have such markers, Chandyos saw, they have no need to stop at all.

That last runner, sent without stopping anything, is the red sash of Chapter IV.

Stated formally The system is a set of processes (the houses) connected by directed channels (the one-way tracks of the ring road). A channel delivers messages and goods in the order sent (FIFO), without loss or corruption, after an arbitrary but finite delay. There is no shared clock, and no process can directly observe another's state. A global state consists of the local state of every process together with the state of every channel.

Lamportios, who had come across from the Dolphin with his tallies, made the observation that unlocked the problem. The scribes had been trying to capture the island at a single physical instant. They should stop trying. What the archon actually needs is a total that is sound — one into which no cart has been counted twice, and from which no cart has fallen into oblivion. Whether such a total was ever simultaneously true of the island in physical time is an entirely separate question, and one the archon has no practical need to ask.

Chapter III

What the Road Is Holding

The step that makes the census possible is simple and had been overlooked: the cargo on the road is part of the island's stock.

This sounds obvious and is not. Each trading house considers its inventory to be what sits under its own timber roof. Grain in a bin or oil in a cellar is inventory; oil in jars lashed to a mule on the cliff road belongs to no house's shed and appears on no house's slate. Yet it exists, it is owned, and it is precisely the quantity that naive counting duplicates or loses.

So the island is not to be counted as three quantities, but as nine: the storehouses of the Dolphin, the Vine, and the Anchor — and, separately, what each road segment is holding. There are six directed tracks along the ring road. There are three stretches of road, one between each pair of houses, and each stretch has two cuttings, the upper walked only sunwise and the lower only against the sun. The two are counted apart. Nothing goes the long way round past a third house's gate, so goods from the Dolphin to the Anchor are only ever on the one track from the Dolphin to the Anchor.

The nine quantities

A complete account of Arche's goods consists of: what sits in each of the three house storehouses, plus what is in transit along each of the six road segments between them. A method that records only the storehouses does not give an incomplete account of Arche; it gives a complete account of a different island, one in which carts take zero time to travel.

Now, no scribe can inspect a road segment while standing at a desk. A cart rounding the promontory of Mount Phyle is hidden behind rock, and the customs house cannot station men every ten paces along the surf. What a house can do is record what passes through its own gate. And that, as Chandyos realized, is sufficient — provided the house knows exactly when to start recording incoming arrivals and when to stop.

Chapter IV

The Red Sash

The mechanism Chandyos and Lamportios settled on requires a single new token: a runner wearing a plain red sash. The runner carries no goods, no purchase orders, and no trade tallies. He is an unmistakable division marker in the traffic of the road, and his entire purpose is to separate what was dispatched before him from what was dispatched after him.

Two rules govern the red sash, one for dispatching and one for receiving, and they constitute the entire algorithm.

The rule of sending
  1. A house records the inventory of its own storehouse.
  2. Immediately, and before dispatching any further carts or orders along any outgoing road segment, it dispatches one runner bearing the red sash down that road.
The rule of receiving
  1. A red sash runner arrives along a road segment from another house. If the receiving house has not yet recorded its own storehouse — it records its storehouse immediately, writes down that this incoming road segment holds nothing, and then obeys the rule of sending, dispatching a red sash runner down all its outgoing road segments.
  2. If the receiving house has already recorded its storehouse — it writes down, as that road segment's holding, all goods that arrived along that road between the moment it recorded its own storehouse and the arrival of this red sash runner.

That is all. Any house may initiate the census; the customs house at the Anchor usually does. No cart is delayed, no mule is halted, and no house needs to know the internal state of any other.

The second clause of the receiving rule does the essential work. A house that has already recorded its own shed continues to receive incoming carts as usual — but from that moment it opens a temporary slate for that specific road segment, listing every jar and sack that arrives along it. When the runner with the red sash finally strides through the gate, that slate is closed. The sum on that slate is the road segment's holding. The house never had to see the road; it only needed to know when to open the slate and when to close it, and the red sash marked both boundaries.

The method leans entirely upon two physical properties of the ring road, established in Book the First:
(1) Strict FIFO ordering. Each cutting is too narrow for overtaking and carries traffic one way only, and nothing reaches a house from another except along the one direct track. A red sash dispatched after a cart cannot pass that cart, and a cart dispatched after the sash cannot pass the sash.
(2) Reliable delivery. No cart or runner falls off the cliff into the sea. If either property fails, the census fails.

Chapter V

Cuts That Hold and Cuts That Do Not

Draw the island's morning as a spacetime diagram, and the algorithm assumes an exact geometric form. Each trading house runs down its own lifeline; each cart is a downward arrow from departure to arrival. The census draws a boundary line across all three lifelines, passing through each house at the precise moment it recorded its storehouse. Chandyos called this boundary a cut. Because the houses record at different moments, the line is not horizontal; it slopes across the page.

Everything above the cut belongs to the past recorded by the census. Everything below belongs to the island's ongoing future. An arrow that crosses the cut going downward represents a cart dispatched before the sender recorded, but received after the receiver recorded — exactly the goods in transit caught by the second receiving rule.

And an arrow that crosses the cut going upward is the fatal error.

The red sash prevents the upward crossing by mechanical necessity rather than vigilance. A house dispatches a red sash runner down a track before sending any further carts down it. Because no cart can overtake the runner on the cliff road, no cart dispatched after a house records can arrive at a neighbor before the red sash arrives. Therefore, no such arrival can be counted as having arrived before the neighbor's recording. The rule of sending is the complete guarantee of consistency.

Proof — that no cart crosses the cut upward
  1. Call an event at a house early if it occurred before that house recorded its own storehouse, and late otherwise.
  2. Suppose some cart were dispatched late at the Vine and arrived early at the Dolphin. This is the upward crossing.
  3. The Vine recorded its storehouse before dispatching that cart, since the dispatch was late. By the rule of sending, the Vine dispatched a red sash runner down the track to the Dolphin at the moment of recording — prior to dispatching any further carts down that track.
  4. The ring road is strict FIFO: carts and runners cannot pass one another. Therefore, the red sash runner reached the Dolphin before that cart did.
  5. By the rule of receiving, the Dolphin recorded its storehouse no later than the arrival of that red sash runner — either it had already recorded, or the sash forced it to record then.
  6. Thus, the Dolphin had recorded before the cart arrived, making the cart's arrival late, not early — contradicting step 2.∎

One further guarantee is required: the census must terminate. Because the ring road forms a connected circuit, a red sash dispatched by any single house travels around the island. Each house that receives a sash records its storehouse and propagates sashes down all its outgoing tracks. Since the network is finite and every runner reaches his destination in finite time, every house records its storehouse and every road slate is closed. The census inevitably concludes.

Chapter VI

A Morning Nobody Lived Through

The nine numbers came in, and Chandyos summed them into a single figure for the archon. Then he did something that unsettled the customs clerks for days: he checked the total against the island's records, and could not find a single hour at which it had been true.

The Dolphin had recorded at the third hour. The Vine had recorded at the fourth. Between those hours, the Vine had milled eighty measures of barley into flour for local distribution — an internal transformation involving no road, no cart, and no sash. The recorded total therefore describes an island in which the Dolphin stands as it stood at the third hour, and the Vine stands as it stood at the fourth. No sundial or water-clock ever marked an instant at which both descriptions were simultaneously true.

And yet the total is not false. This is the central theorem of distributed state.

Theorem 1 (Chandy & Lamport 1985)

Let Sbegin be the global state of the island when the census was initiated, S* the global state recorded by the scribes, and Send the global state when the last road slate was closed. Then:

(1) S* is reachable from Sbegin by a valid execution sequence consisting of the exact events that actually occurred on the island, taken in an order that preserves causality.

(2) Send is reachable from S* by the remainder of those same events.

Hence S* lies on a valid path between Sbegin and Send. It is not an idle fiction; it is a state Arche could have passed through had independent events on opposite sides of Mount Phyle unfolded in a different, equally lawful order.

This re-ordering is justified by the principles of Book the First. Two events occurring at different houses with no causal chain of runners between them stand in no natural order. The census is entitled to consider either as occurring first. What it may never do is reverse events that are causally linked — and the red sash guarantees that no such reversal occurs.

Proof — that the recorded state lies on a valid execution path
  1. Write down the complete sequence of actual events that occurred on the island during the census: e1, e2, …, em. Each event is classified as early or late, depending on whether the house at which it occurred had already recorded its storehouse.
  2. In the actual execution, some late events may precede early events (for example, if the Dolphin records early while the Vine continues normal operations before recording).
  3. Claim: If a late event ei is immediately followed by an early event ei+1, they may be transposed in the sequence without altering any resulting state.
  4. They cannot occur at the same house: a house transitions from unrecorded to recorded exactly once, so at any single house, every early event precedes every late event.
  5. Neither can depend on the other. ei cannot have caused ei+1: that would require a cart dispatched after recording at one house to arrive before recording at another, which was proved impossible in Chapter V. And ei+1 cannot have caused ei, because it follows it in physical time.
  6. Because ei and ei+1 are concurrent events at different houses, they commute: executing them in reverse order produces the identical state at all houses and on all roads.
  7. Repeatedly transpose adjacent (late, early) pairs. Each swap shifts an early event to the left. This sorting terminates in a valid sequence where all early events precede all late events.
  8. Execute the early events from Sbegin. Because every early event has occurred and no late event has occurred, each house reaches its recorded local state, and each road segment contains exactly the items recorded on its slate. This state is precisely S*.
  9. Executing the remaining late events from S* leads directly to Send.∎

Chandyos summarized the finding to the archon in words that entered the customs house tradition: “You asked for a morning, and I have given you a morning; it is only not the particular morning you lived through.”

Chapter VII

What Such a Total Is Good For

A recorded state describing an arrangement that never simultaneously existed in physical time is useless for certain inquiries, and the scribes were careful to delimit its scope.

It cannot resolve disputes tied to the shadow of a sundial. If a merchant insists that the Vine held three hundred jars of vintage oil when the third hour struck, the census cannot verify or refute his claim, because the census does not operate by hours.

What the census resolves with absolute certainty are questions of a specific mathematical form: those whose answer, once it becomes true, remains true forever. Chandyos termed such conditions settled properties (or stable properties).

Settled properties

A property y is settled if for every state S where y(S) holds, y(S') holds for all states S' reachable from S.

Examples on Arche include:
— Distribution termination: every cart has arrived and all houses are idle.
— Deadlock: every house is blocked waiting for carts from another.
— Exhaustion of supply: a specific vintage or grain reserve has been depleted to zero.
— Deficit: an accounting imbalance exists that no internal trade can restore.

The utility of the census for settled properties is decisive:

The decision rule

Evaluate the settled property y on the recorded state S*.

If y(S*) is true: then y is true of the island now, at Send. Because Send is reachable from S*, and a settled property, once true, remains true.

If y(S*) is false: then y was false when the census began, at Sbegin. Because S* is reachable from Sbegin; had y been true at the start, it would still hold at S*.

In either case, the census delivers an indisputable truth about a real state of the island. That the snapshot itself was an interpolated fiction is immaterial, because settled properties cannot distinguish between the path taken and the path that was possible.

For the archon's practical question — how much inventory exists to sustain the island — the census gave a sound inventory where no jar was lost and none was counted twice.

One case is better than sound. On a day when no ship lands at the Anchor and nothing is sold into the port towns, goods only move between the houses, and every move takes jars from one of the nine quantities and adds the same jars to another. The total is then the same at every moment of the day. The recorded state, which never existed, has that same total, so on such a day the census gives the exact figure for any hour the archon cares to name. On a day of landings and sales, the recorded total is true of the recorded state and of no hour anyone lived through.

For settling what was true at the ringing of the midday gong, nothing on an island without global clocks will ever serve.

The ideas, plainly

A distributed system has no single vantage point from which its total state can be observed simultaneously. Halting all processes and waiting for the channels to drain does work, but it is costly, and detecting the drain already needs a marker on every channel. The Chandy–Lamport snapshot algorithm records a consistent global state — both process states and channel states — while normal computation proceeds uninterrupted.

By dispatching markers along all outgoing channels immediately upon recording local state, and before sending further application messages, processes partition each channel's stream. Because channels are FIFO, markers cannot overtake messages, and messages cannot overtake markers. This guarantees that no message sent after a sender records can be received before a receiver records — the fundamental condition of cut consistency.

The resulting snapshot S* may never have occurred in physical time. However, it is causally valid: reachable from the initial state Sbegin, with the final state Send reachable from it. This makes it sufficient to evaluate stable properties like deadlock, garbage collection, and termination: if the property holds in S*, it holds in the running system.
