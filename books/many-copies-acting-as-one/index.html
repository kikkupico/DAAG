Many Copies, Acting as One

When data is stored across multiple copies—or handled by many workers at once—what does it take for the whole system to behave as if there were only a single copy? And who could ever tell the difference?

To find out, we return to the ancient Greek island of Arche, where three trading houses must fill one another's grain requests in the exact order they were truly placed—even when heavy clouds hide the sun.

First, in machine terms

Copies that must act as one

When a shared data structure—like a queue or a database record—is replicated across several machines or accessed by many threads at once, programmers want a simple guarantee: it should behave as if there were only a single copy handling one request at a time.

The trouble is that real operations are not instantaneous. Every call takes time between the moment a client sends it and the moment the answer comes back, and calls from different clients often overlap. Merely putting all calls into some one-at-a-time order isn't enough: if one client's operation completely finished before a second client's operation even started, everyone expects the first one to take effect first. In 1990, Maurice Herlihy and Jeannette Wing defined linearizability to capture this exact guarantee: every operation must appear to take effect instantaneously at some single moment between its start and its completion. Three properties make linearizability especially powerful:

  • Locality: You can verify each shared object on its own. If every individual object is linearizable, the entire system is automatically linearizable.
  • Nonblocking: A linearizable object never forces a client to wait for another client's operation just to preserve the ordering rules.
  • A general proof method: Even when a highly concurrent data structure has no single line of code where an operation "takes effect," we can still prove it is linearizable by tracking the set of possible orders it could represent.

We explore all three through the story of three trading houses on the island of Arche and the request board outside House 3's storehouse.

The goatherd's morning

Which request was placed first?

House 3 has grain in its storehouse, and both other houses need it. All three houses have agreed on a fair rule: whichever request was placed first must be filled first.

With no shared clock to consult, House 3 has been ordering requests using tallies (logical clocks): a rising number stamped on every slip and advanced whenever a slip arrives, with house numbers used to break ties. Tallies guarantee that an effect never comes before its cause along the road, and that no house can jump the queue simply by hiring a faster runner.*

However, tallies only track official slips travelling along the coastal road—and news can also cross the mountain by word of mouth. Scroll through the morning below to see how an unrecorded shortcut can turn the tally order upside down.

As far as the tallies can tell, no official slip connected the two requests, so either one could go first. But House 2 knows—and so does anyone who spoke with the goatherd—that House 1's request was completely finished and confirmed before House 2 even picked up a stylus. In real time, under the sun, House 1 was indisputably first.

We saw earlier that tallies have this blind spot because they only track official slips.* Instead of trying to build synchronized clocks, the houses want a stronger guarantee about how their request board behaves: a promise that any request that finishes before another begins is always served first, so that no goatherd crossing the mountain can ever catch the board out of order.

Since nobody on cloudy Arche can read a sundial, how can the houses even state—much less keep—a promise based on real time?

A promise in what the houses see

The rule of the board

Any promise about the board must be stated in terms of what the houses actually observe at their gates. For a request, a house observes two events: the moment it sends the request slip out, and the moment the confirmation slip arrives back. Somewhere between those two moments, the request took its place on House 3's waiting board.

Serving a request has a start and a finish too: a loader at House 3 walks up to the board, takes the oldest waiting request, and loads the grain sacks onto a cart. Each house—and each loader—performs one piece of business at a time.

Every piece of business therefore spans an interval of real time, from its start to its completion. One piece of business comes before another if it completely finished before the other began; otherwise, the two overlap in time, and neither is strictly first. Now we can state the promise—which computer scientists call linearizability, and which the houses call the rule of the board. Imagine a single, unhurried steward standing beside a single board, doing one thing at a time: either entering a new request at the back of the board, or handing the oldest waiting request to a loader. The houses don't need to see this steward; they only ask that everything they experience at their gates could have come from such a steward, with each action taking effect at a single instant inside its real-time span. More precisely, all requests and servings must fit into a single sequence that satisfies three demands:

First demand

The same as one steward

Every serving hands out the exact request that a single steward, managing a single board in that one-at-a-time sequence, would have handed out.

Second demand

A moment inside the span

Each request or serving takes effect at a single instant (its linearization point) somewhere between its start and its completion.

Third demand

Finished first, comes first

If one piece of business completely finished before another began, it must appear earlier in the sequence.

Notice how the second demand ties the promise to real time under the sun—and automatically guarantees the third! If House 1's request finished before House 2's request even began, then the single instant inside House 1's span must come earlier than the single instant inside House 2's span. A single steward therefore enters House 1's request first and serves it first—which is why the goatherd's morning violates the rule of the board.

A correctness rule can fail by being too strict (forbidding runs that make complete sense) or too loose (allowing runs that are clearly broken). Let's test the rule of the board against four different mornings.

The rule of the board, stated formally

Write <H for the real-time order of a history H: a <H b when operation a receives its response before operation b is invoked. The board is a FIFO queue: placing a request is Enq, and serving a request is Deq.

A history of requests and servings satisfies the rule of the board (linearizability) if all completed operations—plus whichever still-pending operations have already taken effect—can be placed into a single sequential order such that:

  1. Legal sequential behavior: Every serving (Deq) returns the exact request that a single FIFO queue, executing operations one at a time in that sequence, would return.
  2. One instant per span: Each operation takes its place in the sequence at a single moment between its invocation and its response.
  3. Preserves real-time order: Consequently, any operation that completed before another was invoked appears earlier in the sequence (a <H b implies a precedes b).

Herlihy & Wing 1990, §2.2: a history H is linearizable if, after appending responses to some pending invocations and discarding the rest, it is equivalent to a legal sequential history S that preserves the real-time order <H.

These four mornings show why the rule draws its boundary right where it does. When two requests overlap in time, either one may legitimately be served first; when one finishes before the other begins, their order is fixed. A request can even be served while its confirmation slip is still travelling back along the road, because the request took effect the moment it was placed on the board. And no timing overlap can ever excuse serving the same request twice.

What if we stop and judge a morning while some request is still in flight—sent, but not yet confirmed? The rule makes a natural allowance: an unfinished request may either be counted as already on the board or left out, whichever fits what the loaders have already served.

We now have two ways of defining "which request came first": the tallies from our previous book, and the rule of the board. How do they compare?

The rule and the tallies

The tallies' first, and the sun's first

Surprisingly, the tallies (causal order) and the rule of the board (real-time order) give genuinely different answers about which request came first—and neither rule is a stricter version of the other.

For the tallies, one request must precede another if a chain of official slips leads from the first to the second; otherwise, ties are broken by tally and house number.* For the rule of the board, one request must precede another if the first received its confirmation before the second was sent, regardless of whether any slips passed between the two houses. On the goatherd's morning, no official slip connects the two requests, so the tallies allow House 2 to be served first—whereas the rule of the board forces House 1 to go first because its confirmation arrived before House 2 asked. Now flip the situation: suppose House 1 sends a grain request to House 3, and immediately sends a second slip telling House 2 about it. Upon reading that slip, House 2 sends its own grain request while House 1 is still waiting for its confirmation. Because a slip connects them, the tallies force House 1 to be served first—whereas their real-time spans overlap, so the rule of the board allows either house to be served first!

Even without a sundial, House 3 can satisfy the rule of the board in a remarkably simple way: keep a single board at House 3's gate, enter each request the moment its messenger arrives, and send the confirmation slip back right after. Because the entry happens after the request left and before the confirmation returns, that entry is guaranteed to fall inside the request's real-time span.

Two Kinds of First

Entering requests in the order their messengers reach House 3's gate satisfies the rule of the board (linearizability). Serving them by tally order need not. Conversely, tally order can fix the sequence of two overlapping requests where the rule of the board allows either order.

Why did the houses ever bother with tallies in the first place? Because when two requests overlap in time, serving by arrival at the gate always favors whichever house has the faster runner or the shorter stretch of road. The rule of the board doesn't mind that—when two requests overlap, it allows either order. Tallies, by contrast, protect causal chains and prevent faster runners from winning, at the cost of ignoring real-time order when no slip connects two events. The two rules simply protect different guarantees.

We just saw that a single board at House 3's gate satisfies the rule of the board. What if all three houses want to keep copies of the board locally? Does the rule allow multiple copies?

Proof · that serving by arrival at House 3's gate satisfies linearizability

Write <H for the real-time order of a history H: a <H b when a responds before b is invoked. H is linearizable if it can be extended to a history equivalent to a legal sequential history S with <H ⊆ <S. For the board (a FIFO queue), legal means each Deq returns the oldest item Enq-ed and not yet returned. Suppose a single copy sits at House 3's gate, and each operation takes effect at the exact instant it is applied to that board. Claim. Ordering operations by the instant they are applied at House 3's gate forms a valid linearization S.

  1. Order all requests and servings by the exact instant each is performed on House 3's board: a request when the clerk enters it, and a serving when a loader takes a request from the board. Because the board handles one action at a time, this defines a strict sequence S.
  2. Each serving takes the earliest request entered on the board and not yet served, matching the behavior of a single sequential FIFO queue (Demand 1).
  3. A request can only reach House 3's gate after its slip was sent, and its confirmation only arrives back at the sender after leaving House 3's gate. Thus, the instant of entry always lies strictly inside the request's real-time span, and a serving's instant lies inside the loader's span (Demand 2). Demand 3 follows immediately: if a finished before b began, a's instant at the board occurred before b's.∎
One board, or three

Copies nobody can count

Whether the island uses one central board or three separate copies is settled by what the rule of the board talks about: it speaks only of requests sent, confirmations received, and grain served at the houses' gates.

The rule never mentions how the board is built inside the counting rooms. As long as the confirmations and servings seen at the gates match what a single steward with a single board could have produced, the rule is satisfied—whether there is one wooden board at House 3, or three copies kept in sync by messengers behind the scenes.

Acting as one

Indistinguishable from one copy

Even if the houses compared all their logs alongside an observer who watched the sun, no one could ever prove there was more than a single board. That is what it means for many copies to act as one.

Other books in this series explore the protocols that keep replicated copies in sync. Here, we focus on what the guarantee of linearizability itself gives to anyone who relies on it.

First, what happens when the island has more than one kind of trade? Grain is not the only commodity on Arche—and if the houses run separate boards for grain and olive oil, do they have to coordinate the two boards with each other?

Modularity · Locality

Grain and oil

While House 3 stores grain, House 2 presses olive oil and manages oil requests on a separate board of its own. If adding a second board meant the two boards had to coordinate with each other, building large systems out of smaller parts would be a nightmare.

With the rule of the board, they don't have to coordinate at all. Checking each board on its own is enough to guarantee that the whole island's business is linearizable.

One Board at a Time (Locality)

The combined history of all business across the island satisfies the rule of the board if and only if each individual board's history satisfies the rule on its own.

Why do separate boards automatically fit together without any cross-board coordination? Because every board is anchored to the same real-world timeline—the sun—and real time never loops back on itself. Once you pick a valid instant inside the span of each grain operation and a valid instant inside the span of each oil operation, those instants naturally sit in a single timeline across the whole island.

To see why anchoring to real time is essential, consider a slightly weaker rule called sequential consistency (which we'll call the looser rule). It requires that operations behave as if executed by a single steward in an order that respects each individual house's own sequence of actions, without requiring an operation finished earlier in real time to go before one started later. On its own, the looser rule sounds reasonable—it even allows the goatherd's morning. Yet as the interactive below shows, two boards can each pass the looser rule individually while contradicting each other when combined!

Under the looser rule, the grain board looks fine on its own, and the oil board looks fine on its own—yet together they form an impossible cycle where neither house's first request could have happened first! Systems that rely only on sequential consistency cannot be verified one component at a time. Under the rule of the board (linearizability), real time breaks the cycle and pinpoints the exact culprit on the grain board.

So each board can be built and verified independently. Next, could obeying the rule of the board ever force a house to stand waiting at the gate while someone else's request finishes?

Proof · that if every board is linearizable, the whole system is linearizable (Locality)

For a history H and an object x, write H|x for the subhistory of operations on x, and <H for the real-time order: a <H b when a responds before b is invoked. Claim (Locality, Theorem 1, the "if" direction): if every H|x is linearizable, then H is linearizable.

  1. For each object x, choose a valid linearization order <x of H|x. Define a combined relation < as the transitive closure of the union of all <x together with the real-time order <H.
  2. If < is acyclic (contains no loops), we can extend it to a single total order that preserves every <x (satisfying Demand 1 on each object) and preserves <H (satisfying Demands 2 and 3). Thus, it suffices to prove that < contains no cycle.
  3. Suppose for contradiction that a cycle exists, and choose a cycle e₁ < e₂ < … < en < e₁ of minimal length.
  4. The cycle cannot lie entirely on one object x. Because <x is a strict total order on x, any cycle on x would have to use at least one real-time edge b <H a where a <x b—contradicting the fact that <x respects <H. Thus, the cycle must touch at least two objects.
  5. Relabel the cycle so that e₁ is on object x and e₂ is on another object. Since the step from e₁ to e₂ crosses objects, it must be a real-time edge: e₁ <H e₂ (meaning e₁ finished before e₂ began).
  6. No other operation in the minimal cycle can be on object x. Suppose some ei (for i > 2) is the first operation after e₁ on object x. The edge entering ei crosses objects, so ei−1 <H ei. Furthermore, e₂ must have begun before ei−1 finished (otherwise ei−1 <H e₂, giving a shorter cycle e₂ < … < ei−1 < e₂). Chaining these timestamps gives: e₁ finished before e₂ began, which was before ei−1 finished, which was before ei began. Hence e₁ <H ei, yielding a shorter cycle e₁ < ei < … < en < e₁—a contradiction.
  7. Therefore, the final edge en < e₁ also crosses objects and must be a real-time edge en <H e₁. By the same timestamp chain with i = 1, we get en <H e₂, which produces a shorter cycle e₂ < … < en < e₂. Every case contradicts minimality, proving no cycle exists.∎
Could the rule make a house stand at the gate?

Nobody made to wait

The rule of the board never forces a house placing a request to wait for another house's operation to finish. Whatever is currently on the board, a single steward can always append a new request to the end right now—so completing a pending request immediately can never violate the rule.

No Request Waits on Another (Nonblocking)

Whenever the history so far satisfies the rule of the board, any pending operation that has a valid answer in every state (like placing a request) can complete immediately without breaking the rule.

That means if a house ever has to wait for its confirmation, the delay comes from the physical road or the way the counting room was organized—never from the rule of the board itself. What about a loader trying to serve grain when the board is completely empty? In that case, a single steward would naturally have nothing to hand over until a request arrives. Waiting at an empty board is part of what "serve the next request" means, not an artificial delay imposed by the ordering rule.

By contrast, database transactions (serializability)—where a house asks to perform a bundle of several actions all together as one unit—can force callers to wait or even abort. Compare the three scenarios below to see the difference.

Up to now, we have imagined a single steward working alone at the board. In reality, House 3's board is bustling with multiple clerks and loaders working simultaneously—and proving that such a busy board still obeys the rule brings one last surprise.

Proof · that a pending total operation can always complete immediately (Nonblocking)

A total operation (such as Enq on an unbounded queue) is defined in every object state. Claim (Nonblocking property, Theorem 2): if H is linearizable and p is a pending invocation of a total operation in H, then there exists a response r such that H extended by r is also linearizable.

  1. Let S be a legal sequential history that witnesses the linearizability of H.
  2. If S already includes the pending invocation p along with some response r, then returning r right now is valid and witnessed by the exact same S.
  3. If S does not include p, because the operation is total, there is some response r that is legal in the state reached at the very end of S. Append (p, r) to the end of S.
  4. Because (p, r) is placed at the end, earlier operations in S are unaffected (Demand 1 holds), its linearization point is right now, which lies inside p's span (Demand 2 holds), and since no operation in H was invoked after right now, placing (p, r) last cannot violate any real-time precedence (Demand 3 holds).∎
Concurrent implementation · The lock-free queue

The busy board

At House 3's actual portico, several clerks and loaders work at the board at the same time. To keep everyone moving briskly, the master of the board designed a lock-free procedure where no worker ever has to stand aside and wait for another.

The wooden board consists of a long row of numbered slots—each large enough for one clay request tablet—and a movable peg marking the next unused slot number. To enter a request, a clerk performs two steps: first, in one swift motion, they read the slot number at the peg and advance the peg by one; second, they walk to that slot and place their tablet inside. To serve a request, a loader reads the peg, scans the slots starting from slot 1, and takes the first tablet they find. Even if a slow clerk pauses between taking a slot number and placing a tablet, nobody else is held up.

With a single steward, it was easy to point to the exact step where each request joined the board. On this busy board, entering a request takes two separate steps (moving the peg, then placing the tablet)—and as the scroll below reveals, neither step by itself can always be called the exact instant the request took effect!

Now suppose instead that the first clerk slips House 1's tablet into slot 1 just before the loader looks. The loader scans from slot 1, finds House 1's tablet waiting, and serves House 1 first!

Neither ending violates the rule of the board: because the two clerks' spans overlapped, either House 1 or House 2 may legitimately go first. What makes this board fascinating is that at Step 4—when the second clerk has already finished—which order the board is in has not yet been decided! A future race between the first clerk and the loader will settle it, which means no single fixed step in the clerk's routine can be pointed to as the one moment a request takes effect.

If we cannot point to a single step in the clerk's routine as the moment of entry, how can we prove that the busy board always obeys the rule of the board?

The busy board, stated formally

Herlihy and Wing's concurrent lock-free queue (§4.2). The board is represented by an unbounded array items initialized to null and a counter back initialized to 1; array slots are never reused.

  1. To enter a request (Enq(x)): Atomically read the current slot index at the peg and increment it by 1 (i := INC(q.back)), then store the request tablet in that slot (STORE(q.items[i], x)).
  2. To serve a request (Deq()): Read the peg's current position (range := READ(q.back) - 1). Scan slots 1 through range in ascending order, atomically swapping each slot with null (SWAP(q.items[i], null)), and return the first non-null tablet found. If all scanned slots are empty, repeat from the beginning.
Proving correctness · Set-valued abstraction

What the board could mean

Herlihy and Wing solved this puzzle with an elegant shift in perspective: instead of insisting that the board represent one specific queue order at every instant, ask for the set of all possible sequences the board could legitimately stand for right now.

Which sequences can the slots on the wall stand for? Only one strict rule constrains them: if tablet A was already sitting in its slot before clerk B even touched the peg, then A must appear before B. Any two tablets whose clerks overlapped while using the peg and filling their slots may appear in either order! To prove the busy board is linearizable, we only need to show that at every step, every sequence the board could stand for is a sequence that a single steward could legally have reached so far. Two simple facts guarantee this. First, when a clerk places a new tablet into a slot, that tablet can always be placed at the very back of every sequence—because no other tablet could have taken a peg number after an action that is only happening right now. Second, when a loader scans upward from slot 1 and takes the first tablet they see, that tablet is guaranteed to stand at the front of at least one valid sequence—because any tablet required to precede it would sit in an earlier slot that the loader already checked!

The Board Keeps the Rule

At every moment, every sequence of waiting requests the board's slots could stand for is also a valid linearizable sequence of the business so far. Therefore, the busy lock-free board satisfies the rule of the board.

Because the busy board satisfies the rule of the board, any house using it gets the exact same safety guarantees as if a single steward managed everything one step at a time: requests are served in real-time order, no confirmed request ever vanishes, and no phantom request is ever invented.

We have seen what the rule of the board promises and how even a lock-free board keeps it. To finish, let's state plainly the four things the rule of the board does not promise.

Proof · that the busy board is linearizable, in outline

Let A: REP → 2ABS map each state r of the array and peg to the set of abstract FIFO queues it could represent: namely, all permutations of the tablets currently in slots that respect the partial order x <r y (where the STORE of x preceded the INC of y). Let Lin(H|ABS) be the set of abstract queue states reachable by valid linearizations of the history so far. Claim (§4.3.2, Theorem 17): for every reachable state r, A(r) ⊆ Lin(H|ABS).

  1. Base case: Initially the board is empty, representing only the empty queue, which belongs to Lin(H|ABS). We show by induction that every step preserves A(r) ⊆ Lin(H|ABS).
  2. Invocations, responses, INC, and READ: None of these steps adds or removes a tablet from a slot, so A(r) is unchanged (or constrained only in ways consistent with the ongoing operation spans).
  3. Placing a tablet (STORE of x): For another tablet y to be required to follow x (x <r y), y's INC would have to occur after x's STORE—which is happening right now. Thus x is maximal in <r and can be appended to the back of any sequence, matching a legal Enq(x) taking effect right now.
  4. Taking a tablet (SWAP of x at slot i): Suppose the loader scans upward from slot 1 and takes x from slot i. Could some other remaining tablet z satisfy z <r x? If so, z's STORE preceded x's INC, which implies both that z sits in a lower slot (j < i) and that z was already in slot j before the loader began scanning. But then the loader would have encountered and taken z before ever reaching slot i! Thus x is minimal in <r and stands first in at least one valid sequence, matching a legal Deq() returning x.∎

This outlines Herlihy and Wing's proof in §4.3.2 (Lemmas 15–16 and Theorem 17); full formal details appear in their companion technical report.

Boundaries of the guarantee

What the rule does not say

The rule of the board is a clean, powerful guarantee—but a house that relies on it should also know the four things it deliberately leaves out:

Checking from outside

Proved by design, not by clocks

Although the rule is defined using real time under the sun, the houses don't need sundials to keep it. They know the rule holds because the design of the board (like House 3's gate or the busy slot board) mathematically guarantees it.

Liveness

Safety only, not eventual service

Linearizability is a safety property: it forbids wrong answers, but doesn't guarantee that an operation eventually finishes. On the busy board, a slow loader could theoretically see every slot emptied by faster loaders just before reaching it. Guaranteeing progress (wait-freedom or starvation-freedom) is a separate requirement.

Replication protocol

What to promise, not how to copy

The rule defines what multiple copies must look like from the outside, but leaves the replication protocol—the messages that keep copies synchronized across a network—to consensus and replication algorithms.

Overlapping fairness

Real time, not causal fairness

When two requests overlap in real time, linearizability allows either one to go first—even if a message passed between them. Systems that care about message chains rather than wall-clock spans use causal ordering (tallies) instead.

What the rule of the board does promise is the gold standard for single-object consistency: whatever concurrency or replication hides behind the gate, every request is filled as though a single steward with a single board handled them one at a time in real-world order.

A shared object is linearizable if every operation appears to take effect at a single instant between its invocation and its response, in an order that respects real time.

Whether backed by one copy or many, each linearizable object can be verified in isolation, and no caller is forced to wait when an operation is always defined.

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Maurice P. Herlihy and Jeannette M. Wing, Linearizability: A Correctness Condition for Concurrent Objects. ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems 12, 3 (July 1990), 463–492. Covers the definition of linearizability, locality (Theorem 1), the nonblocking property (Theorem 2), the comparison with sequential consistency and serializability, the proof method using set-valued abstraction functions, and the lock-free concurrent queue.

The ideas. Herlihy and Wing propose that a concurrent object is correct if every operation appears to take effect instantaneously at some single moment between its invocation and its response. That ensures the object behaves like a single copy executing operations one at a time, while respecting real-time order: any operation that finishes before another begins must take effect first. Because linearizability is defined purely in terms of external calls and responses, clients cannot tell whether an object is implemented by a single copy, replicated across machines, or shared without locks.

Crucially, real-time order is distinct from Lamport's causal order. One operation can finish before another starts without any system message passing between them (as when information travels out-of-band, like our goatherd), and conversely, two operations linked by a message can still overlap in real time. Neither ordering contains the other.

Two properties make linearizability ideal for building modular systems: it is local (a system is linearizable if and only if each individual object is linearizable) and nonblocking (a pending total operation is never forced to wait for another operation). Dropping the real-time requirement gives sequential consistency, which loses locality; applying the one-at-a-time requirement to multi-operation transactions gives serializability, which loses both locality and the nonblocking property.

Finally, when concurrent races make it impossible to point to a single line of code as an operation's linearization point—as in Herlihy and Wing's lock-free queue—we can still prove linearizability by mapping each concrete state to the set of abstract queue orders it could represent, and showing that every such order is valid.
