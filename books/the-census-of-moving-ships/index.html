The Census of Moving Ships

Being an account of how the grain of a whole harbour was counted without stopping a single lighter

In which the scribes produce a total that is certainly correct, that answers the question the archon asked, and that describes a morning nobody in Arche ever lived through.

Chapter I

The Archon's Question

Kleitos, archon of Arche in a year when the grain ships came late, asked the customs house a question that sounded simple and was not. He wanted to know how much grain the harbour held. Not how much had been landed, which the tolls recorded, nor how much had been sold, which the houses recorded, but how much was there, at one moment, everywhere at once.

The difficulty is the shape of the harbour. Grain sits in three places: the town granary behind the customs house, and two outports on the low islets offshore — the Beacon, which is a rock with a light and a stone shed, and the Reef, which is a longer islet with a stone jetty and two sheds. Between these three places grain moves continually, in lighters, all day. A lighter loaded at the Beacon at dawn may be alongside the town quay by mid-morning or, if the wind fails, by dusk.

The scribes' first proposal was to send a man to each place to count, and to add up the three counts. Chandyos, who kept the customs house, sent the men out and then went down to the quay and watched the lighters for an hour, and came back and said the proposal was worthless. He was able to show why with two mornings' worth of arithmetic.

Suppose the Beacon's man counts at dawn and the town's man counts at noon. In between, a lighter carries two hundred measures from the Beacon to the town. The Beacon counted them; the town counted them again. The archon's total is two hundred measures too high, and the harbour has grown grain by being asked about it.

Reverse it. The town counts at dawn and the Beacon at noon, and in between the lighter carries two hundred measures from the town to the Beacon. Now nobody counted them at all: they had left the town before the town looked, and had not reached the Beacon when the Beacon looked. Two hundred measures have vanished, and there is no thief.

The error, stated once

Counting the places one after another gives a total that is wrong by exactly the grain that moved between one counting and the next. The error can run either way, it cannot be bounded in advance, and nothing about the three counts reveals that it has happened.

Chapter II

Why the Harbour Cannot Be Stopped

There is an obvious remedy and the archon proposed it: stop the harbour. Let no lighter move from one hour, count everything, and let them move again. Then there is nothing in transit and the three counts add up.

Chandyos objected on two grounds, and the second is the interesting one.

The first is practical. Arche's business is the moving of grain, and a harbour that stops moving grain has stopped being a harbour. A census that costs the town a day's trade every time the archon is curious will be taken once and never again.

The second is that it cannot be done. To stop the harbour, the customs house must tell the Beacon and the Reef to stop, and that telling goes by boat, and the boat takes as long as it takes. The Beacon may receive the order an hour before the Reef. In that hour the Reef, which has not yet heard, will quite properly load a lighter and send it off. There is no instant at which the customs house can know that everything has stopped, because knowing that would require exactly the thing the harbour does not have — a way of learning what is true elsewhere at this moment.

So the order to stop does not produce a stopped harbour. It produces a harbour in which each place has stopped at a different time, which is the same difficulty as before, wearing a costume.

Stated formally The system is a set of processes connected by channels. A channel delivers messages in the order sent, without error, after an arbitrary but finite delay. There is no shared clock and no process can observe another's state directly. A global state is the state of every process together with the state of every channel.

Lamportios, who had come across from the tally house to listen, made the observation that unlocked the whole thing. The scribes had been trying to catch the harbour at an instant. They should stop. What the archon actually needs is a total that is sound — one that no lighter has been double-counted into and none has fallen out of. Whether such a total was ever simultaneously true of the harbour is a separate question, and, it turns out, one the archon does not need answered.

Chapter III

What the Lanes Are Holding

The step that makes the census possible is small and nobody had taken it: the grain in the lighters is part of the harbour's grain.

This sounds like a truism and is not. All three counting places think of themselves as counting sheds. Grain in a shed is grain; grain in a lighter halfway across the water is in nobody's shed and appears in nobody's count. But it exists, the archon is asking about it, and it is precisely the quantity that the naïve method loses or duplicates.

So the harbour is to be counted as six things, not three: the town granary, the Beacon's shed, the Reef's two sheds — and, separately, what each lane is holding. There are six lanes, since each of the three places sends to each of the other two, and the outbound and inbound lanes are counted apart.

The six quantities

A complete account of the harbour's grain is: what is in each of the three places, plus what is afloat on each of the six lanes between them. A method that records only the first three is not an incomplete account of the harbour. It is a complete account of a different harbour, one in which lighters do not exist.

Now, no one can see a lane. A lighter in mid-water is not observable from either shore, and the customs house cannot send a man to stand on the sea. What a place can do is notice what comes in through its own jetty. And that, as it turns out, is enough — provided the place knows when to start noticing and when to stop.

Chapter IV

The Census Pennant

The arrangement Chandyos and Lamportios settled on uses one new object: a plain red pennant, of which the customs house keeps a chest. A pennant is not a message about grain. It carries no cargo and no instruction. It is a mark in a lane, and its whole purpose is to divide what came before it from what came after.

Two rules govern it, one for sending and one for receiving, and they are the entire method.

The rule of sending
  1. A place records what is in its own sheds.
  2. Then, down every lane leading out of it, and before it despatches any further grain down that lane, it sends one pennant.
The rule of receiving
  1. A pennant arrives up some lane. If the place has not yet recorded its own sheds — it records them now, writes down that this lane was holding nothing, and then obeys the rule of sending, pennanting every lane out of it.
  2. If the place has already recorded its sheds — it writes down, as that lane's holding, everything that arrived up that lane between the moment it recorded and the arrival of this pennant.

That is all. Any place may begin; the customs house usually does. Nothing stops, no lighter waits, and no place has to know anything about any other place's business.

The second clause of the receiving rule is the one that does the real work, and it is worth reading twice. A place that has already counted its sheds goes on accepting lighters as usual — but from that moment it keeps a second list, per lane, of what comes up that lane. When the pennant finally appears at the end of that lane, the list is closed, and the list is the lane's holding. The place never had to see the water. It only had to know when to open the list and when to close it, and the pennant told it both.

Two properties of the lanes are being leaned on, and both should be said aloud. Lighters on a given lane do not overtake one another, so a pennant sent down a lane after some grain arrives after that grain and before anything sent later. And no lighter is lost. Neither is a law of the sea; both are arrangements of the harbour, and the census fails if they fail.

Notice that the town did not need to know how many lighters were on the water, and the Beacon did not need to know when the town had recorded. The pennant carried the only fact either of them required, and it carried it by arriving.

Chapter V

Cuts That Hold and Cuts That Do Not

Draw the morning as a picture and the method acquires a shape. Each place runs down its own line; each lighter is an arrow from a loading to an unloading. The census draws a line across the whole picture — through each place at the moment it recorded its sheds. Chandyos called it a cut, and it need not be straight: each place recorded when it recorded, and the line bends to suit.

Everything above the cut is the harbour the census describes. Everything below is the harbour's afternoon. An arrow that crosses the cut going downward is a lighter afloat at the moment of the census — the thing the second receiving rule catches.

And an arrow that crosses the cut going upward is the disaster.

The pennant makes the upward crossing impossible, and it does so by construction rather than by vigilance. A place pennants a lane before sending any further grain down it. So no grain despatched after a place recorded can overtake the pennant, and therefore no such grain can be counted by a receiver as having arrived before its own recording. The rule of sending is not an efficiency; it is the whole of the guarantee.

Proof — that no lighter crosses the cut upward
  1. Call an event at a place early if it happened before that place recorded its sheds, and late otherwise.
  2. Suppose some lighter were loaded late at the Reef and unloaded early at the town. That is the upward crossing.
  3. The Reef recorded before it loaded that lighter, since the loading was late. By the rule of sending, the Reef had already sent a pennant down the town lane at the moment it recorded — before despatching any further grain down that lane.
  4. Lighters on a lane do not overtake one another. So the pennant reached the town before that lighter did.
  5. By the rule of receiving, the town recorded its sheds no later than the arrival of that pennant — either it had already recorded, or the pennant made it record then.
  6. So the town had recorded before the lighter arrived, which makes the unloading late, not early — contradicting step 2.∎

One further matter, easily overlooked: the census ends. If every place can be reached from every other by some chain of lanes, then once any one place records, pennants travel out along every lane, and each place that receives one records and pennants onward. Since lanes are finite in number and no lighter is lost, every place records and every lane is closed, and the customs house eventually has six numbers.

Chapter VI

A Morning Nobody Lived Through

The six numbers came in and Chandyos added them, and then he did something that unsettled the customs house for a week: he checked the total against the harbour, and could not find a moment when it had been true.

The town had recorded at the third hour. The Reef had recorded at the fourth. Between those hours the Reef had sold eighty measures over its own jetty to a coasting ship — a transaction entirely internal to the Reef, involving no lane and no pennant. The recorded total therefore describes a harbour in which the town stands as it stood at the third hour and the Reef stands as it stood at the fourth. No clock ever struck an hour at which both were true together.

And yet the total is not wrong. This is the part that requires care.

What the total actually is

Call the recorded total S*, the harbour as it stood when the census began Sbegin, and the harbour as it stood when the last lane was closed Send. Then:

S* is a harbour the morning could have reached from Sbegin, by the same events that really happened, merely taken in a different order. And from S*, the same events continue and arrive at Send, which really did happen.

So S* sits between the two, on a road the harbour could have walked. It is not a fiction. It is a morning made of Arche's own real events, re-ordered only where the harbour itself never fixed an order.

The re-ordering is licensed by exactly the thing Book the First was about. Two events at two different places, with no chain of lighters joining them, stand in no order at all as far as the harbour is concerned. The census is free to treat either as first, because nothing in the harbour distinguishes them. What it may never do is reverse two events that are joined — and the pennant is what prevents that.

Proof — that the recorded total lies on a road the harbour could have walked
  1. Write the morning's real events in the order they occurred: e1, e2, … Each is early or late, as defined before, according to whether the place it happened at had yet recorded.
  2. Claim: wherever a late event is immediately followed by an early one in this list, the two may be swapped without changing anything that follows.
  3. They are at different places. If they were at the same place, the earlier of the two would be early and the later late, never the other way round, since a place records once and does not un-record.
  4. Neither can depend on the other. The late event cannot have caused the early one: that would require a lighter from the late event's place arriving at the early event's place before it recorded, which the previous chapter showed is impossible. And the early event cannot have caused the late one, because it comes after it in the list.
  5. Two events at different places with no dependence between them commute: performing them in either order leaves every place and every lane in the same condition.
  6. Apply the swap repeatedly. Each swap moves an early event one place earlier in the list, so the process terminates, and it ends with a list in which every early event precedes every late one.
  7. That re-ordered list is a perfectly possible morning for Arche — it is made of the same events and violates no dependence. Run it, and pause at the moment the last early event finishes. Every place stands exactly as it recorded itself, and every lane holds exactly what was recorded for it. That is S*.
  8. Hence S* is reachable from Sbegin, and continuing the list to its end reaches Send.∎

Chandyos put it to the archon in a sentence that the customs house has repeated ever since: you asked for a morning, and I have given you a morning; it is only not the one you had.

Chapter VII

What Such a Total Is Good For

A total describing a morning that never happened is obviously useless for some purposes and it is worth being exact about which.

It will not settle a dispute about the third hour. If a merchant claims the Reef held four hundred measures when the bell rang, the census cannot confirm or deny it, because the census is not about the bell.

What it settles — completely, with no doubt at all — are questions of a particular shape: those whose answer, once it becomes yes, stays yes. Chandyos called such a question settled. Whether grain has been lost is settled: grain that has gone missing does not come back. Whether every lighter has finished its run is settled: a harbour at rest does not start itself. Whether a lane has silted closed is settled.

Why settled questions can be answered

Ask a settled question of the recorded total.

If the answer is yes — then it is yes of the harbour now. For S* lies on a road to Send, and a settled question that is yes anywhere on a road is yes everywhere further along it.

If the answer is no — then it was no when the census began. For S* lies on a road from Sbegin, and had it been yes at the beginning it would still have been yes at S*.

Either way the census gives a fact about a moment that really existed. That the morning in between was invented does not matter, because a settled question cannot tell the difference.

The archon's question — how much grain is there — is not itself of this shape, and Chandyos said so. What the archon got was a sound accounting: a total in which nothing was counted twice and nothing fell in the water. For the purpose he had, which was to know whether the granaries would hold out, it served. For settling what the Reef held at the third hour, nothing whatever will serve, and the customs house has stopped apologising for it.

Chapter VIII

In the Book, In the Paper

So that a reader may check the allegory rather than trust it.

In this bookWhat it means
a placeThe town, the Beacon, the Reef — each holding grain and acting only on what reaches its own jetty. A process.
a laneA route between two places, one-way, carrying lighters in the order despatched, losing none, taking as long as it takes. A channel: FIFO, error-free, arbitrary but finite delay.
what a lane is holdingGrain afloat: despatched and not yet arrived. Invisible from either shore. Channel state.
the six quantitiesThree sheds and six lanes together. A global state — process states plus channel states.
the census pennantA mark sent down a lane, carrying nothing, whose only function is to divide before from after. A marker.
the rule of sendingRecord your sheds, then pennant every outgoing lane before despatching anything further down it. The Marker-Sending Rule.
the rule of receivingIf you have not recorded, record now and call this lane empty; if you have, this lane held everything that arrived since you recorded. The Marker-Receiving Rule.
a cutThe line through each place at the moment it recorded. Bends, because places record at different times. A consistent cut.
crossing upwardA lighter unloaded before the cut and loaded after it. Describes a harbour that cannot exist, and the pennant makes it impossible. An inconsistent cut — a receive recorded without its send.
a settled questionOne whose answer, once yes, stays yes. Grain lost; work finished; a lane silted. A stable property.
a road the harbour could have walkedThe same real events in a different order, where the harbour itself fixed no order. A permutation of the computation preserving the happened-before relation.
Sbegin, S*, SendThe harbour when the census began; the total recorded; the harbour when the last lane closed. Sι, S*, Sφ; S* is reachable from Sι, and Sφ from S* — Theorem 1.
The ideas, plainly

A distributed system has no instant at which its whole state can be observed, and halting it to look is both expensive and impossible to do simultaneously. The snapshot algorithm records a global state — every process's state and every channel's contents — while the system continues to run.

Each process, on recording its own state, sends a marker down every outgoing channel before any further message. A process receiving a marker either records its state and declares that channel empty, or, if already recorded, declares that channel's state to be everything received on it since recording. Because markers cannot be overtaken, no message sent after a sender's recording can be counted as received before a receiver's recording — which is exactly the condition that makes the cut consistent.

The recorded state need not be one the system ever occupied. What is guaranteed is that it is reachable from the state at which the algorithm started, and that the state at which the algorithm finished is reachable from it. It lies on a possible execution between two real ones.

That is sufficient for detecting stable properties — those which, once true, remain true. If the property holds in the recorded state it holds now; if it does not, it did not hold when the algorithm began. Deadlock and termination are the standard examples.
