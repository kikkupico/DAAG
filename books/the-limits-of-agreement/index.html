The Limits of Agreement

Can a group of computers always reach an agreement when messages can take any amount of time and even one machine might silently stop?

To find out, we follow four part-time legislators in the Chamber of Paxos. Separated across a wide rotunda with poor acoustics, communicating only by messengers across the floor, they must agree whether to paint it blue or red, knowing that at any moment, one of them may step out into the olive grove.

To find out, we follow the proof step by step. Four processes, each holding an input value, must settle on one decision over a network with no bound on message delay, knowing that any one of them may already have crashed.

After Michael J. Fischer, Nancy A. Lynch and Michael S. Paterson, Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process, 1985

Each demand closes off an easy shortcut. The second demand rules out agreeing in advance to paint it blue regardless of what anyone favoured on entering, which keeps everyone in agreement but makes their opinions pointless. The third demand rules out never painting anything at all, which keeps the first two demands by doing nothing. One more rule binds the four: they cannot draw lots or flip coins. Each legislator acts by a fixed rule, based only on their own initial opinion and the notes that have reached their bench.Each property closes off an easy shortcut. Validity rules out the trivial protocol that always decides 0 regardless of the inputs, which guarantees agreement but makes the inputs pointless. Termination rules out the protocol where nobody ever decides, which satisfies the first two properties by doing nothing. The second property is slightly stronger than what the paper requires, which is only that both outcomes be possible; the stronger form keeps the argument short. One more rule binds the four: there is no randomization. Each process acts deterministically, driven only by its own input and the messages that have reached it.

Three ways out

What would have to change

Slow messengers alone do not cause the deadlock, and neither do notes that arrive out of order. The deadlock comes from two things together: no longest walk for a messenger, and an absent legislator looking just like a slow messenger. Changing any one of three assumptions breaks it.Slow messages alone do not cause the impossibility, and neither do messages that arrive out of order. The impossibility comes from two things together: no bound on message delay, and a crashed process looking just like a slow message. Changing any one of three assumptions breaks it.

One

A deadline on messengersA bound on message delay

If every messenger arrives within a known time, silence past that time proves a bench is empty, and the remaining legislators can go on without it.If every message arrives within a known time bound, silence past that deadline proves a process has crashed, and the remaining processes can go on without it.

Two

An attendance slateAn imperfect failure detector

Give each legislator an attendance slate that marks who appears to have left, provided it eventually marks every absent member and eventually spares at least one present member.*Give each process a failure detector that suspects crashed processes, provided it eventually suspects every crashed process and eventually spares at least one correct process.*

Three

Departures only before the sessionCrashes only before the start

If no legislator steps out once the session has begun, and absences happen only beforehand, a present majority can always settle the colour.If no process crashes once the protocol has begun, and crashes happen only beforehand, a correct majority can always reach agreement.

When none of these holds in the Chamber, the legislators give up the promise that every session finishes on its own: their numbered ballots keep the scrolls safe through any delay or absence, and settle the colour as soon as one president's notes go back and forth unchallenged.* Across the water on Mount Phyle, the mercenaries take two other paths: drawing lots in the tent breaks a tie by chance,* and waiting for the mountain wind to drop gives their birds a bounded flight at last.*When none of these holds in an asynchronous network, practical protocols give up unconditional termination: Paxos guarantees safety under all delays and crashes, and makes progress whenever a single leader is able to communicate with a majority.* Randomization* and partial synchrony* take the other two paths.

When messages may take any amount of time and even one machine may crash, no deterministic protocol can guarantee that the group ever decides.

Agreement itself is never broken. What cannot be promised is an end to the waiting.

Real-world distributed systems live with this result by relaxing the rules slightly: assuming message delays eventually stay within bounds, using imperfect failure detectors, or flipping coins.

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Michael J. Fischer, Nancy A. Lynch and Michael S. Paterson, “Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process,” Journal of the ACM 32, 2 (April 1985), 374–382.

The ideas. No deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous network if even a single process is allowed to crash. The proof centers on bivalence (what our story calls an open position): a system configuration from which both possible decisions are still reachable. The authors prove two key lemmas: first, every protocol has at least one bivalent initial configuration (Lemma 2); second, from any bivalent configuration, any pending message delivery can be delayed behind other steps so that delivering it still leaves the system in a bivalent configuration (Lemma 3). Chaining these together lets an adversarial scheduler construct an infinite, fair run in which every process takes steps and every message is delivered, yet no decision is ever reached. Three subtleties make the theorem especially striking: the infinite run constructed by the proof contains no actual crash at all (the single permitted fault is used only to show that processes cannot wait for everyone); safety (agreement and validity) is never violated, and only termination (liveness) is lost; and the impossibility holds even when we only require a single process to eventually decide. The root obstacle is not slow messages alone, but the inability to distinguish a crashed process from a slow one. Relaxing that constraint by bounding message delays (even only eventually), adding an unreliable failure detector, allowing randomness, or restricting crashes to occur only before the protocol begins (as shown in §4 of the paper) makes consensus solvable again.
