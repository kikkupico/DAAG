One Leader at a Time

Many replicated systems put a single leader in charge at a time so that operations can be ordered without dueling ballots. When that leader crashes or falls silent, how does a new leader take over without losing anything the previous leader promised?

To find out, we visit Schedia — a sister city on the eastern arm of Paxos with laws of its own — where five legislators maintain the city's law from a central podium held by one person at a time, and see how a new holder takes the podium whenever the previous one goes silent.

We find out by following the protocol directly: a group of five replicas with one primary at a time, and how a new primary takes over whenever the old one goes silent without losing anything committed.

After Brian M. Oki and Barbara H. Liskov, Viewstamped Replication: A New Primary Copy Method to Support Highly-Available Distributed Systems, 1988

In the paper · viewids and viewstamps

A view identifier is a pair viewid = ⟨cnt, mid⟩, totally ordered lexicographically and unique to its creator mid; a timestamp ts is unique and ordered within a view; a viewstamp = ⟨viewid, ts⟩ pairs the two. Each cohort's history is a sequence of viewstamps (the largest timestamp it holds for each view it has participated in), and because event records arrive in order without gaps, its state reflects every event up to each viewstamp in its history.       Overlapping majorities

      Both protocols rest on the exact same mathematical foundation: any two majorities of a group share at least one member, ensuring that whatever a majority committed in the past is discovered and preserved in the future.

      The paper behind this book

      Brian M. Oki and Barbara H. Liskov, Viewstamped Replication: A New Primary Copy Method to Support Highly-Available Distributed Systems. Proceedings of the Seventh Annual ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), Toronto, August 1988, 8–17. — Introduces the primary-copy replication method, viewids and viewstamps, forcing event records to a sub-majority of backups, the view-change algorithm and its crash-safe formation conditions, and integration with two-phase commit.

      The ideas, plainly.

      Oki and Liskov replicate a service across a group of cohorts that may crash, recover, and suffer network partitions or lost messages, but never lie. In each view, a single cohort acts as the primary, executing client calls and streaming event records in timestamp order to the passive backups. An event is committed once a majority of the group holds it (the primary plus a sub-majority of backups via force-to).

      Each period under a single primary is a view identified by a unique, totally ordered viewid = ⟨cnt, mid⟩, and each event is tagged with a viewstamp = ⟨viewid, ts⟩. When the primary appears unreachable or membership changes, any cohort can act as a view manager and invite the group into a higher view. It collects responses from a majority of cohorts, chooses the respondent with the latest viewstamp as the new primary, and starts the new view by broadcasting that primary's full state. Because any two majorities intersect and event records arrive in order without gaps, the respondent with the largest viewstamp in a majority is guaranteed to hold every committed event.

      Because cohorts keep their logs and state in volatile memory rather than writing every event to disk, a cohort that crashes and recovers loses its log and remembers only the cur-viewid stored on disk. Three view-formation conditions prevent a new view from forming if too many cohorts have lost their volatile state to guarantee that all committed events are present: if a majority crash at once, the group halts safely rather than starting a view with missing history. Meanwhile, a deposed primary in a minority partition can do no harm because its old viewid is rejected by the majority in the new view.
