Paxos · The Scholars' Coast

Libraries That Agree in the End

Libraries up and down a coast, each keeping its own copy of one compendium of knowledge, changing it at once, sending word of every change by courier, and never waiting for a vote.

Read this first, and any book set on the scholars' coast can be read next, in any order. It says who the libraries are, what carries word between them and what the couriers carry, which every book here takes for granted. The coast itself comes last.

Libraries and Readers

The coast is a coast of libraries, and together they keep one compendium of what is known: an entry for each subject, filed under its heading. Every library keeps its own copy of the whole compendium, and its scholars add entries, revise them and withdraw them, at once and without asking anyone. The libraries are alike; nothing sets one apart but the part an example gives it. They hold no assembly, and nothing here waits for a vote.

A reader is whoever consults a library. A library answers a reader from its own copy, as a matter of course, and lives with copies that differ for a while. An out-of-date entry is put right when word arrives, and what matters is that every copy agrees in the end. An entry is knowledge, not law, so agreeing in the end is enough. Nothing leaves a library: a reader consults the compendium in its reading room.

That is the line between this coast and the rest of the series. At the camps of Arche, answering from a stale note is done knowingly and only while birds are lost, because two mercenaries told different plans is a real harm. Here, copies that differ for a while are the ordinary state of things. The scholars call it concord: everyone here ends of one mind, by merging rather than by assembly.

Couriers

Word travels between the libraries by courier and by word of mouth. Couriers take routes of different lengths and are delayed or stopped, and libraries close. A courier who is stopped cuts one part of the coast off from the rest for as long as the courier is stopped. An inlet cuts a few libraries off from the rest by land, and the only crossing is a ferry, which many routes share.

Travelling scholars carry news by word of mouth, passing it to whomever they meet. Nobody organises the spread, and nobody knows who has heard what.

What the Couriers Carry

Couriers carry revised entries, slips naming one change, and notices that an entry is withdrawn. An entry is one thing kept, and a version is one state of it. Which version is newer is settled by a tally: the day and hour by the sun at which it was written, with the library's number to break a tie. The plain rule keeps the version with the larger tally; a withdrawal travels as a notice with a tally of its own.

Every library keeps the whole compendium, but each entry has three home libraries that answer for it: a revision goes to them first, and a reader who wants the newest version asks them. The other libraries learn of it later, by the spread.

Every book on the coast keeps the same libraries and the same couriers. What changes from book to book is the question asked of them, and with it what the couriers carry.

Homonoia

Paxos is shaped like a Y. Two arms reach north and face each other across a bay: the western arm holds the Chamber of the Paxon Parliament, the eastern arm the city of Schedia and its Tholos. The arms join at a fork, where the oracle stands, and below the fork the stem widens into the body of the island, the scholars' coast, which the scholars call Homonoia, concord. The libraries are within casual reach of one another, and the island road runs up the stem to the fork.

The Chamber is a morning's walk away. The Parliament passes the law of Paxos, but an entry in the compendium is not law, so nothing on the coast is taken to it.

Most of what happens here happens in the colonnades. There is a stoa where travelling scholars pass on news, a festival ground where copyists once a year compare their whole copies of the compendium, and in every library a reading room with the compendium in its pigeonholes, an entry to a short scroll, and a desk where marble boards of pebbles keep the counts some entries hold, one board to an entry. Greek scholarship already did everything this coast needs: collating manuscripts line by line, and spreading the epics by rhapsode.

Close means the real thing is much like it; loose means it is borrowed and changed; invented means the setting made it up; anachronism means the real thing is of another age.
