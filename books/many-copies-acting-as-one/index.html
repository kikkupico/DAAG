Many Copies, Acting as One

When data is kept in several copies, what does it take for them to act as one? And who could ever tell the difference?

We find out by exploring how three ancient Greek trading houses filled one another's orders in the sequence they really came, though none of them could read the hour.

After Maurice P. Herlihy and Jeannette M. Wing, Linearizability: A Correctness Condition for Concurrent Objects, 1990

First, in machine terms

Copies that must act as one

A shared object, such as a queue, may be kept in several copies on several machines. Programmers want it to behave as if there were one copy.

  • The problem: calls on the object overlap in time, and each takes time. Agreeing on some one-at-a-time order is not enough: a call that finished before another began must come first.
  • The condition (Herlihy and Wing, 1990), linearizability: every call appears to take effect at one instant between its start and its answer, and the answers are those of a single copy.
  • The results: each object can be checked alone, a caller is never made to wait where the call is always defined, and the condition can be proved even for a queue no one can pause.

This edition tells it with three trading houses and the board where House 3 keeps its waiting orders.

Did anything go wrong? By the tallies, no: no slip joined the two orders, so either could come first. But House 2 knows, and so does anyone who talked to the goatherd, that House 1's order had been entered and confirmed before House 2 even asked. What the sun saw, House 1 was first.

The tallies have always had this gap. They respect every slip, and the goatherd carries none. One answer is to make better clocks, kept so close together by the slips that nobody crossing the mountain can arrive before leaving.* That is a way of catching the goatherd. The houses want something else: a promise about how orders are filled that no goatherd can ever catch them breaking, whatever clocks they keep or do without. What would such a promise say?

A promise in what the houses see

The rule of the board

A house sees two moments of its order: when it sends the order, and when the confirmation comes back. In between, the order was put on the board where House 3 keeps its waiting orders.

A loader at House 3 goes to the board, takes an order and loads the grain for it. That too begins and ends. Each house, and each loader, does one thing at a time.

So every piece of business has a span, from its beginning to its end. One piece of business comes before another when it was over before the other began. Otherwise the two overlap, and neither came first. This is judged by the sun, whether or not anyone could see it.

Before, or overlap? choose two orders

Each line is one house. A thick stretch is the span of one order, from its sending to its confirmation. Later moments are further out. Choose the first dot of any two orders.

Now the promise. Picture a single keeper with a single board, doing one thing at a time: they enter an order, or they serve the oldest waiting one. No order goes onto their board and none comes off it except through their own hands. The houses cannot see the keeper and do not need to. They need only this: all the orders and all the servings can be set in one sequence such that three demands hold.

First demand

The same as one keeper

Every serving takes the order that a single keeper, with a single board, taking everything in that sequence, would have served.

Second demand

A moment inside the span

Each piece of business takes its place in the sequence at some moment between its beginning and its end.

Third demand

Finished first, comes first

Anything finished before another began comes before it.

Why does the second demand matter? Without it the keeper could act whenever it suited them, and the promise would say nothing about the sun. With it, the third follows: if House 1's order was over before House 2's began, the keeper's moment for House 1 came first, and a single keeper serves the older order first. So the goatherd's morning breaks the rule.

The rule of the board, stated formally

Write <H for the real-time order of a history H: a <H b when a responds before b is invoked. The board is a FIFO queue: an order is Enq, a serving is Deq.

All the orders and all the servings can be set in a single sequence such that:

  1. every serving takes the order that a single keeper, with a single board, taking everything in that sequence, would have served;
  2. each piece of business takes its place in the sequence at some moment between its beginning and its end; so that
  3. anything finished before another began comes before it.

Linearizability, §2.2: a history is linearizable if, after adding responses to some pending invocations and dropping the rest, it is equivalent to a legal sequential history that respects the real-time order <H.

The rule and the tallies

The tallies' first, and the sun's first

The rule and the tallies do not give the same answer, and neither includes the other.

For the tallies, one order is first if a chain of slips leads from it to the other. Orders no chain joins are put in sequence by tally and house number, fairly but arbitrarily.* For the sun, one order is first if it was over before the other began, whether or not any slip joined them.

Two mornings choose one

The number in a dot is the tally the order was written under. A thick stretch is an order's span. An arc is a slip. Later moments are further out.

Choose a morning.

On the goatherd's morning no slip joined the two orders, so the tallies could put House 2 first, and the sun put House 1 first. Turn it round: House 1 sends an order, and before it is confirmed sends House 2 a slip about it, and House 2 then orders. A chain of slips joins them, so the tallies put House 1 first. But House 1's order was not over when House 2's began, so for the sun they overlap, and the rule would let House 2 go first.

Can the rule be kept at all? Easily, by the arrangement the tallies were invented to avoid. Let House 3 keep the only board and enter each order when its slip reaches the gate. The entry falls after the slip was written and before the confirmation leaves, so it lies inside the order's span. Serve in order of entry, and the keeper of the rule is House 3 itself.

Two Kinds of First

Serving orders in the sequence they reach House 3's gate keeps the rule of the board. Serving them in the sequence of their tallies need not. And the tallies' sequence can put one order before another where the rule of the board would allow either.

Proof — that serving by entry keeps the rule

Write <H for the real-time order of a history H: a <H b when a responds before b is invoked. H is linearizable if it can be extended to a history equivalent to a legal sequential history S with <H ⊆ <S. For the board, a FIFO queue, legal means each Deq returns the oldest item Enq-ed and not yet returned. The implementation is one copy at House 3's gate, and an operation takes effect at the step where its request is applied. Claim. The operations in the order of those steps form such an S.

  1. Put all orders and servings in sequence by the moment each is done at the board: an order when the keeper enters it, a serving when a loader takes an order. This is one sequence.
  2. Each serving takes the earliest order entered and not yet served, which is what a single keeper with a single board would serve, so clause (1) holds.
  3. An order is entered after its slip was written and before word of its entry leaves, so its moment lies within it. A serving's moment lies within the loader's business. That is clause (2), and clause (3) follows: whatever was finished before another began had its moment first.∎

Then what was wrong with serving by arrival? Only that the house with the shorter road wins every pair of orders that overlap. The rule does not mind, because it says nothing about orders that overlap. The tallies do mind, and pay for it by sometimes serving a later order first. The two answers protect different things.

One board, or three

Copies nobody can count

One board at House 3's gate keeps the rule. But must there be only one? Each house might rather keep a copy of the board close at hand.

The rule mentions only sendings, confirmations and servings, all things that happen at the houses' gates. It never mentions the board itself. So it cannot tell one board from three copies kept in step by slips, or from any other arrangement behind the gates. Whatever stands behind them is judged by what comes out of them.

At the gate

What a house sees

A house sends an order, gets its confirmation, and sees loaders serve.

Behind the gate

What it cannot see

One board, three copies kept in step by slips, or any other arrangement. The rule is silent on all of them.

Acting as one

No way to tell

No house, comparing notes with every other and with anyone who watched the sun, could tell that there is more than one board.

How copies might be kept so is another matter, and this book does not take it up. What it takes up is what the promise gives to a house that relies on it.

Grain is not the only thing on order

Grain and oil

House 2 presses oil, and all three houses order it the same way, on a board of its own at House 2. If the rule had to hold over both boards together, every change to how one board is kept would have to be weighed against the other. Does it?

No. It is enough that each board keeps the rule by itself.

One Board at a Time

All the business keeps the rule if and only if, for every board, the business on that board, taken by itself, keeps it.

Why should two boards fit together without anyone arranging it? Because the sun ties them, and the sun never contradicts itself. Two boards could fail to fit only through a loop: one order before another on the grain board, that one before a third by the sun, and so round to the start. The proof shows that such a loop would need the sun to put something before itself.

Is this true of any sensible rule? Take the sun out and see. Ask only that everything could have come from a single keeper, with each house's own business in the order it did it, and nothing said about moments. Under that looser rule the goatherd's morning is fine. Now let House 1 order grain and then oil, and House 2 order oil and then grain. Choose a rule and the page tests the three boards.

Each board passes the looser rule alone, and together they fail it. Houses that kept only the looser rule would need one plan spanning every board. The sun's demand is what lets each board be kept, and judged, on its own.

Proof — that if every board keeps the rule, all the business does

For a history H and an object x, write H|x for the subhistory of operations on x, and <H for the real-time order: a <H b when a responds before b is invoked. Claim (locality, Theorem 1, the "if" direction): if every H|x is linearizable, then H is.

  1. For each board, choose a sequence and moments that show it keeps the rule. Say one piece of business must go before another if they are on the same board and its sequence puts the first earlier, or if the sun does: the first finished before the second began. Follow chains of these.
  2. If no chain leads from a piece of business back to itself, set everything in any single sequence that keeps every must go before. It keeps each board's own sequence, so every serving is what a single keeper would do; and it keeps the sun's sequence, which is clause (3), and clause (2) with it. So it remains to show there is no loop.
  3. Suppose there is one, e₁, …, en, back to e₁, as short as any loop can be.
  4. All on one board. A board's sequence puts any two of its pieces one way or the other, so some step of the loop runs against it, and that step must be the sun's: b finished before a began, though the board put a first. Then that board did not keep clause (3). So the loop touches at least two boards.
  5. Number it so that e₁ is on board x and e₂ on another. The step between them crosses boards, so it is the sun's: e₁ finished before e₂ began.
  6. Nothing else in the loop is on board x. Suppose something is, and let ei be the first after e₂. The step into it crosses boards, so ei−1 finished before ei began. And e₂ began before ei−1 finished, or else e₂, …, ei−1 would be a shorter loop. So e₁ finished before e₂ began, before ei−1 finished, before ei began: e₁ came before ei by the sun, and e₁, ei, …, en is a shorter loop.
  7. So the last step, into e₁, also crosses boards: en finished before e₁ began, and therefore before e₂ began, making e₂, …, en a shorter loop. Every case contradicts the choice of a shortest loop.∎
Could the rule make a house stand at the gate?

Nobody made to wait

Whatever is on the board, a single keeper can always put a new order at the end, so the rule never makes an order wait on anyone else's business.

No Order Waits on Another

If the business so far keeps the rule, a waiting order can be entered now and the rule still kept.

So when a house does wait for its confirmation, the cause is the arrangement behind the board, a slip still on the road, and never the rule. Serving is different: a loader who comes to an empty board has nothing to take, and a single keeper would make them wait until an order comes. That wait is asked for, not forced. Proof — that a waiting order can always be entered

A total operation, such as Enq, has a response in every state. Claim (the nonblocking property, Theorem 2): if H is linearizable and p is a pending invocation of a total operation, then there is a response r such that H extended by p and r is linearizable.

  1. Take a single sequence that shows the business so far keeps the rule.
  2. If it already counts the order as entered, confirm it now; the same sequence still works.
  3. If not, put the order at the end of the sequence and confirm it now. Nothing earlier changes, so clause (1) holds. Its moment is now, within its span, which is clause (2). Nothing began after it finished, so putting it last breaks no before, which is clause (3).∎

If there is no moment to point to, how can anyone show that this board keeps the rule?

The busy board, stated formally

The queue of §4.2. The board is an array items and an index back; slots are never reused.

  1. To enter an order: note the peg's number and move the peg on one, in a single movement. Put the order tablet in that slot. Enq is INC(back) then STORE.
  2. To serve: look at the peg. Go along the slots from 1 up to the one before the peg, and take the first tablet you find, emptying its slot in a single movement. If you find none, go back to the peg and start again. Deq is READ(back) then SWAP along the array.

The way through is to ask what the board could mean at each moment. Once the first clerk's tablet is in, the board could stand for House 2, then House 1, or for House 1, then House 2.

A tablet put in before another clerk even took a number must come first. Any two not so placed may go either way. The page shows the board only once every tablet is in.

The thing to show is this: at every moment, every sequence the board could stand for is one the business so far could honestly have produced. Two facts carry it. A tablet just put in never has to stand ahead of anything already waiting. And a loader going up from slot 1 always takes a tablet that could be first.

The Board Keeps the Rule

At every moment, every sequence of waiting orders the board could stand for is one the business so far could have produced, so the busy board keeps the rule.

Proof — that the board keeps the rule, in outline

Let A: REP → 2ABS map each state of the array and index to the set of queue values it could stand for, and let Lin(H|ABS) be the queue values the business so far could have produced. Write x <r y when the STORE of x precedes the INC of y. Claim (§4.3.2, Theorem 17): for every linearized representation value r, A(r) ⊆ Lin(H|ABS).

  1. An empty board stands only for no orders, which is what no business produces. It is enough to show that every single movement, and every beginning and end of a clerk's or loader's business, keeps it so.
  2. Beginnings and ends put nothing in a slot and take nothing out. A beginning adds business that may or may not have had its moment yet; an end settles one such choice. Neither removes a sequence the board could stand for.
  3. Moving the peg and looking at it move no tablet, so they change nothing.
  4. Putting a tablet in. For a waiting tablet to have to stand behind this one, its clerk would have taken their number after this one was put in, and this one is only now being put in. So the new tablet may go last in every sequence, and the business agrees: an entry may have its moment now, at the back.
  5. Emptying a slot. Say the loader, going up from slot 1, empties slot i and finds tablet x, and some waiting tablet z must come before x because z was put in before x's clerk took their number. Then z's slot is lower, so the loader has already passed it. They went only as far as the peg showed when they looked, so x's number was taken before they looked, and z was put in before that. So z was in its slot when the loader passed it, and they would have taken it. Hence x could be first, and a serving may have its moment now, with x.
  6. Every movement keeps the order, so it holds at every moment.∎

This is an outline, and so is the paper's. Herlihy and Wing give the two facts of steps 4 and 5 and the shape of the argument; the lemmas that make it airtight are in their technical report.

What does a house get from a board known to keep the rule? Everything a single keeper would guarantee: orders are served in the sequence they came by the sun, none vanishes, and none is served that nobody made. Would the obvious one-line promise, no later order is served before an earlier one, have done as well? No: a board that lost tablets would keep that promise.

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Maurice P. Herlihy and Jeannette M. Wing, Linearizability: A Correctness Condition for Concurrent Objects. ACM Transactions on Programming Languages and Systems 12, 3 (July 1990), 463–492. The definition, locality (Theorem 1), the nonblocking property (Theorem 2), the comparison with sequential consistency and serializability, the proof method with set-valued abstraction functions, and the concurrent queue.

The ideas. A shared object is correct, Herlihy and Wing propose, if every operation appears to take effect at a single instant somewhere between its call and its return. Then the results are those of some one-at-a-time execution, and that execution respects real time: an operation that finished before another started comes first. This is linearizability. It lets a concurrent queue be specified as an ordinary queue, and it says nothing about how the object is built, so one copy or many are judged alike.

Real-time order is not Lamport's causal order. An operation can finish before another starts with no message between them, and operations joined by messages can still overlap. Neither order contains the other, and linearizability is the one an outside observer comparing notes would expect.

Linearizability is local: a system is linearizable exactly when each object is. And it is nonblocking: a pending call of an operation that is always defined can always be completed. Sequential consistency drops real time and loses locality; serializability, over multi-operation transactions, loses both and needs a way to abort.

When no single step of an implementation can be named as an operation's instant, Herlihy and Wing map each state to the set of abstract values it could stand for, and show that set is always among the values the history could have produced. Their example is a lock-free queue built from fetch-and-increment and swap.

Herlihy, M. P., and Wing, J. M. Linearizability: a correctness condition for concurrent objects. ACM Trans. Program. Lang. Syst. 12, 3 (1990), 463–492.
