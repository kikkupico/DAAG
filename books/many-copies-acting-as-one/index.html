Many Copies, Acting as One

When data is kept in several copies, what does it take for them to act as one? And who could ever tell the difference?

Grain lands at House 3 and the three houses claim it, first come, first served. The tallies put the claims in an order every house can work out from its slips. But a claim that was entered and confirmed in the morning can still be served after one made at noon. This book asks for first come judged by the sun: every claim served as though one keeper had entered them all on one board, in the order they really came.

Chapter I

First Come

The three trading houses of Arche, House 1, House 2 and House 3, stand round the foot of Mount Phyle. Word passes between them only by messengers on the ring road, and in the trading season the cloud lies so low that no sundial can be read and nobody on the island knows the hour*.

Foreign grain lands at all three houses; take a cargo landed at House 3, which every house needs. A house that wants a cargo sends a claim by messenger, and cargoes go out first come, first served. But which claim came first, when no one can read the hour? The houses settled it with tallies, numbers written on every slip and raised on every arrival, with the houses' numbers to break ties. Claims are served in the order of their tallies. That order never puts a claim ahead of one that led to it, and no house can win by keeping a quicker messenger*.

Here is what the tallies allow. In the morning House 1 claims a cargo; House 3 enters the claim and sends back word that it is entered. A goatherd crossing the mountain by the smugglers' tracks brings the news to House 2, which has had a quiet morning*. At noon House 2 sends a claim of its own. Its tally is low, because no slip has reached it for hours, lower than House 1's. So House 2's claim is served first.

House 2, and anyone who talked to the goatherd, knows what happened by the sun: House 1's claim was entered and confirmed before House 2 had even asked. First come, as the sun would judge it, was House 1. The tallies could not see that, because no slip joined the two claims; the path that joined them went over the mountain.

This book asks what it would take to serve claims first come by the sun, and what such a promise gives. The claims are kept on a board, and it does not matter yet where the board is or how many copies of it there are. What matters is what the houses can see: when they sent each claim, when they heard it was entered, and which claims were served in what order.

Chapter II

The Rule of the Board

A claim takes time. The house sends it; somewhere it is put on the board; then the house hears that it is entered. Serving takes time too: a loader at House 3 goes to the board, takes the next claim, and loads the cargo for that house. Each house and each loader does one thing at a time, but different ones may be in the middle of their business at once.

So two pieces of business stand in one of two relations. If one was finished before the other began, the first came before the second. House 1's claim came before House 2's. Otherwise the two overlap, and neither came before the other.

Before here is judged by the sun, by what actually happened first, whether anyone saw it or not. Nobody on Arche can see that order. It is not something a clerk checks. It is a promise about what anyone comparing notes will find.

The rule of the board All the claims and all the servings can be set in a single order such that:
(1) every serving takes the claim that a single keeper, with a single board, taking everything in that order, would have served;
(2) each piece of business takes its place in the order at some moment between its beginning and its end; so that
(3) anything finished before another began comes before it.
Linearizability, §2.2: a history is linearizable if, after adding responses to some pending invocations and dropping the rest, it is equivalent to a legal sequential history that respects the real-time order <H. The board is a FIFO queue: a claim is Enq, a serving is Deq.

Clause (2) is the heart of it. Each claim must seem to land on the board all at once, at a single moment between its sending and its confirmation, and each serving must seem to take its claim at a single moment too. Clause (3) follows: if House 1's claim was confirmed before House 2's was sent, House 1's moment came first, and a single keeper would serve House 1 first. The morning of Chapter I breaks the rule.

Three smaller cases show the rule's edges.

Claims made at the same time may go either way. House 1 and House 2 send claims at the same time, and neither is confirmed before the other is sent. A loader serves House 2, then House 1. That is allowed: the two claims overlap, and House 2's moment may have come first.

A claim can be served before its confirmation arrives. House 1's claim is entered; before House 1 has heard so, a loader serves it. That is allowed too. The claim's moment came when it was entered, and the word of it was simply still on the road.

A claim is served once. Two loaders each take House 2's single claim and load a cargo for it. No single keeper serves one claim twice, so that is forbidden, however the times fall.

Business still unfinished when we look may be counted either way. A claim sent but not yet confirmed may already be on the board, or not; the rule lets the houses decide, as long as what happens next agrees with the choice.

Chapter III

Two Kinds of First

The tallies and the rule of the board both answer which came first, and they answer differently.

The tallies' first. One claim comes before another if a chain of slips leads from the first to the second: House 3 could only have heard of the second after hearing of the first. Claims with no chain between them are ordered by tally and house number, which is fair but arbitrary*.

The sun's first. One claim comes before another if it was finished before the other began, whether or not any slip joined them. Claims that overlap may go either way.

Neither contains the other. The morning of Chapter I has no chain of slips from House 1's claim to House 2's, so the tallies were free to put House 2 first, and they did; but by the sun House 1 came first. And the other way round: suppose House 1 sends a claim, and before it is confirmed sends House 2 a slip about it, and House 2 then claims. There is a chain of slips from House 1's claim to House 2's, so the tallies put House 1 first. But House 1's claim was not finished when House 2's began. By the sun the two overlap, and the rule of the board would let House 2 be served first.

There is a simple way to keep the rule of the board, and it is the one the tallies were invented to avoid. Let House 3 keep the only board, and enter each claim when its slip reaches the gate. The claim's moment is its entry. That is after the house sent it, since the slip was written first, and before the house hears it is entered, since that word goes out after the entry. Serve in order of entry, and the rule is kept.

Two Kinds of First

Serving claims in the order they reach House 3's gate keeps the rule of the board. Serving them in the order of their tallies need not. And the tallies' order can put one claim before another where the rule of the board would allow either.

Proof — that serving by entry keeps the rule
  1. Order all claims and servings by the moment each is done at the board: a claim when the keeper enters it, a serving when a loader takes a claim. This is one order.
  2. Each serving takes the earliest claim entered and not yet served, which is what a single keeper with a single board would serve, so clause (1) holds.
  3. A claim is entered after its slip was written and before word of its entry leaves, so its moment lies within it. A serving's moment lies within the loader's business. That is clause (2), and clause (3) follows: whatever was finished before another began had its moment first.∎

The trouble with serving by arrival was never the rule of the board. It was fairness between claims that overlap: the house with the shorter road wins them. The rule does not mind that, because it says nothing about claims that overlap. The tallies mind it, and pay for it by sometimes serving a later claim first. The two orders protect different things.

A single board at House 3 is not the only way. The houses could keep copies of the board, one at each house, and still keep the rule, if the copies answer as though they were one. That is the sense of this book's title, and Chapter VIII returns to it.

Chapter IV

One Board at a Time

Grain is not the only thing claimed. Oil from House 2's presses goes out to all three houses, House 2's own storehouse among them, the same way, on a board of its own. Must the houses check the rule over both boards at once? If so, every change in how one board is kept would have to be weighed against the other.

They need not. It is enough that each board keeps the rule by itself.

One Board at a Time

All the business keeps the rule if and only if, for every board, the business on that board, taken by itself, keeps it.

So the grain board can be kept one way and the oil board another, and each need only be shown to keep the rule for itself. Why should two boards fit together without anyone arranging it? Because the sun ties them together, and the sun never contradicts itself. The only way two boards' orders could fail to fit is a loop: this claim before that one on the grain board, that one before another by the sun, and so round to the start. The proof shows such a loop would need the sun to put something before itself.

Proof — that if every board keeps the rule, all the business does
  1. For each board, choose an order and moments that show it keeps the rule. Say one piece of business must go before another if they are on the same board and its order puts the first earlier, or if the sun does: the first finished before the second began. Follow chains of these.
  2. If no chain leads from a piece of business back to itself, set everything in any single order that keeps every must go before. It keeps each board's own order, so every serving is what a single keeper would do; and it keeps the sun's order, which is clause (3), and clause (2) with it. So it remains to show there is no loop.
  3. Suppose there is one, e₁, …, en, back to e₁, as short as any loop can be.
  4. All on one board. A board's order puts any two of its pieces one way or the other, so some step of the loop runs against it, and that step must be the sun's: b finished before a began, though the board put a first. Then that board did not keep clause (3). So the loop touches at least two boards.
  5. Number it so that e₁ is on board x and e₂ on another. The step between them crosses boards, so it is the sun's: e₁ finished before e₂ began.
  6. Nothing else in the loop is on board x. Suppose something is, and let ei be the first after e₂. The step into it crosses boards, so ei−1 finished before ei began. And e₂ began before ei−1 finished, or else e₂, …, ei−1 would be a shorter loop. So e₁ finished before e₂ began, before ei−1 finished, before ei began: e₁ came before ei by the sun, and e₁, ei, …, en is a shorter loop.
  7. So the last step, into e₁, also crosses boards: en finished before e₁ began, and therefore before e₂ began, making e₂, …, en a shorter loop. Every case contradicts the choice of a shortest loop.∎

This should not be taken for granted. Chapter VI shows a reasonable rule under which it fails.

Chapter V

No Claim Waits on Another

A claim can always be entered: whatever is on the board, a single keeper would put the new claim at the end. Could the rule ever force a house to wait for its claim to be entered until someone else's business finished, because entering it now would break the rule? It could not.

No Claim Waits on Another

If the business so far keeps the rule, a waiting claim can be entered now and the rule still kept.

Proof — that a waiting claim can always be entered
  1. Take a single order that shows the business so far keeps the rule.
  2. If it already counts the claim as entered, confirm it now; the same order still works.
  3. If not, put the claim at the end of the order and confirm it now. Nothing earlier changes, so clause (1) holds. Its moment is now, within its span, which is clause (2). Nothing began after it finished, so putting it last breaks no before, which is clause (3).∎

So if a house waits to hear its claim is entered, it is because of how the board is kept, a slip still on the road, and not because the rule demands it.

Serving is different. A loader who comes to an empty board has nothing to take, and a single keeper with an empty board would make him wait until a claim arrives. When he waits, it is because serve the next claim has no answer while there are no claims. The result above is about business that always has an answer.

Chapter VI

Weaker Rules

The rule without the sun. Drop clause (3). Ask only that everything can be set in one order that a single keeper would have followed, with each house's own business in the order it did it. Call it the rule without the sun. Under it the morning of Chapter I is no fault at all: put House 2's claim first, then House 1's, and a single keeper serves exactly as the loader did. Only the sun's clause forbids serving House 2 first.

And the rule without the sun cannot be checked one board at a time. House 1 claims grain and then oil. House 2 claims oil and then grain. The grain loader serves House 2 first; the oil loader serves House 1 first.

Each board kept the rule without the sun, and the two together did not. Houses keeping that rule would need one arrangement spanning every board. Under the rule of the board this cannot happen, by Chapter IV.

Bargains. Some houses want several things done as one: look at the oil board, and if it is empty, claim grain, with nobody else's business in between. Call that a bargain. A rule that all bargains can be set in one order, each done whole, is the rule databases keep. For a bargain of one claim or one serving, its strict form is exactly the rule of the board. The trouble is in the longer bargains. If House 1's bargain looks at the oil board and then claims grain, while House 2's looks at the grain board and then claims oil, and both look first and find their boards empty, then each bargain must come before the other's claim. Neither claim can be confirmed without breaking the rule, and one bargain must be called off. The rule of bargains fails one board at a time as well. So houses that promise bargains need a way to call one off; the rule of the board, whose business is always single, needs none.

Chapter VII

The Board Itself

A rule nobody can check by looking has to be kept by arrangement, and the arrangement shown to keep it. Chapter III did that for one keeper at one board: point to the moment of entry, and the rule is shown. House 3's real board is busier. Several clerks enter claims as their slips come in, and several loaders serve at once, and Herlios, who keeps the board, arranged it so that none of them ever stands aside for another. Then there is no single moment to point to.

The board has numbered slots, each holding one claim tablet or nothing, and a single peg standing at the first slot nobody has been given yet.

Herlios' board To enter a claim: note the peg's number and move the peg on one, in a single movement. Put the claim tablet in that slot.
To serve: look at the peg. Go along the slots from 1 up to the one before the peg, and take the first tablet you find, emptying its slot in a single movement. If you find none, go back to the peg and start again.
The queue of §4.2: Enq is INC(back) then STORE; Deq is READ(back) then SWAP along the array. Slots are never reused.

Nobody holds anything another needs: no key, no queue at the board. A clerk who stops halfway to his slot stops no one else. All of this happens at one house, before one board; no slip crosses the road.

The race. Glaukos is entering House 1's claim and Mikon House 2's, at the same time. The peg stands at 1. Glaukos takes slot 1, Mikon slot 2, and the peg moves to 3. Mikon puts House 2's tablet in slot 2 and is done. Glaukos has not yet reached slot 1.

Is House 1's claim on the board yet? Either answer fails. If it counts as first, then Sosias, a loader who comes now, finds slot 1 still empty and serves House 2 from slot 2, which is wrong. If it does not count yet, or counts behind House 2's, then let Glaukos put it in slot 1; now Sosias comes, finds House 1's tablet in slot 1 and serves it, while House 2's was first.

Nothing has gone wrong. The two claims were entered at the same time, so the rule is happy either way. The trouble is only that when Mikon is done, nobody can yet say which it will be. A race still to be run will settle it.

Many possible orders. So Herlios and Wingaia of House 2 stopped asking what the board means and asked what it could mean. Once Glaukos has put in House 1's tablet, the board could mean House 2, then House 1 or House 1, then House 2. A claim put in its slot before another clerk even took his number must come first; any two not so ordered may go either way.

The proof shows one thing, at every moment and not only when the board is quiet: every order the board could mean is one the business so far could honestly have produced. Two facts carry it. A tablet just put in never has to stand ahead of anything already waiting. And a loader going up from slot 1 always takes a tablet that could be first.

The Board Keeps the Rule

At every moment, every order of waiting claims the board could stand for is one the business so far could have produced, so Herlios' board keeps the rule.

Proof — that the board keeps the rule, in outline
  1. An empty board stands only for no claims, which is what no business produces. It is enough to show that every single movement, and every beginning and end of a clerk's or loader's business, keeps it so.
  2. Beginnings and ends put nothing in a slot and take nothing out. A beginning adds business that may or may not have had its moment yet; an end settles one such choice. Neither removes an order the board could stand for.
  3. Moving the peg and looking at it move no tablet, so they change nothing.
  4. Putting a tablet in. For a waiting tablet to have to stand behind this one, its clerk would have taken his number after this one was put in, and this one is only now being put in. So the new tablet may go last in every order, and the business agrees: an entry may have its moment now, at the back.
  5. Emptying a slot. Say Sosias, going up from slot 1, empties slot i and finds tablet x, and some waiting tablet z must come before x because z was put in before x's clerk took his number. Then z's slot is lower, so Sosias has already passed it. He went only as far as the peg showed when he looked, so x's number was taken before he looked, and z was put in before that. So z was in its slot when he passed it, and he would have taken it. Hence x could be first, and a serving may have its moment now, with x.
  6. Every movement keeps the claim, so it holds at every moment.∎

This is an outline, and so is the paper's. Herlihy and Wing give the two facts of steps 4 and 5 and the shape of the argument; the lemmas that make it airtight are proved in their technical report.

Once the board is known to keep the rule, the houses can rely on everything a single keeper would guarantee: claims are served in the order they came by the sun, none vanishes, and none is served that nobody made. The obvious one-line promise, no later claim is served before an earlier one, would not have guaranteed the last two; a board that lost tablets would keep it.

Chapter VIII

What the Rule Does Not Say

Nobody checks it. It is judged by the sun, which nobody on Arche can read in the trading season. A house knows the rule is kept only because its arrangement has been shown to keep it, as the single keeper and Herlios' board were. The rule gives the island no hour and needs none.

It forbids; it does not require. Every clause says what must not happen. A board that never served anyone would break none of them. A loader may even be overtaken on every round, finding each tablet taken just before he reaches it, and go round for ever while the rule is kept throughout. That every claim is served in the end is a separate promise.

It does not care how many copies there are. One board at House 3 keeps it. So could three copies, one at each house, kept in step by slips, if they answered as though they were one. The rule cannot tell the difference, and that is its purpose: a house owed one board is owed that whatever stands behind it. Many copies act as one exactly when no house comparing notes could tell that there are many.

It is not the only reading of before. On Paxos, Lamport tells of a farmer who got the parliament to permit the sale of black goats, and a merchant who asked a legislator whose ledger stopped a decree short and was told the sale was illegal*. The Paxons' remedy was that if one inquiry precedes a second, the second cannot reveal an older state of the law. At first they read precedes as finished before the other began, the sun's reading, and met it by passing a decree for every inquiry. That was too cumbersome, and they weakened it to could have affected it, the tallies' reading. The two kinds of first of Chapter III are the same two readings.

The ideas, plainly

A shared object is correct, Herlihy and Wing propose, if every operation appears to take effect at a single instant somewhere between its call and its return. Then the results are those of some one-at-a-time execution, and that execution respects real time: an operation that finished before another started comes first. This is linearizability. It lets a concurrent queue be specified as an ordinary queue.

Real-time order is not Lamport's causal order. An operation can finish before another starts with no message between them, and operations joined by messages can still overlap. Neither order contains the other, and linearizability is the one an outside observer comparing notes would expect.

Linearizability is local: a system is linearizable exactly when each object is. And it is nonblocking: a pending call of an operation that is always defined can always be completed. Sequential consistency drops real time and loses locality; serializability, over multi-operation transactions, loses both and needs a way to abort.

When no single step of an implementation can be named as an operation's instant, Herlihy and Wing map each state to the set of abstract values it could stand for, and show that set is always among the values the history could have produced. Their example is a lock-free queue built from fetch-and-increment and swap.
