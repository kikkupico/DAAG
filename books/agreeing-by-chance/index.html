Agreeing by Chance

Based on “Another Advantage of Free Choice: Completely Asynchronous Agreement Protocols” by Michael Ben‑Or (1983)

If no plan can guarantee agreement when a machine may stop without warning, can chance succeed where planning cannot? What if a machine that is stuck simply tosses a coin?

Four mercenaries at the foot of the hill must all attack or all hold, and any one of them may fall silent while no bird has a longest flight. No arrangement guarantees that any of them ever commits. Change one thing: let a man who cannot settle toss a coin, alone in his own tent. Then the four never commit to different answers, and they commit in the end with probability one, though on no night anyone can name.

Chapter I

The Same Four Tents

At the foot of Mount Phyle four mercenaries, Number 1 to Number 4, keep four tents out of sight of one another and send word only by raven*. They must all attack or all hold. Each formed his own opinion at dusk, his sighting, and committing is final: attacking or holding, a man leaves his tent and its birds for the night.

The birds always arrive, and what they carry is what was written. But there is no longest flight, and birds sent an hour apart arrive in either order. Any one of the four may be killed. It has not happened, and no one is killed in this book; what matters is that it may. A dead man's tent falls silent, and a silent tent cannot be told from a live one whose bird is slow.

What follows from that is known. Nobody lies, nothing goes wrong, and still there is no arrangement the four could make which guarantees that any of them ever commits; the birds can always be ordered so that the night stays open*. The three demands are these, put in the form this book's paper uses:

The three demands First. No two of them commit to different answers.
Second. If all four sighted the same answer, that is the answer they commit to.
Third. Every man who is not killed eventually commits.

Are these the same demands as before? Two of them ask more than the impossibility needs. For it, it is enough that both answers stay possible and that somebody commit*. Here the second says exactly how the answers stay possible: when the sightings all agree, the four follow them, which is what rules out agreeing to hold in advance. And the third asks committing of every man left alive. Even the weaker demands cannot be guaranteed, so the stronger ones cannot either.

Is there a way out, then? The impossibility rested on one assumption, stated early and easy to pass over: that the four may not draw lots. Each acts only on what has reached him, so that given the same sighting and the same birds in the same order, he does the same thing every time.

What if that one assumption is dropped, and nothing else is changed? What could four men do with chance?

Chapter II

A Coin in Each Tent

Every man at the foot was paid in Attic silver, and every Attic coin has the goddess's head on one face and her owl on the other. Before the tents were pitched it was settled, as part of the arrangement all four knew, that the owl stands for holding and the goddess for attacking. A man who, by the rules below, cannot settle on an answer tosses a coin high off his thumb, catches it, and favours the answer it shows.

Why not toss one coin for all four? They cannot toss one coin together, or draw one lot from one bag: that would need them in one place, and nothing on the hill brings them there. Could one man toss for all and send the result round? No: a bird from him might be the one still in the pines, or he might be the one who is killed. So there are four coins and four separate tosses, and nobody learns another's toss except by bird, if at all.

What is asked of a coin? Three things, and a silver coin tossed high and caught gives them. A spun coin would not: it tends to fall to one face, and a struck coin is never quite even.

What a toss is taken to be (1) Fair. Owl and goddess come up equally often.
(2) Fresh. Each toss owes nothing to any toss before it, in this tent or any other.
(3) Unforeseen. Before it is made, no one knows how it will fall. After it is made, anyone may know it.

Why does the third clause matter? Because of how the arguments of this book treat the birds. Without coins, the birds are taken in the worst order the argument can find: whatever the four arrange, the order of arrivals can be chosen against them*. The same is done here, and done harder. Think of the order in which birds come down as arranged by someone who can see into every tent, read every strip and see every toss already made, and who arranges the arrivals to keep the four apart for as long as possible. What can this arranger not do? See a toss before it is made. He is only the worst case, and no such person exists; but anything that holds against him holds on every night.

A coin in each tent, then. But when does a man toss, and what does he do with what it shows?

Chapter III

Rounds of Birds

The arrangement goes in numbered rounds, and every bird carries the number of its round, so that a man can tell a late bird of the second round from an early one of the third. In each round each man sends two birds to each of the others, and counts his own two as though they had come to him.

The first is his report: the answer he favours at the start of the round, beginning with his sighting. The second says one of two things: sure of an answer, or unsure. When may a man write sure of hold? Only when the reports that reached him in that round show more than half of all four men favouring hold.

Why wait for three birds and not four? With four men and at most one silent, three always come; a man who waited for four would wait for ever whenever one of them had been killed, and could not know it.

One round, r, at one tent (1) Send the report of round r, the answer favoured now, to all.
(2) Wait for three reports of round r. If more than two of them, that is all three, name the same answer, send sure of that answer to all; otherwise send unsure.
(3) Wait for three second birds of round r. Then:
  (a) if at least one says sure of an answer, favour that answer from now on;
  (b) if two or more say sure of it, be settled on it;
  (c) if none says sure, toss the coin and favour the answer it shows.
(4) Go on to round r + 1.
The same, for N men of whom t may fall silent Wait each time for N − t birds. Send sure of v when more than N/2 of the reports received name v. Favour v on one sure of v; settle on v on more than t of them; toss otherwise. The arrangement needs N > 2t. On the hill, N = 4 and t = 1.

A settled man still has to commit, and is there a catch in it? There is. A man who leaves his tent to attack or to hold falls as silent as a dead one, and the others are waiting for three birds each round; if two men committed and a third were killed, the last would wait for ever. So a settled man does one thing more before he commits.

The rule of parting A man who becomes settled on an answer in round r at once sends both his birds of round r + 1 to all: the report, that answer, and the second bird, sure of that answer. Then he leaves his tent and commits to it.

Is this rule in Ben-Or's paper? No. There a process that has decided simply goes on taking part, and nothing is said of stopping. On the hill deciding is committing, and committing is silence, so the rule is needed; as we will see, the two birds it sends early are exactly the two the man would have sent had he stayed.

Each man now tosses whenever he cannot settle, alone and unseen. Could two coins falling differently commit two men to different answers?

Chapter IV

Never Two Answers

Two coins can never commit two men to different answers. The first two demands hold on every night, whatever the birds and whatever the coins. The whole of it rests on one small fact about majorities.

One sure answer a round

In any round, every sure bird names the same answer.

Why? Each man sends one report a round, so the round has at most four reports. A man writes sure of hold only after seeing three reports of hold, and sure of attack only after seeing three of attack. Three and three out of four must share a man, and he cannot have reported both.

Never two answers

No two men commit to different answers. If one man is settled on an answer in round r, every man who finishes round r favours that answer, and every man who is not killed is settled on it in round r + 1.

Proof — that once one man is settled, all end at his answer
  1. Say Number 1 is settled on hold in round r. Then two or more of the three second birds that reached him said sure of hold, so at least two such birds were sent in round r.
  2. Any other man who finishes round r read three of the four second birds of that round, so he missed at most one. He therefore read at least one sure of hold. By one sure answer a round he read no sure of attack, so he favours hold: by rule (a) if he read one, and he may also be settled on hold by rule (b). He does not toss.
  3. So every report of round r + 1 names hold. Every man who reaches step (2) of that round sees three reports of hold and sends sure of hold; every man who reaches step (3) reads three sure of hold and is settled on hold.
  4. The birds a settled man sends early under the rule of parting are a report of hold and sure of hold. By step 3 these are exactly the birds he would have sent in round r + 1 had he stayed, whichever three reports he had read. So the rule changes nothing the others see, and each of them still gets three birds at every step, since at most one man is dead and the settled ones have sent theirs.
  5. No man is settled on attack in round r or any later round, since no sure of attack is ever sent from round r on. So no two men commit to different answers.∎
The sightings respected

If all four sighted the same answer, every man who is not killed is settled on it in the first round.

Proof — that four matching sightings settle the first round
  1. Every report of round 1 names that answer, so every man reads three reports of it and sends sure of it.
  2. Every man then reads three sure birds for it, more than one, and is settled on it. No coin is tossed.∎

Where does chance come in? Nowhere in either result. A coin is tossed only by a man who read no sure bird at all, and one sure answer a round means that while anyone at all is settled, no one is tossing against him. The coins can delay the four. They can never divide them.

The coins cannot divide the four. Can they bring them together, on a night when the sightings are split?

Chapter V

The Toss

The coin does its work in the rounds where nobody is sure. Here is one way a night can go, with tent 4 silent from the start, so that every waiting is for the three who remain. The sightings were hold at tent 1, attack at tent 2 and hold at tent 3.

What is worth seeing in it? First, the tosses did not have to agree with anybody's sighting. Number 3 sighted hold and tossed hold, Number 1 sighted hold and tossed attack; that is allowed, because the second demand speaks only of nights when all four sightings agree. Second, why did round 2 settle nothing, when two of the three favoured attack? Because sure needs more than half of all four men, not more than half of those heard from. That is what keeps one sure answer a round true when a slow bird may be carrying the fourth report.

Is there anything to be read into which answer the coins chose? Nothing. The coins pick an answer the way the birds' order would otherwise have picked it: by nothing anyone can know in advance. The night could as well have ended in attack.

That night ended in three rounds. Must every night end?

Chapter VI

With Probability One

Not on every course of birds and coins. The example could have taken thirty rounds, and there is a course of birds and coins, one course among very many, in which it never ends at all: the tosses keep coming up split, round after round, for ever. Then what can be promised? That such courses, taken together, have no chance whatever of happening.

Committing with probability one

With at most one of the four silent, and however the birds are ordered, the chance that every man who is not killed eventually commits is one.

Why? The coins keep giving the four a way out, and the birds cannot shut it for good. In any round at most one answer can be the subject of sure birds. If every man who tosses in that round comes up with that answer, or, when there is none, all with the same answer, then every man starts the next round favouring one answer, and the next round settles everyone, as the proof of never two answers showed. The arranger can make that harder, round by round, but a way out that keeps coming back with some chance is taken in the end with chance one.

Proof — a sketch, that the four commit with probability one
  1. Fix a round r in which no one is yet settled. Let g be the answer named by sure birds in round r, if any are sent, and let g be hold otherwise.
  2. Every man who finishes round r either reads a sure of g and favours g, or tosses. Each toss is fair and fresh, and the arranger cannot see it before it is made, so each comes up g with chance one half, whatever has gone before.
  3. At most four men toss in round r, so with chance at least 1/16 (the sketch's figure, not a proven bound) every one of them comes up g. Then every report of round r + 1 names g, and as in the proof of never two answers, every man who is not killed is settled on g in round r + 1.
  4. The rounds' tosses are fresh, so the chance that this fails in each of k rounds in a row is at most (15/16)k, which shrinks towards nothing as k grows. The chance that it fails for ever is therefore nothing.∎

Is the sketch airtight? No, and it should be said plainly where it fails. Step 1 treats the answer g as fixed before the round's tosses. But a slow man's report for round r comes from how his round r − 1 ended, and the arranger chooses which second birds he reads there, and may choose after seeing some tosses of round r. So the answer named sure in round r can be picked against the early tosses, and the one-in-sixteen of step 3 is not a bound anyone has proved. Ben-Or's paper states its theorems without any proof. A proof that closes every such gap was published only in 2012, by Aguilera and Toueg; it comes to the same conclusion by a longer road.

What is not claimed? No night can be named by which the four will have committed. There is no round after which committing is certain; there is only a chance, growing towards one, that it has happened by then. Ben-Or's paper gives no figure for how fast that chance grows, and this book gives none. A man who asks will we have committed by the fortieth round? can only be told almost certainly. And the courses that never end still exist. They have been given chance nothing, not abolished.

If endless courses still exist, what exactly did the coin change about the impossibility?

Chapter VII

What the Coin Changed

Recall how the endless night is built without coins, in three steps*: there is a way for the sightings to fall that leaves the night open; from any open standing, any arrival can be deferred and taken without closing the night; and so an arranger who defers well can keep it open for ever. Every step leans on the four being unable to draw lots. Given the same birds in the same order, each man does the same thing, so the arranger knows, before choosing an arrival, what it will lead to.

And with coins? The arranger no longer knows. At a toss the night forks, one way for the owl and one for the goddess, and the fork is taken by the coin, not by him. He can still be as clever as before about the birds. What he cannot do is steer both forks at once. The open standings are still there, and so are the courses that stay open for ever: fix every toss in advance and the argument without coins runs unchanged*. What has changed is that an endless course now needs the coins to cooperate, round after round, and the coins do not.

Does this contradict the result without coins? No, it stands. No arrangement guarantees that anybody ever commits. The arrangement here does not guarantee it either. It guarantees that the four never commit to different answers, and makes committing certain in the sense that failing to commit has no chance at all. The difference between on every course and with probability one is the whole of what the coin bought.

Three things would change matters at the foot of the hill: a longest flight, or some report of who has died, or all the deaths happening before the evening begins*. The coin is a fourth way, and a different kind. The other three give the four men more knowledge of the world. The coin gives them none. It gives the world less power over them.

The coin takes power from the world. What does it leave the world holding?

Chapter VIII

What Chance Does Not Buy

Could the coins carry the four past two deaths? No. The arrangement needs the dead to be fewer than half, and that is not a weakness of the arrangement. With two of the four allowed to fall silent, no arrangement at all, with coins or without, can keep the first demand while keeping the other two.

Half is too many

If half the men or more may fall silent, no arrangement, with or without coins, can meet all three demands: that no two commit to different answers, that matching sightings are followed, and that every man not killed eventually commits.

Proof — that with two of four silent, the answers can divide
  1. Suppose Numbers 1 and 2 sighted hold and Numbers 3 and 4 sighted attack, and take any arrangement that follows matching sightings and makes every man not killed commit eventually.
  2. Two men may be silent, so Numbers 1 and 2 must be able to commit without ever hearing from Number 3 or Number 4: for all they can tell, those two are dead. Keep every bird from Numbers 3 and 4 in the pines; Numbers 1 and 2, hearing only each other, commit. Whatever their coins show, what they see is exactly what they would see on a night when all four sighted hold and the other two were killed at dusk, and on that night the second demand has them hold. So they hold.
  3. In the same way keep back every bird from Numbers 1 and 2, and Numbers 3 and 4 attack.
  4. Do both at once, releasing the held birds only after all four have committed: all four are alive, every bird is merely late, and neither pair can tell. The pairs commit to different answers.∎

Ben-Or notes this in a single line: with half or more silent, the order of birds can imitate a hill split in two. Four tents bear one silent man, and no more.

What about liars? Nobody lies at the foot of the hill, and this book has not needed to ask what a lying man could do. Ben-Or's paper gives a second arrangement, of the same shape, for men who may lie as well as die: each man must be able to tell whose bird he holds, and the liars must be fewer than one in five. Against one liar that is six men. The four bandits on the crown could not use it, and do not need to; they have their own methods*. Ben-Or did not know whether one in five was the best that could be done.

Is it quick? No. With many men and many possible deaths, the tosses that must all come up alike are many too, and Ben-Or warns that the expected number of rounds may grow very fast. Ben-Or showed one happier case: if the silent are few compared with the whole, no more than about the square root of the number of men, and the birds happen to come in step, the rounds needed on average do not grow at all. No deterministic arrangement can do that when birds come in step, since those need at least one more round than there are men who may fail.

Does it choose a good answer? The coins say nothing about which answer is wiser. When the sightings agree, the four do what they sighted; when they do not, the answer is whichever the coins and the birds happen to reach. As always on the hill, nothing follows from it: no assault is told, and no one learns whether it was the right answer.

The ideas, plainly

Fischer, Lynch and Paterson proved that no deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system if even one process may crash: an adversary ordering the messages can always keep the outcome undecided. Their proof depends on determinism, since the adversary must know in advance what each step will lead to.

Ben-Or showed that randomness removes the obstacle. Processes run in rounds. In each round every process broadcasts its current value, waits for N − t values, and broadcasts a proposal marked D if a strict majority of all N processes hold the same value. It then waits for N − t proposals: it decides on more than t D-proposals for a value, adopts the value on at least one, and otherwise flips a private fair coin.

Safety holds on every run. Two strict majorities intersect, so only one value can be proposed with D in a round; a decision means more than t D-proposals exist, so every process that finishes the round sees one and adopts the value, and all decide it in the next round. Termination holds with probability 1 when N > 2t: once every correct process holds the same value, the next round decides, and the coin flips keep giving that a chance the adversary cannot remove. Ben-Or states this without proof; a complete proof, which must handle an adversary that sees earlier flips, is Aguilera and Toueg's (2012). Runs that never terminate still exist, but together they have probability zero.

The protocol does not contradict FLP; it changes a hypothesis. It needs a correct majority, which is necessary, and it may take many rounds. Later work replaced the private coins with a shared coin to make the expected number of rounds small.
