Many Copies, Acting as One

Based on “Linearizability: A Correctness Condition for Concurrent Objects” by Maurice Herlihy and Jeannette Wing (1990)

When data is kept in several copies, what does it take for them to act as one? And who could ever tell the difference?

House 3 fills the other houses' orders from its storehouse, and the order placed first is filled first. The tallies put the orders in a sequence every house can work out from its slips. But an order that was entered and confirmed in the morning can still be filled after one made at noon. This book asks for placed first judged by the sun: every order filled as though one keeper had entered them all on one board, in the sequence they really came.

Chapter I

The Goatherd's Morning

The three trading houses of Arche, House 1, House 2 and House 3, send word to one another only by messenger on the ring road, and in the trading season nobody on the island can read the hour*. House 3 has grain, both other houses need it, and the houses have agreed that the order placed first is filled first.

How is House 3 to know which was placed first, with no hour to go by? By tallies: a number written on every slip and raised on every arrival, with the houses' numbers to break ties. The tallies never put an order ahead of one that led to it, and no house can win by keeping a quicker messenger*.

Now take one morning. House 1 sends House 3 an order. House 3 enters it and sends back word that it is entered; House 1 has its confirmation before the sun is high. A goatherd crossing Mount Phyle by the smugglers' tracks mentions it at House 2, which has heard nothing by slip all morning*. At noon House 2 sends an order of its own. Its tally is low, since no slip has reached it for hours, lower than House 1's. So House 3 serves House 2 first.

Did anything go wrong? By the tallies, no: no slip joined the two orders, so either could come first. But House 2 knows, and so does anyone who talked to the goatherd, that House 1's order had been entered and confirmed before House 2 even asked. What the sun saw, House 1 was first.

The tallies have always had this gap. They respect every slip, and the goatherd carries none. One answer is to make better clocks: clocks that run true, kept close together by the slips, so that nobody crossing the mountain can arrive before he left*. That is a way of catching the goatherd. The houses want something else: a promise about how orders are filled that no goatherd can ever catch them breaking, whatever clocks they keep or do without.

Could the houses promise instead to fill first the order placed first as the sun saw it? Nobody on Arche can read the sun. What would such a promise even say?

Chapter II

The Rule of the Board

A promise about orders has to be said in terms of what the houses themselves see. What does a house see of its order? Two moments: when it sends the order, and when the confirmation comes back. Somewhere in between, the order was put on the board where House 3 keeps its waiting orders. And serving? A loader at House 3 goes to the board, takes an order and loads the grain for it; that too begins and ends. Each house, and each loader, does one thing at a time.

So every piece of business has a span, from its beginning to its end. When does one come before another? When the first was over before the second began: House 1's order came before House 2's. Otherwise the two overlap, and neither came first. This is judged by the sun, whether or not anyone could see it.

Now the promise. Picture a single keeper with a single board, doing one thing at a time: he enters an order, or he serves the oldest waiting one. No order goes onto his board and none comes off it except through his own hands, one at a time. The houses cannot see him and do not need to. They need only this: that everything they got could have come from him, with each thing he did falling somewhere inside the span of the business it belonged to.

The rule of the board All the orders and all the servings can be set in a single sequence such that:
(1) every serving takes the order that a single keeper, with a single board, taking everything in that sequence, would have served;
(2) each piece of business takes its place in the sequence at some moment between its beginning and its end; so that
(3) anything finished before another began comes before it.
Linearizability, §2.2: a history is linearizable if, after adding responses to some pending invocations and dropping the rest, it is equivalent to a legal sequential history that respects the real-time order <H. The board is a FIFO queue: an order is Enq, a serving is Deq.

Why does clause (2) matter? Without it the keeper could do things whenever it suited him, and the promise would say nothing about the sun. With it, clause (3) follows: if House 1's order was over before House 2's began, the keeper's moment for House 1 came first, and a single keeper serves the older order first. So the goatherd's morning breaks the rule.

Is the rule too strict, forbidding mornings that are plainly fine? Or too loose, allowing ones that are plainly wrong?

Chapter III

Four Mornings

Try it on four mornings.

Two orders sent at the same time. Neither is confirmed before the other is sent, and a loader serves House 2 first. Is that allowed? Yes: the orders overlap, and the keeper may have entered House 2's first.

One order after the other. House 1's order is confirmed before House 2's is sent, and House 2 is served first. That is the goatherd's morning, and it is forbidden.

An order served before its confirmation arrives. House 1's order is entered, and a loader serves it while word of the entry is still on the road. Allowed: the order's moment came at its entry, and the house simply has not heard yet.

One order served twice. Two loaders each take House 2's single order and load grain for it. No single keeper serves one order twice, so this is forbidden, however the times fall.

What about business still unfinished when we look? An order sent but not yet confirmed may be counted as on the board or not, whichever fits what happens next.

The rule and the tallies both claim to say which order came first. Are they the same answer, one stricter than the other?

Chapter IV

Two Kinds of First

The rule and the tallies do not give the same answer. For the tallies, one order is first if a chain of slips leads from it to the other; orders no chain joins are put in sequence by tally and house number, fairly but arbitrarily*. For the sun, one order is first if it was over before the other began, whether or not any slip joined them.

Does either include the other? No. On the goatherd's morning no slip joined the two orders, so the tallies could put House 2 first; the sun put House 1 first. Turn it round: House 1 sends an order, and before it is confirmed sends House 2 a slip about it, and House 2 then orders. A chain of slips joins them, so the tallies put House 1 first. But House 1's order was not over when House 2's began, so for the sun they overlap, and the rule would let House 2 go first.

Can the rule be kept at all? Easily, by the arrangement the tallies were invented to avoid. Let House 3 keep the only board and enter each order when its slip reaches the gate. The entry falls after the slip was written and before the confirmation leaves, so it lies inside the order's span. Serve in order of entry, and the keeper of the rule is House 3 itself.

Two Kinds of First

Serving orders in the sequence they reach House 3's gate keeps the rule of the board. Serving them in the sequence of their tallies need not. And the tallies' sequence can put one order before another where the rule of the board would allow either.

Proof — that serving by entry keeps the rule
  1. Put all orders and servings in sequence by the moment each is done at the board: an order when the keeper enters it, a serving when a loader takes an order. This is one sequence.
  2. Each serving takes the earliest order entered and not yet served, which is what a single keeper with a single board would serve, so clause (1) holds.
  3. An order is entered after its slip was written and before word of its entry leaves, so its moment lies within it. A serving's moment lies within the loader's business. That is clause (2), and clause (3) follows: whatever was finished before another began had its moment first.∎

Then what was wrong with serving by arrival? Only that the house with the shorter road wins every pair of orders that overlap. The rule does not mind, because it says nothing about orders that overlap. The tallies do mind, and pay for it by sometimes serving a later order first. The two answers protect different things.

One board at House 3's gate keeps the rule. But must there be only one? Each house might rather keep a copy of the board close at hand.

Chapter V

Copies Nobody Can Count

Look again at what the rule mentions: sendings, confirmations and servings, all things that happen at the houses' gates. It never mentions the board itself. So it cannot tell one board from three copies kept in step by slips, or from any other arrangement behind the gates. Whatever stands behind them is judged by what comes out of them.

That is what it means for many copies to act as one: no house, comparing notes with every other and with anyone who watched the sun, could tell that there is more than one board. How copies might be kept so is another matter, and this book does not take it up. What it takes up is what the promise gives to a house that relies on it.

Grain is not the only thing on order. If the houses keep several boards, must the rule be checked across all of them at once?

Chapter VI

Grain and Oil

House 2 presses oil, and all three houses order it the same way, on a board of its own at House 2. If the rule had to hold over both boards together, then every change to how one board is kept would have to be weighed against the other. Does it?

No. It is enough that each board keeps the rule by itself.

One Board at a Time

All the business keeps the rule if and only if, for every board, the business on that board, taken by itself, keeps it.

Why should two boards fit together without anyone arranging it? Because the sun ties them, and the sun never contradicts itself. Two boards could fail to fit only through a loop: one order before another on the grain board, that one before a third by the sun, and so round to the start. The proof shows that such a loop would need the sun to put something before itself.

Proof — that if every board keeps the rule, all the business does
  1. For each board, choose a sequence and moments that show it keeps the rule. Say one piece of business must go before another if they are on the same board and its sequence puts the first earlier, or if the sun does: the first finished before the second began. Follow chains of these.
  2. If no chain leads from a piece of business back to itself, set everything in any single sequence that keeps every must go before. It keeps each board's own sequence, so every serving is what a single keeper would do; and it keeps the sun's sequence, which is clause (3), and clause (2) with it. So it remains to show there is no loop.
  3. Suppose there is one, e₁, …, en, back to e₁, as short as any loop can be.
  4. All on one board. A board's sequence puts any two of its pieces one way or the other, so some step of the loop runs against it, and that step must be the sun's: b finished before a began, though the board put a first. Then that board did not keep clause (3). So the loop touches at least two boards.
  5. Number it so that e₁ is on board x and e₂ on another. The step between them crosses boards, so it is the sun's: e₁ finished before e₂ began.
  6. Nothing else in the loop is on board x. Suppose something is, and let ei be the first after e₂. The step into it crosses boards, so ei−1 finished before ei began. And e₂ began before ei−1 finished, or else e₂, …, ei−1 would be a shorter loop. So e₁ finished before e₂ began, before ei−1 finished, before ei began: e₁ came before ei by the sun, and e₁, ei, …, en is a shorter loop.
  7. So the last step, into e₁, also crosses boards: en finished before e₁ began, and therefore before e₂ began, making e₂, …, en a shorter loop. Every case contradicts the choice of a shortest loop.∎

Is this true of any sensible rule? Take the sun out and see. Ask only that everything could have come from a single keeper, with each house's own business in the order it did it, and nothing said about moments. Under that looser rule the goatherd's morning is fine. But now let House 1 order grain and then oil, and House 2 order oil and then grain; the grain loader serves House 2 first and the oil loader serves House 1 first.

Each board passes the looser rule alone, and together they fail it. Houses that kept only the looser rule would need one plan spanning every board. The sun's clause is what lets each board be kept, and judged, on its own.

Each board can be judged alone. Could keeping the rule ever force a house to stand at the gate and wait on someone else's business?

Chapter VII

Nobody Made to Wait

Not for an order. Whatever is on the board, a single keeper can always put a new order at the end, so the rule never makes an order wait on anyone else's business.

No Order Waits on Another

If the business so far keeps the rule, a waiting order can be entered now and the rule still kept.

Proof — that a waiting order can always be entered
  1. Take a single sequence that shows the business so far keeps the rule.
  2. If it already counts the order as entered, confirm it now; the same sequence still works.
  3. If not, put the order at the end of the sequence and confirm it now. Nothing earlier changes, so clause (1) holds. Its moment is now, within its span, which is clause (2). Nothing began after it finished, so putting it last breaks no before, which is clause (3).∎

So when a house does wait for its confirmation, the cause is the arrangement behind the board, a slip still on the road, and never the rule.

Serving is different. A loader who comes to an empty board has nothing to take, and a single keeper would make him wait until an order comes. That wait is asked for, not forced: serve the oldest order has no answer while there are no orders.

Would a stronger promise keep this? Not always. Suppose a house wanted several things done as one, with nothing else between them: look at the oil board, and if it is empty, order grain. If House 1 does that while House 2 looks at the grain board and orders oil, and both find their board empty, then each must have gone before the other's order, and one of them has to be called off. Promises about such groups can force a house to undo its business; the rule of the board, whose business is always single, never does.

So far the board has had one keeper, doing one thing at a time. House 3's real board is busier. With many clerks and loaders at work at once, is there still a moment to point to?

Chapter VIII

The Busy Board

Herlios keeps House 3's board, and he has arranged it so that nobody ever stands aside for anybody. It has numbered slots, each holding one order tablet or nothing, and a peg at the first slot nobody has been given yet. Clerks enter orders as slips come in; loaders serve.

Herlios' board To enter an order: note the peg's number and move the peg on one, in a single movement. Put the order tablet in that slot.
To serve: look at the peg. Go along the slots from 1 up to the one before the peg, and take the first tablet you find, emptying its slot in a single movement. If you find none, go back to the peg and start again.
The queue of §4.2: Enq is INC(back) then STORE; Deq is READ(back) then SWAP along the array. Slots are never reused.

No key, no queue, and a clerk who stops halfway stops no one else. Everything happens at one board at one house; no slip crosses the road. Is there still a moment for each order?

Watch a race. Glaukos is entering House 1's order and Mikon House 2's, at the same time. The peg stands at 1. Glaukos takes slot 1, Mikon slot 2, and the peg moves to 3. Mikon puts House 2's tablet in slot 2 and is done. Glaukos has not yet reached slot 1.

Is House 1's order on the board yet? Say yes, ahead of House 2's: then Sosias, a loader who comes now, finds slot 1 empty and serves House 2 from slot 2. Say no: then let Glaukos put his tablet in, and Sosias, coming after, finds it in slot 1 and serves House 1 first.

Has anything gone wrong? No: the two orders overlapped, so either sequence keeps the rule. The trouble is only that when Mikon is done, which one it will be is not yet settled. A race still to be run will settle it, so no step anyone has taken so far can be called the moment House 1's order joined the board.

If there is no moment to point to, how can anyone show that Herlios' board keeps the rule?

Chapter IX

What the Board Could Mean

By asking not what the board means but what it could mean. Once Glaukos's tablet is in, the board could stand for House 2, then House 1 or for House 1, then House 2. A tablet put in before another clerk even took his number must come first; any two not so placed may go either way.

Then the thing to show is this: at every moment, every sequence the board could stand for is one the business so far could honestly have produced. Two facts carry it. A tablet just put in never has to stand ahead of anything already waiting. And a loader going up from slot 1 always takes a tablet that could be first.

The Board Keeps the Rule

At every moment, every sequence of waiting orders the board could stand for is one the business so far could have produced, so Herlios' board keeps the rule.

Proof — that the board keeps the rule, in outline
  1. An empty board stands only for no orders, which is what no business produces. It is enough to show that every single movement, and every beginning and end of a clerk's or loader's business, keeps it so.
  2. Beginnings and ends put nothing in a slot and take nothing out. A beginning adds business that may or may not have had its moment yet; an end settles one such choice. Neither removes a sequence the board could stand for.
  3. Moving the peg and looking at it move no tablet, so they change nothing.
  4. Putting a tablet in. For a waiting tablet to have to stand behind this one, its clerk would have taken his number after this one was put in, and this one is only now being put in. So the new tablet may go last in every sequence, and the business agrees: an entry may have its moment now, at the back.
  5. Emptying a slot. Say Sosias, going up from slot 1, empties slot i and finds tablet x, and some waiting tablet z must come before x because z was put in before x's clerk took his number. Then z's slot is lower, so Sosias has already passed it. He went only as far as the peg showed when he looked, so x's number was taken before he looked, and z was put in before that. So z was in its slot when he passed it, and he would have taken it. Hence x could be first, and a serving may have its moment now, with x.
  6. Every movement keeps the order, so it holds at every moment.∎

This is an outline, and so is the paper's. Herlihy and Wing give the two facts of steps 4 and 5 and the shape of the argument; the lemmas that make it airtight are in their technical report.

What does a house get from a board known to keep the rule? Everything a single keeper would guarantee: orders are served in the sequence they came by the sun, none vanishes, and none is served that nobody made. Would the obvious one-line promise, no later order is served before an earlier one, have done as well? No: a board that lost tablets would keep that promise.

The rule, then, can be kept even by a board no one can pause. What has it left unpromised?

Chapter X

What the Rule Does Not Say

Can anyone check it? No one. It is judged by the sun, which nobody on Arche can read in the trading season. A house knows the rule is kept only because the arrangement behind the board has been shown to keep it, as House 3's gate and Herlios' board were. The rule gives the island no hour and needs none.

Does it promise that orders are served? No. Every clause says what must not happen; a board that never served anyone would break none of them. A loader may even be overtaken on every round, finding each tablet taken just before he reaches it, and go round for ever while the rule holds. That every order is served in the end is a separate promise.

Does it say how copies are kept? No. It says what copies must look like from the gates, and nothing about the slips that would keep them in step.

Does it make the tallies wrong? No. The tallies keep a different promise, fairness between orders that overlap, and give up the sun to keep it. Which first the houses want is theirs to choose.

The ideas, plainly

A shared object is correct, Herlihy and Wing propose, if every operation appears to take effect at a single instant somewhere between its call and its return. Then the results are those of some one-at-a-time execution, and that execution respects real time: an operation that finished before another started comes first. This is linearizability. It lets a concurrent queue be specified as an ordinary queue, and it says nothing about how the object is built, so one copy or many are judged alike.

Real-time order is not Lamport's causal order. An operation can finish before another starts with no message between them, and operations joined by messages can still overlap. Neither order contains the other, and linearizability is the one an outside observer comparing notes would expect.

Linearizability is local: a system is linearizable exactly when each object is. And it is nonblocking: a pending call of an operation that is always defined can always be completed. Sequential consistency drops real time and loses locality; serializability, over multi-operation transactions, loses both and needs a way to abort.

When no single step of an implementation can be named as an operation's instant, Herlihy and Wing map each state to the set of abstract values it could stand for, and show that set is always among the values the history could have produced. Their example is a lock-free queue built from fetch-and-increment and swap.
