The Ring of Five Seats

An account of a council on a walled hilltop, where every man could hear every other man speak, and some of what was said was not true, and no one ever found out which.

Chapter I

The Crown and the Ring

The crown of the hill is a flat place a little over sixty paces across, and an old wall goes round the whole of it. The wall is polygonal masonry of the rough sort, laid without mortar, waist-thick and rather more than twice a man's height, and it is older than anyone now on the hill by a great many lifetimes. It has failed in places. Where it has, the men who hold it have put in piled stone and cut timber, and one stretch on the west is timber almost to the top.

Two gates pierce it, on opposite sides. The north gate is a slot under one flat stone lintel, so narrow that men go through it in single file; it has never been repaired because it has never needed to be. The south gate is half again as wide and its stone surround gave way at some point, so that it is now a timber frame set inside a stone hole — two squared posts, a head beam, raking braces angled back onto the rock, a timber sill across the threshold. The new wood is pale against the old grey blocks and you can tell the two gates apart from anywhere on the crown, or for that matter from the plain.

Inside the wall there is a ring of five seats cut from limestone blocks, set well apart in a circle some eleven paces across, all facing inward, open to the sky. There is no roof over them and never was. One of the five stands on a single shallow step, so that it sits about half a knee higher than the other four. At the exact centre of the ring, clear of every seat, there is a short round plinth, and on the plinth a water-clock. Behind the ring, dug down into the rock so that it stands only chest-high above it, is a stone storeroom with a timber door; there are pithoi in it, and stacked bronze, and bolts of cloth. It will not be mentioned again. It is where the things are kept.

Below the wall, on the outer side, a band of rough terraces has been cut into the slope, and the gangs live there in lean-tos of branches and stitched hide propped against dry-stone retaining walls. Each shelter flies a strip of cloth in its gang's colour — ochre, black, blue, green — and each man of that gang wears the same colour somewhere, at the arm or at the brow.

Five men might sit in the ring. Four do. Lamportos, who is called the chief and who sits on the raised seat, is a heavy man in his fifties with a full dark beard going white and a staff of carved olive-wood; he does not raise his voice and has not needed to. Shostakos wears a wolf pelt over one shoulder and has a scar through an eyebrow. Peaseus is long-limbed and quiet and wears a wide bronze ring on each forearm. Dolevios has his beard in two short braids and wears his seal ring on a thong at his neck rather than on a finger. The fifth seat is empty. It has been cut and dressed like the others and nobody sits in it, and whether that is because the ring was made for a council larger than the one that now uses it, or because it is convenient to be reminded that a ring can be the wrong size, is not recorded.

The gangs are not in the council. This wants saying plainly, because a reader may reasonably assume that a few hundred armed men have some say in the matter and they have none. A gang follows its own leader and nobody else's; its men do not confer with another gang's men, do not compare what they were told, and do not read. If you want to know what the council decided, you do not ask anyone in it. You stand above the terraces and watch which way the gangs go.

Chapter II

Two Conditions

What the council is for is orders of this shape: the chief has concluded something about the soldiers below, and the gangs must act on it together — all to the north gate, or all to the south. It does not matter for our purposes which is the better answer. What matters is that a gang at each gate is worse than every gang at the wrong gate, because the wall is long and the men are few, and half the men at each of two places is no defence of either.

So two conditions are wanted of whatever the council does, and they are not the same condition twice.

The two conditions of the ring

The first. Every loyal leader takes his gang to the same gate as every other loyal leader.

The second. If the chief is loyal, every loyal leader takes his gang to the gate the chief named.

Read them again, because the relation between them is the hinge of the whole book. If the chief is loyal then the second condition delivers the first for nothing: everyone loyal does what he said, so everyone loyal does the same thing. One might conclude the first condition is redundant. It is not, and the reason is short: the chief is not guaranteed loyal. He is a member of the council like the others, and nothing in this account exempts him. If he is a traitor the second condition says nothing at all — it only ever spoke about a loyal chief — and the first must be met anyway. The loyal leaders must come to the same gate even when the man who told them where to go was lying to them severally.

And that is the whole difficulty in one sentence. It is not hard to obey a man. It is hard to agree with your peers about what a man said, when he may have said different things to each of you and none of you can prove what you heard.

A word about what "some leaders lie" means here, since it is easy to underrate. A leader who is not loyal is not merely unhelpful. He may say anything to anybody. He may tell one man the chief said north and another man the chief said south, and do it with a straight face, and do it consistently for as long as it serves him. He may say nothing at all. He may work in concert with another such leader, the two of them arranging their lies in advance so that the accounts they give agree with each other and disagree with the truth. Nothing constrains him but the physical facts of the crown.

Why any of them would do this is not explained anywhere in this book, and the absence is deliberate. It is not a mystery being saved for later; there is nothing to come. The council is being described as a mechanism, and a mechanism that works only when you can see into men's reasons is not a mechanism. What the storeroom holds and who wants it are not the subject.

Chapter III

The Klepsydra

The crown is small and there is no weather on it worth speaking of, and the council has therefore one advantage the camps below do not have: when a man speaks, everyone hears him, at once, and knows which man it was. Nothing is carried, nothing is relayed by a third party, nothing is delayed. There are no birds up here. A raven on that crown would be a curiosity.

What the council needs, and has, is a way of dividing the talking into turns, so that "he did not say anything" is a fact and not an impatience. On the plinth at the centre of the ring stands a water-clock of the sort used to time speeches in the courts at Athens: a terracotta jar with a small bronze spout in its side, dripping into a second jar set below, and the lower jar's wall marked with a ladder of incised lines. One line to the next is a filling. Within one filling, every man who is going to speak speaks, and when the water reaches the line the filling is over.

It is worth being clear that there is no timekeeper. No herald calls the fillings, no servant refills the jar between them, no arbiter rules on whether a man spoke in time. The council is five seats and no more, and if there were an honest official standing at the plinth then the council's problem would be solved by employing him, and there would be no book. The clock is a piece of pottery. It is exactly as reliable as a piece of pottery, which is to say: entirely, and about nothing except water.

From this one small arrangement comes the thing the camps below would give a great deal for. Silence is detectable. If a man says nothing in a filling, then when the water reaches the line, every other man in the ring knows that he said nothing — not "has not said anything yet", but said nothing, finally, in that filling. Nobody on the crown ever waits on a man who is simply slow, because there is no such thing as slow up here. There is only speaking and not speaking.

Detectable silence is useless, though, unless silence means something, because a man who wishes to prevent a decision has only to keep his mouth shut. So the council long ago settled the obvious remedy: hold. Saying nothing in a filling is taken to be saying hold — stay where you are, defend where you stand. It is not a good answer and it is not meant to be a good answer. It is a definite one, which is all that is required of it, and it means that the worst a silent man can do is to have said something dull.

The contrast with the camps below is worth holding onto, since it is the only real difference between the two halves of this siege. Down there, absence of a message means nothing whatever, and that is the whole of their trouble. Up here, absence of a message is itself a message. Everything the council can do that the camps cannot, it can do because of a jar of water.
Chapter IV

Three in the Ring

Begin with the ring nearly empty: the chief and two leaders, three men in a circle cut for five, and suppose that one of the three is a traitor and nobody knows which. This is the smallest council worth discussing, and it cannot be made to work at all.

Take a loyal leader — call him the first, and understand that the numbering is the order of seats and nothing else — and put him in two situations.

In the first situation the chief is loyal. He says north, in the open, in the first filling, and both leaders hear him say it. Then in the second filling the other leader turns to the first and reports: he told me south. This is a lie, but the first leader does not know that. What he knows is that he heard the chief say north with his own ears, and the second condition binds him: if the chief is loyal he must go where the chief said. He must go north. He has no choice about this; it is the condition itself, and any arrangement that let him do otherwise would fail it.

In the second situation the chief is a traitor. He says north to the first leader and south to the second — he can, because the two things he says are two separate utterances to two separate men, and nothing prevents him. Then in the second filling the second leader, who is loyal and honest and doing exactly what he ought, turns to the first and reports: he told me south.

Now set the two situations side by side, from where the first leader sits. In the first he heard north from the chief and he told me south from his peer. In the second he heard north from the chief and he told me south from his peer. They are the same. Not similar: the same. Every sound that reached him, every word, every man's face, identical. And we have already established that in the first he must go north. So in the second he goes north too, because he cannot tell them apart.

But run the mirror of the argument on the second leader in that same second situation. He heard south from the chief and would hear he told me north from the first; by the identical reasoning he must go south. So in a single situation the two loyal leaders go to two different gates, and the first condition is broken — and it was broken without anyone doing anything wrong except one man, whom neither of them can identify.

The reader should be told that the argument just given, which is persuasive and which is the one everybody remembers, is not a proof. It has the shape of a proof and it reaches the right conclusion, and reasoning of exactly this shape has produced confident demonstrations of things that are false. The honest position is this: the result is correct, a rigorous demonstration of it exists and is not short, and it was published by Pease, Shostak and Lamport two years before the account this book follows. The informal version is kept here because it is the version that shows you where the trouble is, which is the only thing a book like this can usefully do. It is not kept because it settles anything.

Proof — that three in the ring cannot be made to work
  1. Suppose some arrangement did work for three men with one traitor among them. “Works” means: all loyal men go the same way, and if the chief is loyal they go the way he said.
  2. Situation A. The chief is loyal and says north to both leaders. The second leader is the traitor and reports to the first: he told me south.
  3. Situation B. The chief is the traitor, saying north to the first leader and south to the second. The second leader is loyal and reports honestly to the first: he told me south.
  4. In both, the first leader receives exactly two things: the word north from the chief, and the report he told me south from his peer. His own conduct can depend on nothing else, since nothing else reaches him. So he does the same thing in A as in B.
  5. In A the chief is loyal, so the arrangement obliges the first leader to go north. Hence he goes north in B also.
  6. Consider now the second leader in situation B. He is loyal. Construct Situation C, the mirror of A: the chief loyal, saying south to both, and the first leader the traitor, reporting he told me north. By the same argument the second leader goes south in C, and B is indistinguishable from C for him, so he goes south in B.
  7. But in B the chief is the traitor and both leaders are loyal, so the arrangement requires them to go the same way — and one goes north while the other goes south. Contradiction.∎

The same argument, applied to groups of men rather than single men, shows that no arrangement works whenever the traitors number a third of the ring or more: divide the ring into three parts and let one part play the single traitor above.

Chapter V

The Repeating

Add a seat. With the chief and three leaders — four men in the ring, one traitor among them, nobody knowing who — there is something that works, and it is called the Repeating.

The method is a short one, and it is defined for any number of traitors you wish to tolerate. Tolerate none, and it is trivial: the chief says a gate, and each leader takes the gate he heard, or hold if the chief said nothing. Tolerate one more than that, and one further filling is added, in which each leader repeats what he heard to the others; then each leader looks at the answers he has collected and takes the greater count of them — the gate that the most of them name, with hold if there is no such gate. Tolerate two, and each of those repetitions is itself carried out by the Repeating for one, and so on: one further filling for each traitor to be borne.

It is worth running all the way through, with four in the ring and one filling of repetition, in both of the cases that can arise. In what follows the leaders are called first, second and third by their seats, and that is all the numbering means: any of the three could be in any of the positions, and nothing below identifies anybody.

Case one: the chief is loyal and one of the leaders is not. In the first filling the chief says north to the ring, and all three hear it, because they are all sitting in it. In the second filling each leader repeats what he heard. The first, loyal, says north. The second, loyal, says north. The third says whatever he likes — south, say, or nothing, which is hold.

Now what does the first leader hold? His own hearing, north; the second's report, north; the third's report, south. Three answers: north, north, south. The greater count is north. The second leader, by the same arithmetic on the same three answers, also gets north. So both loyal leaders go north, which is where the chief sent them: both conditions met. And notice that the liar's lie did not need to be detected. Nobody worked out that the third leader was lying. It was simply outvoted.

Case two: the chief is the traitor. Then all three leaders are loyal, and the chief may say three different things: north to the first, south to the second, hold to the third. In the second filling each of the three honestly repeats what he was given. So the first leader ends holding north, south and hold. So does the second. So does the third. They hold the same three answers, because each of them contributed his own honestly and heard the other two honestly.

Three answers, all different: north, south, hold. There is no greater count, so the rule sends them all to hold. All three go to the same place. The first condition is met. The second condition says nothing, since the chief was not loyal — and this is the point at which one sees why the two conditions had to be separated. The council has not discovered the truth; there was no truth to discover. It has done the only thing that was ever available, which is to be wrong together rather than divided.

Two remarks on the cost, since the method is cheap here and is not cheap in general. Bearing one traitor took one extra filling and three reports. Bearing two takes a further filling in which each of those reports is itself repeated round the ring, and bearing three takes another; the talking multiplies as fast as the council grows, and a report late in the process is a report about a report about a report, which must be kept distinct from all the others by naming the whole line of men it came through. The Repeating works. It is not thrifty.

And the second remark, which matters more. Nothing in the Repeating requires the greater count to be right about anything. It requires only that when most of the answers a loyal man holds say north, he ends up at north. Take the commonest answer with hold as the fallback, as above; or, if the answers can be ordered, take the middle one. Either serves. The method does not care.

Proof — that the repeating works when loyal men outnumber traitors two to one
  1. Let the ring hold n men of whom at most m are traitors, with n > 3m. The method runs in m + 1 fillings: the chief speaks, and thereafter each man repeats what he heard to all who have not yet heard it through him, each man finally taking the majority of the accounts he holds.
  2. First, the case of a loyal chief. He says the same thing — call it v — to every leader. Each loyal leader therefore holds v from the chief directly, and receives from the other loyal leaders their honest repetitions of v.
  3. Of the n − 1 accounts a loyal leader holds, at most m come from traitors. The rest, at least n − 1 − m, say v. Since n > 3m, we have n − 1 − m > 2m − 1, so the honest accounts outnumber the traitorous ones and the majority is v. The second condition is met.
  4. Now the case of a traitorous chief. He is one of the at most m traitors, so among the n − 1 leaders at most m − 1 are traitors, and n − 1 > 3(m − 1) holds as well.
  5. So the leaders, repeating among themselves, are running the same method one size smaller with one fewer traitor permitted. By induction on m it works for them: every loyal leader arrives at the same account of what each given leader reported, whatever that leader actually is.
  6. Since all loyal leaders hold the same set of accounts, they take the majority of the same set, and therefore reach the same decision. The first condition is met — and the second is vacuous, the chief being a traitor.
  7. The base case m = 0 is immediate: no traitors, one filling, everyone repeats the truth.∎
Chapter VI

Why Four and Not Three

The fourth seat is not a convenience. Three in the ring cannot bear one traitor, four can, and the rule is exactly this: to bear a given number of traitors the ring must hold more than three times that number. One traitor wants four in the ring. Two want seven. Three want ten. Fewer than that and there is nothing to be arranged, however the council arranges it.

The argument for the general rule is a pretty one and does not need any new machinery, only the result of Chapter IV. Suppose someone claims a method that gets a ring of three or fewer per traitor safely through. Then put three men in a ring — one of them a traitor — and have each of them privately play the parts of a third of the claimed council, doing in his head everything his several characters would say and hear. One of the three is a traitor, so the characters he plays may all misbehave; but they are only a third of the claimed council, and the claimed method was supposed to survive that many. So the claimed method delivers its guarantee among the characters; and since each of the three real men is simply reading off what his own characters agreed, the three real men agree too — and we have got a ring of three safely through one traitor, which Chapter IV says cannot be done.

So no such method exists. One man standing in for several is the whole trick, and it is worth noticing what it exploits: nothing about the ring is being cheated. The impersonation is honest. A council cannot tell whether the voice it hears speaks for one man or for three, and that is why a small council cannot borrow the strength of a large one.

There is a temptation, at this point, to suppose that all this difficulty comes from insisting on exact agreement, and that a council content with rough agreement would have an easier time. It would not. Suppose the leaders needed only to reach the gates within a little of one another — no plan, no named gate, merely near enough. But hand me any method that achieves that, and I will build exact agreement out of it, which is the wrong way round for anyone hoping the rough version is the easier one. The construction: let the chief name an early hour to mean one gate and a late hour to mean the other. Each leader runs the rough method, ends holding some hour, and reads a gate off it — early enough, the first gate; late enough, the second; and if his hour falls in the middle, he asks his peer which gate that peer settled on and takes the same. Rough agreement about the hour is all that is needed to make that work. So feed a three-man ring into it and you have exact agreement among three against one traitor, which Chapter IV says cannot be had. So rough agreement is exactly as hard as agreement. The difficulty was never the precision.

Chapter VII

The Seal Rings

Every man in the ring has a signet ring, and each ring's bezel carries its own device. The chief's is an owl on a heavy gold bezel. The others are bronze: a wolf, a ship, a key. The devices are old and were not chosen for this purpose, and the key on Dolevios's is thought to have come off the hill with the rest of the storeroom's contents.

When an order is to be passed on, it is not merely spoken. It is cut into the wax of a hinged tablet, and the man who first writes it presses his ring into a blob of wax at the tablet's edge. The next man to carry it further adds his own impression beside the first, and the next his beside that, so the tablet comes to carry a row of seals along its edge in the order they were added, and anyone may read the row and see the line the order came down.

What this does and does not buy needs stating carefully, because the seal is the most over-trusted object in the book.

What a seal is worth

A loyal man's seal cannot be forged, and an order under his seal cannot be altered without the alteration showing. If a tablet bears the wolf's impression over the words north, then the man with the wolf ring put it there, over those words.

Anyone may check. A seal is not a secret; every man in the ring knows every device, and a leader handed a tablet can read the whole row without asking anybody's leave.

Nothing at all is promised about a traitor's own seal. A traitor may hand his ring to another traitor and let him seal with it. Two of them may swap rings for an evening. So a row of seals proves what the loyal men in it did, and proves nothing whatever about the rest.

And a seal cannot compel. A man holding a sealed order may simply not pass it on. He cannot change it; he can drop it in the dark. Withholding survives the seal entirely.

This account does not say whose hand gave the ring and whose received it, and the reason is not delicacy. To say it would be to answer the question the book does not answer and cannot: it would be telling you who the traitors are. Nobody on that crown ever finds that out, and neither does the reader. What can be set down is the mechanism — that rings change hands — and the mechanism is set down.

Proof — that seals remove the bound entirely
  1. Suppose every utterance is sealed: a man's seal cannot be counterfeited by anyone else, any tampering with a sealed tablet is evident, and any man can tell whose seal is whose. A traitor may still lie — but only by putting his own seal to the lie.
  2. The chief seals his order and sends it. Each leader who receives a properly sealed order not already in his collection adds it, then adds his own seal beneath and passes the tablet to every leader who has not yet signed it.
  3. When no further tablets arrive, each loyal leader looks at the set of distinct orders he has collected. If it holds exactly one, he obeys it; otherwise he falls back on a fixed rule known to all — retreat, say.
  4. All loyal leaders end with the same collection. If a loyal leader holds an order, it arrived on a tablet bearing a chain of seals. He appends his own and forwards it to everyone who has not signed, so every other loyal leader receives it too — unless they already had it. And a traitor cannot fabricate an order the chief never sealed, since he cannot forge the chief's seal.
  5. Equal collections give equal decisions, so the loyal leaders agree.
  6. And if the chief is loyal, he sealed exactly one order and no traitor can produce a second one bearing his seal. Every loyal leader's collection holds that one order alone, and all obey it.
  7. Nowhere did the argument count the traitors. The bound of a third is a consequence of unsealed speech, not of treachery as such, and seals dissolve it: the method copes with m traitors for any number of men whatever.∎

The paper notes only that the problem becomes vacuous below m + 2 men. That is a remark about when the question is worth asking, not a bound on when it can be answered.

Chapter VIII

The Sealed Repeating

With seals in use the council can do something it could not do before, and the change is larger than it looks. The method is the Sealed Repeating, and it goes as follows.

Each leader keeps a tally of the orders he has properly received — not of tablets, of orders; several tablets may carry the same order down different lines of men, and they count once. The chief writes his order, seals it with the owl, and gives it to each leader. A leader who receives an order he has not seen before adds it to his tally and, if the row of seals on it is not yet as long as the number of traitors being borne, adds his own seal and passes it to every leader not already named in the row. When no further tablet can reach him — and he can tell, because a man who will send nothing says so within a filling, and silence within a filling is a fact — he looks at his tally. If it holds exactly one order, he obeys it. If it holds none, he holds. If it holds several, he and every other loyal leader hold the same several, and so any fixed rule applied to them gives the same answer to all of them; take the middle one and be done.

Two things follow, and the first is startling.

Three in the ring is now enough. The council that Chapter IV showed to be hopeless is not hopeless once the rings are in use. Take the chief and two leaders, and suppose the chief is the traitor, and suppose he seals north to one and south to the other. Each leader adds his own seal to what he got and passes it across. So each of them ends holding two orders, north and south — the same two — and each applies the same rule to the same pair and they go to the same gate. The first condition is met by three men, which oral speech could not manage.

And something else has happened, which is worth pausing on because it is the only moment in either book of this setting when anybody learns anything they could not have guessed. Each of those two leaders is now holding two tablets, both carrying the owl, over two different orders. The chief's own seal stands on both. A loyal man's seal cannot be forged, and so, if these were the sealings of a loyal man, they could not both exist. They do. Each leader therefore knows, with certainty, that the chief is a traitor.

Notice exactly how far that knowledge reaches, because it does not reach far. It is knowledge about the chief and about nobody else. It tells neither leader anything about the other. It arises only because the chief was careless enough to seal two contradictory orders, and a traitor need not be; he could have sealed one order and said nothing to the second man, and been merely silent rather than exposed. The seal makes a particular kind of lie self-reporting. It does not make lying visible.

And any number of traitors can be borne. This is the second consequence and it is the reason the seals matter at all. Oral speech needed more than three men per traitor; the Sealed Repeating needs no such thing at all. It bears as many traitors as there happen to be, whatever the size of the ring: there is no threshold to clear and no proportion to maintain. One remark only, and it is not a condition the method imposes — once the ring is smaller than two more than the number of traitors the question has stopped meaning anything, because by then there are not enough loyal men left for agreement among them to be a subject. Where the Repeating bought each additional traitor with a whole extra council's worth of men, the Sealed Repeating buys it with a filling.

A small technical note, for completeness. A leader's own seal on a tablet is only ever an acknowledgement that it passed through his hands; the last man in a row adds nothing by sealing, since no one will relay his tablet further. Where only one traitor is being borne, the leaders need not seal at all — the chief's seal alone does the work, and the row is one impression long.

Chapter IX

What the Ring Cannot Learn

It remains to say what the council has and has not got, and the distinction is the thing this book exists for.

It has agreement, and it has it cheaply, in a few fillings of a clay jar. Four in the ring bear one traitor by plain speech; three bear any number once the rings are in use. In every case the loyal men leave the ring and go to the same gate, and the gangs on the terraces below move as one body, and a man standing above them sees a single column and not two. That is not a small thing. It is the whole of what was wanted.

What it has not got, and will not get, is any knowledge of who lied. Consider a loyal leader at the end of a filling in which he has heard the chief say one thing and a peer report that the chief said another. He holds two accounts of one utterance and they disagree, and exactly one of the following is true, and nothing available to him distinguishes them:

The thing that cannot be settled

Either the chief is a traitor and said different things to different men, and the peer reporting to him is honest —

— or the chief is loyal and said one thing to everybody, and the peer reporting to him is lying.

Both accounts fit every sound he has heard, every face he has watched and every seal he can read. He will not find out. The methods in this book were not built to tell him; they were built so that he does not need to be told.

That is the point on which the two halves of this siege turn out to be the same problem wearing different clothes. A commander in a camp at the foot of the hill cannot tell a dead man from a slow bird, and so can never safely stop waiting. A leader in the ring on the crown cannot tell a lying chief from a lying peer, and so can never safely believe one man. Neither of them is short of information about the world; both are short of exactly one distinction, and in both cases the distinction is not there to be had. The camps lose the promise to finish. The council keeps that and loses the truth. Neither loses agreement, which is the one thing both were built to protect, and which is the reason both arrangements are worth describing at all.

So the water reaches the line, and the filling ends, and four men rise from a ring cut for five and go down to the terraces to fetch their gangs. Which of them meant what he said is not recorded anywhere, by anyone, and there is no later part of this account in which it comes out. The storeroom is still locked. The soldiers are still in the dark at the bottom of the hill with their rams pointed at nothing. Nothing is concluded, and the reader who has followed this far should understand by now that nothing was ever going to be: what has been described is not a night on which something happened but an arrangement which had these properties, and would have had them on any night.

The ideas. A commander must send an order to n − 1 lieutenants so that all loyal lieutenants obey the same order, and so that they obey his order if he is loyal. The second condition implies the first only when the commander is loyal, and he may not be; that is the whole of the problem. With oral messages — messages whose contents are entirely under the sender's control — no solution exists for three generals facing one traitor, because a loyal lieutenant cannot distinguish a lying commander from a lying peer. More generally nothing works with fewer than 3m + 1 generals for m traitors, shown by having three generals each simulate a third of a hypothetical smaller solution.

Algorithm OM(m) does work above that bound: the commander sends his value, each lieutenant relays what he received by OM(m − 1), and each takes the majority of what he collects. One extra round per traitor tolerated, at a cost in messages that grows accordingly. Adding unforgeable signatures changes the economics completely: SM(m) tolerates any number of traitors with no requirement on the number of generals at all — the paper remarks only that the problem is vacuous below m + 2 — and in particular three generals now suffice against one. Crucially, no assumption is made about a traitorous general's signature — it may be forged by another traitor — so signatures prove what the loyal did and nothing about the rest.

What none of these algorithms provides, and what is often expected of them, is identification. A loyal lieutenant ends in agreement without ever learning who lied. The single exception is narrow and self-inflicted: a commander who signs two contradictory orders convicts himself, because his signature cannot be forged and therefore cannot stand on both by accident.
