Distributed Algorithms of Ancient Greece · Paxos · Schedia

One Leader at a Time

Based on “Viewstamped Replication: A New Primary Copy Method to Support Highly-Available Distributed Systems” by Brian Oki and Barbara Liskov (1988)

Many systems put one leader in charge at a time. When the leader falls silent, how does another take over without losing anything the last one promised?

Planned cover

Inside the Tholos of Schedia in morning light: the round podium at the centre under the roof's lantern, its holder seated at the desk beside the board on its post, and runners in short brown tunics setting off from the podium towards the benches round the wall, each with a wax tablet. Through the porch door, the town's roofs and the bay.

In the Tholos of Schedia five legislators keep one law book, and only the holder of the podium may add to it. Messengers are slow, legislators come and go, and one who goes home comes back to smooth wax. A law passes once three of the five hold it, and a new holder starts from the fullest book a majority can show, so no law that passed is ever lost.

Chapter I

The Council and Its Law

Schedia, the city on the eastern arm of Paxos, keeps its own law, apart from the Paxon Parliament's. Its law is made in the Tholos, a round hall on a terrace above the town, where nobody can be heard across the floor and everything goes by messenger*. The council has five legislators, Okios, Liskovia, Kleon, Melissa and Theron, and each keeps a copy of the law book at a bench of their own.

What is the law book? A list of lines, written one after another: harbour dues are two obols a hull; the cove is closed to nets in spring; the dues above are halved for boats of Schedia. Why must every copy hold the same lines in the same order? Because a line can depend on the lines before it. The halving means nothing without the dues it halves.

What can go wrong in the Tholos? Three things, and only these. Messengers are slow, and some never arrive. A legislator may step out of the hall for a while, and then word reaches them late or not at all. And a legislator may go home, and come back later. Nobody lies, and nobody forges a note.

What the council owes the city One book. The five copies behave as a single book kept in a single place: a citizen never sees two different laws standing in the same place.
Passed stays passed. Once a law has passed, it stands in the book for good, in its place, whoever holds the podium afterwards.
A module group of cohorts behaves as a single logical entity; nodes crash (fail-stop) and recover; the network may lose, delay, duplicate and reorder messages and may partition; failures are not Byzantine. Events known to a majority of cohorts survive view changes. §1–2, §4.

Five copies, and legislators forever coming and going. Who decides what goes into the book, and in what order?

Chapter II

The Podium

What goes into the book is decided at the podium. The podium stands at the centre of the Tholos, and one legislator at a time holds it: the holder. Only the holder adds lines to the book. The others copy what the holder sends them, and do nothing else with the law.

Is the podium a pulpit, then? No. Nobody can be heard from it either. It is an office. The holder writes each line at the desk on the podium and gives it the next number, and then it is carried to every bench.

Who carries it? Each bench has its own runner from the podium, a boy who takes the lines to that bench in the order they were written and never skips one. A runner may be held up, and a bench may be out of reach for a while; the lines still arrive in order when they arrive. So a bench that holds line 12 holds lines 1 to 11 as well.

And the city, how does it ask for a law? Citizens at the doors send petitions to the podium: new dues, a closed season, a ruling on a quay. A petition that reaches a bench instead is sent back with the name of the holder. The holder weighs each petition and writes the lines it calls for.

The podium's work Writing. The holder numbers each line in turn and gives it to the runners.
Running. Each bench's runner delivers the lines in number order, skipping none, for as long as the bench is within reach.
Petitions. Only the holder answers them; a bench returns a petition with the holder's name.
The primary executes calls; backups are passive and receive event records. The communication buffer delivers event records reliably, in timestamp order, to every backup in the view; a failure to deliver leads to a view change. A cohort that is not the active primary rejects calls with the current viewid and primary. §2, §3.3.

The holder writes a line and hands it to the runners. When may the holder tell a citizen that the petition is law?

Chapter III

When a Law Passes

A petition becomes law when its line is safe, and a line is safe once enough of the council hold it. How many is enough? A majority: three of the five, the holder and two others. So the holder does not wait for everyone. Each runner comes back with the bench's word, I hold everything up to line 12, and as soon as two benches have said so, line 12 passes. Only then does the holder answer the petitioner: it is law.

Why not wait for all five? Because someone is nearly always out of reach, and a council that waited for all five would pass nothing. Why not one other, or none? Because the holder may be gone tomorrow, and the next holder will start from what a majority can show. Any two majorities of five share a legislator, as any two majorities in the Chamber across the bay share a member*. A line three hold is always found again.

Passed Means Three Hold It

A law has passed when its line, and every line before it, is held by the holder and at least two others.

What of lines that no majority holds yet? They are drafts. A change of holder may lose them, as we will see. Is that a harm? No. Nobody was told they were law.

Waiting for two Before answering a petition, the holder waits until two benches hold every line up to its line.
force-to(v) waits until a sub-majority of backups, one fewer than a majority of the configuration, know every event of the current view up to v.ts; with the primary, a majority then knows it. §3. In the paper the moments that must wait are a transaction's prepare and commit; this book, keeping one law book, has only one, the passing of a law.

The holder numbers the lines. But holders change. How does a number written under one holder compare with a number written under the next?

Chapter IV

Board and Line

A line's number means something only under one holder, so every spell at the podium gets a mark of its own. It is a board, which hangs on its post beside the desk and bears a count and the name of the legislator who raised it: board 7, Liskovia. Boards are ordered by their count, and two with the same count by name, in the order of the alphabet. Each legislator raises boards only in their own name, so no two boards are ever the same.

Line numbers start again under every board: the first line written under board 7 is line 1. A line is known by board and line, board 7, line 12, and two of them compare easily: the later board is the later, whatever the line numbers; under the same board, the higher line is the later. Board 6, line 40 comes before board 7, line 1.

Each legislator also keeps a note in the margin of their tablets: for every board they have served under, the last line they hold. Liskovia's might read board 6 to line 40; board 7 to line 12.

The Later Line Knows More

Under one board, a legislator who holds a later line holds every earlier one. And a legislator who holds any line of a later board holds everything its holder started that board with.

Why is that so? Under one board, because runners never skip a line. Across boards, because every board opens, as we will see, with the holder sending the whole book. What does it not say? That a higher line number means more across boards. Board 6, line 40 may hold far more lines than board 7, line 1, and still be the older copy.

Boards and lines viewid = ⟨cnt, mid⟩, totally ordered and unique to its creator; a timestamp, unique and ordered within a view; viewstamp = ⟨viewid, ts⟩; each cohort's history is a sequence of viewstamps, one per viewid, and its state reflects exactly the events up to each. §2, Figures 1 and 4.

The board changes when the podium falls silent. Who notices, and what do they do?

Chapter V

An Empty Podium

A silent podium is noticed through the roll. Every so often each legislator sends each of the others a note, I am here: the roll. A legislator who stops hearing from someone, or hears again from someone who was away, or has just come back from home, calls for a new board. Whoever does so is the caller.

Does a silent legislator mean one who has gone home? Not necessarily. They may have stepped out, or their notes may be slow. The caller cannot tell, and does not need to: a board is called either way.

How is a board called? The caller takes a count higher than any board they have seen, adds their own name, and sends every legislator an invitation to it. A legislator invited to a board higher than any they have seen accepts, stops working under the old board, and answers in one of two ways:

The two answers With tablets. I hold up to board 6, line 40, and whether they held the podium under board 6.
Without. I have lost my tablets. My bench says board 6.
“I'm alive” messages; the view manager's invitations with a new viewid; a normal acceptance carries the cohort's viewstamp and whether it was primary, a crash-accept only its viewid. §4.

And if two legislators call at once? The higher board wins. The lower caller, invited to the higher board, accepts it and answers like anyone else. Several callers slow the council down, with more notes crossing the floor, but they never confuse it.

Invitations out, answers coming in. When may the caller form the new board, and who takes the podium?

Chapter VI

Who Takes the Podium

A new board can form only when two things are true: a majority of the council has answered, and among those who answered is someone sure to hold every law that has ever passed. When everyone who answered still has their tablets, the second follows from the first. Three answers are a majority; every passed law is held by three; the two threes share someone. Of the answers, the one showing the latest board and line holds everything that passed, by The Later Line Knows More.

So that legislator takes the podium. If the old holder is among those with the latest line, the old holder is preferred: nothing then changes but the board. The new holder opens the board by sending every member the whole book as they hold it, with its notes, as the board's first line, the opening line. A member whose tablets ran past it, with drafts that never passed, smooths those lines away.

What Passed Survives

A law that passed under any board stands in the book under every later board.

Sketch — that what passed survives, while everyone keeps their tablets
  1. The paper argues this informally (§2, §4); what follows is that argument, laid out by board.
  2. Suppose a law's line passed under board b: the holder and two others held it, and every line before it.
  3. The next board to form, c, formed on answers from a majority. Those answers and the three who held the law share at least one legislator, who answered with board b and a line at or past the law's line.
  4. The new holder is the answerer with the latest board and line. By The Later Line Knows More, their copy holds everything the shared legislator's does, the law included.
  5. Board c opens with the new holder's whole book, so every member of c holds the law, and any later line of c is written after it.
  6. The same step carries the law from c to the board after, and so on to every later board.∎

What changes when some answers come without tablets? Take board 6, held by Okios. Line 12 passed: Okios, Liskovia and Kleon hold it. Melissa and Theron hold only to line 10. Then Okios goes home and comes back with smooth wax, and Liskovia and Kleon step out. A board is called, and Okios, Melissa and Theron answer: a majority. Should it form? No. The only answers with tablets stop at line 10, and line 12 has passed. A board formed now would start without a law the city was told had passed.

So a lost tablet is not simply left out. The rule for forming a board, when a majority has answered, is that one of three things must also hold:

When a board may form A majority of the council has answered, and
1. a majority of the whole council answered with tablets; or
2. every answer without tablets names an older board than the latest shown by an answer with tablets; or
3. they name the same board, and that board's holder answered with tablets.
A majority accepted, and (1) a majority accepted normally, or (2) crash-viewid < normal-viewid, or (3) crash-viewid = normal-viewid and the primary of that view accepted normally. The new primary is the cohort with the largest viewstamp, the old primary if possible. §4.

Why each? With a majority holding tablets, the overlap works as before. A lost tablet from an older board lost nothing that matters: whatever passed before its board ended was carried into every later board, and an answer with tablets shows a later one. And under one board the holder holds at least as much as any bench, since every line starts at the podium, so the holder's tablets cover anyone else's that were lost.

What happens on the left, then? Nothing, for now. The caller waits and calls again. When Liskovia or Kleon comes back within reach, a board forms, and line 12 is in it.

The old holder may have heard none of this. What happens when two legislators both believe they hold the podium?

Chapter VII

The Holder Who Has Not Noticed

Two legislators can each believe they hold the podium. Say Okios holds board 6 and steps out onto the porch, and for a while no runner reaches him and none of his reaches the benches. Inside, the others notice, raise board 7, and Liskovia takes the podium. Out on the porch, Okios has heard nothing of it. As far as he knows, board 6 still stands.

Does the holding go with the podium? No. The podium is where the holder sits when present; the holding rides on the board's count, which every note carries. Okios may go on taking petitions at the porch door and writing lines under board 6. Can he pass any of them? He cannot. A line passes when two others hold it under his board, and board 7 formed on a majority, three who have taken the new board and refuse any note that carries board 6. That leaves at most one other who might take his lines. He can never reach three.

Two Holders, One Law

A holder who has missed a later board can still write lines, but can pass none of them.

Can anything go wrong while it lasts? A citizen at the porch who asks Okios what the book says may hear his copy's answer, which may lack laws passed under board 7. He answers from what he holds, and passes nothing new. As soon as a runner reaches him, or he walks back in, he sees board 7 and joins it.

Several holders “The system performs correctly even if there are several active primaries … The old primary will not be able to prepare and commit user transactions, however, since it cannot force their effects to the backups.” §4.1.

Only the holder's tablets cover for lost ones, and only a majority can pass a law. What if too many go home at once?

Chapter VIII

Wax, Not Ink

Going home costs the tablets because Schedia keeps its law in wax. The tablets stay on the bench, and are smoothed when their legislator goes home. Only two things are cut into the bench's stone: the legislator's name, and the last board they served under, recut whenever a board forms. That is the stone. Stepping out keeps the tablets. Going home loses them.

What does a legislator who comes back from home know? Their name and their last board, and nothing of the law. They call for a new board at once, answer without tablets, and are brought up to date only by the new board's opening line.

Why would a city keep its law in wax? Because cutting every line in stone is slow, and wax is quick. Schedia counts on a majority remembering, where each legislator could instead keep a record that nothing wipes.

What if a majority go home at once? Then the rule for forming a board may never again be met: too few answers can show what passed, and no holder with tablets remains to cover for them. The council stops for good. Is the law then wrong? No. It is lost, but no board ever starts without it.

Stopped, Never Wrong

Lost tablets can stop the council for ever. They can never make a board start from a book missing a law that passed.

What survives going home Most of a cohort's state is volatile. Only mymid, configuration and mygroupid, written when the cohort is created, and cur-viewid, written at the end of each view change, are on stable storage. A recovered cohort sets up-to-date to false and starts a view change. A catastrophe, a majority crashed at once, “does not cause a group to enter a new view missing some needed information. Rather, it causes the algorithm to never again form a new view.” §4, §4.2.

The paper names ways to make this rarer: keep stable storage at the holder only, or give each member a supply that lets it write to lasting storage in the background. It leaves the choice to how much the law is worth.

Across the bay, the Parliament keeps its law in ink, and its Chamber has no podium. What does each arrangement buy?

Chapter IX

Across the Bay

The Parliament across the bay keeps the law of the rest of Paxos in a hall of the same kind, a rotunda where nobody can be heard, with legislators who come and go and messengers who take as long as they take*. The two law books never meet: each covers its own city*. So the two halls can be compared like with like.

What does the Chamber's ink buy? A Paxon legislator writes the decrees in indelible ink in a ledger of their own, and a legislator back from any absence still has everything*. The Parliament cannot be stopped for good by legislators going home. Schedia's wax is quicker, and it asks that a majority not go home at once.

What does Schedia's podium buy? In the Chamber, any legislator may start a ballot for a decree. A president is chosen only so that ballots do not collide, and several may believe themselves president for a while, which costs time but never makes two ledgers disagree*. In Schedia only the holder writes, the benches only copy, and a change of holder is a step of its own, in which the new holder takes over the fullest book a majority can show. Most of the time a single trip of the runners out and back passes a law.

What do the two halls share? The one fact both rest on: any two majorities share a legislator. The Chamber uses it for each decree's ballot; the Tholos uses it for each change of holder.

Were the two arrangements one idea in two dresses? They were found apart. Oki and Liskov published theirs in 1988; Lamport's account of the Parliament first circulated in 1989 and was not printed until 1998. That is why Schedia has a hall of its own on its own arm of the island.

The Tholos keeps passed laws through any change of holder. What does it not promise?

Chapter X

What It Does Not Say

Does the council always get a new board in the end? No. If notes keep going astray, a caller who stops waiting too soon forms a board without someone who could have answered, that legislator soon calls again, and the council can go on changing boards. The paper says so plainly and advises long waits and duplicate notes. Passed laws are kept whatever happens; getting a new board is hoped for, not promised.

What of a petitioner who gets no answer? They cannot tell whether their petition was written down before the holder fell silent, and sending it again to the new holder might put it in the book twice. The paper's answer is to abandon the whole piece of work rather than risk doing it twice.

Is one law book the paper's case? No. The paper builds all this for transactions, pieces of work that call on several replicated groups and are settled together by two-phase commit. There, a call is answered as soon as the primary has done it, and the forcing to a majority comes when the transaction prepares and commits; work lost in a change of holder makes its transaction abort. This book keeps one council and one book, and lets the passing of a law stand for those moments.

Could a legislator lie? Not here. Legislators stop, go home, come back and miss notes, but what they write and answer is true. Keeping order when some members lie is a harder task, with more members and more rounds of notes.

How does a citizen know who holds the podium? They ask. A petition that reaches the wrong bench comes back with the holder's name. The paper leans on a directory, always available, that says who belongs to each group, and notes that no group can be more available than it.

The ideas, plainly

Oki and Liskov replicate a service over a small group of machines that may crash, recover and be cut off from one another, but never lie. One machine, the primary, does all the work and sends a record of each step to the backups, in order. A step is safe once a majority of the group knows it, so the primary waits for enough backups before it tells anyone a result is final.

Each period with one primary is a view, named by a view number, and each step is stamped with the view and its position in it: a viewstamp. When the primary seems to be gone, or the group's members change, any member can call a view change. It collects the viewstamps of a majority, and the member with the latest one becomes the new primary and starts the view by sending its whole state. Because any two majorities overlap, and because a later viewstamp implies knowledge of everything earlier, whatever a majority knew survives.

The machines keep almost everything in memory, so a crash loses a member's state; three extra conditions stop a view from forming on answers that cannot show what was made final. If a majority lose their state at once, the group stops for good rather than go on with something missing. An old primary that has not noticed a change can do no harm, because it can no longer reach a majority.
