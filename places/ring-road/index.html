Arche · The Ring Road

Three Houses Keeping Order

Three trading houses that keep no common hour, sending one another slips and goods down a road on which no messenger can pass another.

Read this first, and any book set on the ring road can be read next, in any order. It says who the houses are, what carries word between them and what goes down the road, which every book here takes for granted. The island they stand on comes last.

The Three Houses

Three trading houses pass goods and orders among themselves, and they are known by number: House 1, House 2 and House 3. They are alike. Each has a storehouse of goods, a counting room with a scroll in which every piece of its business is written in order, and clerks who keep the scroll. Nothing sets one house apart from the others except the part an example gives it: the house an order is sent to, the house that begins a count. Where the houses must be put in some order, the order is by number.

A house knows only what is in its own scroll and what has reached its gate. It cannot look into another house's scroll, and it learns of another house's business only when a slip about it arrives.

Nor do the houses share an hour. The books are set in the trading season, which on Arche is the season of low cloud, and the cloud silences the sundials. Each counting room has a water-clock, filled by clerks who cannot see one another's, and the three keep no common hour. Nobody knows what time it is anywhere but in his own room, and not truly there.

Messengers and Carts

Messengers carry written slips between the houses, and carts and pack-trains carry goods, all on the one coastal road. The law of the road is old and nobody on Arche breaks it: nobody overtakes. A fast messenger caught behind a slow cart walks behind it until the cart turns in at a gate. By the same law a slip always goes by the direct stretch between two houses, never the long way round past a third house's gate. So there are six tracks, one for each ordered pair of houses, and each carries only its own traffic.

A messenger may be fast or slow, and a cart may be held up behind a lame mule for hours, so nobody can say how long anything will take. But nothing is lost: no messenger or cart falls from the cliff. And nothing is passed: whatever a house sends down a track arrives at the other end in the order it was sent.

Nothing else carries word between the houses. They light no beacon on the mountain and send no word over it. A goatherd who knows the smugglers' tracks sometimes crosses, but he may take two hours or two days or never come down, and no house sends its business that way. When he does carry news, it is news the houses' account scrolls cannot see.

What a Slip Says

Every book set on the ring road starts from the same few facts, and states them again in its own words. (1) Three houses, alike, each acting only on its own scroll and on what reaches its gate. (2) Six one-way tracks, one for each ordered pair of houses. What goes down a track arrives, in the order it was sent, after a time nobody can bound. (3) No common hour. (4) Nothing else carries word.

What goes down the tracks is orders and goods: a slip asking for goods or answering such a slip, and the carts that carry what was asked for. Each book then asks one question of that traffic, and adds to the slips whatever its answer needs.

The Island

The houses stand evenly spaced round the coast of Arche, each with a harbour town about it. A single limestone massif, Mount Phyle, fills the middle of the island and leaves only a strip of coast round it. The mountain is broad as well as high, so it stands between every pair of houses: no house can see another, and no line of sight crosses the island.

The mountain is no way across. It is held by a siege, with bandits on its crown and mercenaries at its foot, and in the trading season its summit is lost in cloud. The mercenaries' tents carry nothing from one house to another. Nor is the sea a way round: reefs and tide-races off every headland stop small boats coasting from one cove to the next, and ships go out to sea, not round the island. The ring road, cut into the cliffs round the coast, is all there is.

Reality Checks

What each part of this place has in common with the real Greek world, so that a reader can tell what is borrowed from what is made up. Close means the real thing is much like it; loose means it is borrowed and changed; invented means the setting made it up; anachronism means the real thing is of another age. A dagger (†) marks a citation given from memory and not checked against the text.
