Agreeing When Messages Run Late

Based on “Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony” by Cynthia Dwork, Nancy Lynch and Larry Stockmeyer (1988)

If messages can run late without limit, agreement cannot be guaranteed; yet real networks are prompt most of the time. Can a protocol stay safe however late messages run, and still decide once they arrive on time again?

The four mercenaries at the foot of the hill must go in at one gate, and any one of them may fall silent. While the wind blows, no one can say how long a raven will take, and no arrangement can make any of them go. But the wind always drops in the end, and in still air every bird is at its perch within a glass. Change only that, and the four never go to different gates, whatever the wind does, and once it has dropped they all go, though none of them can tell when it dropped.

Chapter I

A Windy Night

At the foot of Mount Phyle four mercenaries, Number 1 to Number 4, keep four tents out of sight of one another and send word only by raven*. They must all go in at one gate, north or south. Each formed his own opinion at dusk, his sighting, and going to a gate is final: what a man thinks before he goes does not count.

The birds always arrive, and what they carry is what was written. Nobody lies. Any one of the four may be killed. It has not happened, and no one is killed in this book; what matters is that it may. A dead man's tent falls silent and stays silent.

The three demands First. No two of them go to different gates.
Second. If all four sighted the same gate, that is the gate they go to.
Third. Every man who is not killed eventually goes.

Can they be sure of meeting all three? With no promise about the birds, no arrangement the four could make guarantees that any of them ever goes*. The trouble is two things together: there is no longest flight, and a dead man looks exactly like a slow bird. Would a longest flight change that? It would. If it were known truly that no bird had ever taken more than five hours, silence of six hours would mean something*. No such promise is made at the foot of the hill.

Is there something less than a longest flight that would still be enough? There is.

What keeps a raven in the folds is the wind. While it blows, a bird let go into the gullies goes down among the hill's crows and sits it out in the pines, for an hour or for the night, and nobody can say for how long; a bird that has gone down among them may stay there as long as it likes, even after the wind has dropped. A bird let go in still air comes straight. On the nights of this book the wind blows over the whole hill, and while it blows the four can arrange nothing that makes anyone go*. But it always drops in the end, and once it has dropped it stays down, and no bird let go in still air takes longer than a short span that every man knows.

That is the whole change. Without it, nobody could count on the wind dropping, and that is the whole of the trouble. Here the four can count on it. Can they see it? No. A lull at one tent says nothing about the folds two spurs away, where a bird may still be sitting in a pine, and a lull that ends in a fresh gust looks exactly like the end of the wind. No man ever knows that the wind has dropped over the whole hill. He knows only that it will.

So the four need an arrangement that never sends two men to different gates however long the wind blows, since no one can tell when it stops, and that sends everyone once it has stopped. But how can a man use a span of time he has no way to measure?

Chapter II

Glasses and Late Birds

Every man measures time with sand. Each tent has a sandglass, and the glasses run alike. On the evenings of this book the crown stands clear at sunset, and the last light leaves the top of the hill at one moment, seen from every tent at the foot. At that moment each man turned his glass. He turns it again the moment it runs out and keeps count of the glasses, and since the four glasses started together and run alike, all four men always have the same count. Do the glasses drift apart over a night? They do, as any sand does, but by less than a flight, which is all that is needed.

Is the last light a signal? No. It is the only common moment the four have, and nobody made it; the sun did, once, at dusk, and nothing like it happens again until morning. The bandits cannot use it on the crown. Their posts are tucked below the rim of the crags, and none can see the peak; the last light on the peak is seen only from away from it.

How long is a glass? Long enough for a man to write and tie a strip for every tent, and for any bird let go in still air to reach its perch before the sand runs out. That is what the known span buys: once the wind is down, a bird let go in a glass is taken in within the same glass.

And a bird that comes late? Every strip carries the count of the glass in which it was written. A man who takes in a bird in a later glass than the one on its strip reads it, and sets it aside. Nothing in it counts: not what it asks, not what it answers, not what it says was seen at dusk. Only a bird of one kind is taken whenever it comes, and we will meet it. Setting birds aside loses nothing that matters, and it makes every glass stand alone. What a man does in a glass depends only on the birds written in that glass, and while the wind blows, a glass's birds may simply fail to come.

The hill, as this book uses it (1) While the wind blows, a bird may take any time. None is lost.
(2) The wind drops at some moment and stays down. From then on, a bird let go in a glass reaches its perch within the same glass. A bird let go before the drop may still be in the pines.
(3) No man knows when the wind has dropped.
(4) The glasses were turned together at last light and run alike, so every man's count of glasses is the same.
(5) Late birds: a strip taken in during a later glass than the one it was written in is set aside, except a going bird.
(6) At most one of the four is killed. No one lies.

Take away line (2), and what is left? The camps on a night when nothing can be arranged*. The glasses on their own would not help. With no longest flight, a glass that runs out with no bird tells a man nothing, however many glasses he counts. What gives the glasses their meaning is the wind's dropping, and the wind's dropping is something no man sees.

Every man now shares a count of glasses, and late birds are set aside. What do the four do with that count?

Chapter III

Turns and Pledges

The four group the glasses in fours, and each four is a turn. The first turn is glasses one to four, the second five to eight, and so on through the night. Each turn belongs to one man, in the order of the tents' numbers: the first to Number 1, the second to Number 2, the third to Number 3, the fourth to Number 4, the fifth to Number 1 again. A turn's man is its owner.

Is the owner a leader? No. Nobody chose him; the count of glasses did, and it would have chosen him if he were dead. He gives no orders. He may only put forward a gate that three men, himself included, have already told him they could accept, and his turn passes when its fourth glass runs out, whatever has happened in it.

What does each man keep besides his sighting? Two things.

The first is the sighted gates: the gates he knows someone sighted. It starts as his own sighting. Every strip he sends carries it, and when a strip he does not set aside names a gate as seen, he adds it. So if all four sighted north, the gates seen at dusk are only ever north, in every tent.

The second is his pledges. A pledge is a strip tied to his tent pole, naming a gate and a turn: north, turn five. It means: in turn five, the owner may have gone north. A man may hold pledges on both gates at once. He can accept a gate if he holds no pledge on the other.

The four glasses of a turn The asking glass. Every man, the owner included, sends the owner the gates he can accept that are among the sighted gates.
The calling glass. If at least three men, himself included, named the same gate, the owner calls it: he sends pledge to north, turn five, say, to every tent, his own included. If both gates qualify he calls either; if neither, he calls nothing.
The answering glass. A man who took in the call in the calling glass ties a pledge for that gate and turn, unties any earlier pledge on the same gate, and sends the owner pledged. A pledge on the other gate stays where it is. If the owner takes in pledged from at least two men, himself possibly among them, he goes to the gate he called.
The untying glass. Every man sends every tent all his pledges, gate and turn. A man holding a pledge on one gate who learns of a pledge on the other gate at a turn as late as his own, or later, unties his.

Why these numbers? Four men, of whom one may be dead: so an owner can never count on hearing from more than three, and he never waits for a fourth. A call needs three men who can accept its gate. And why does an owner go on two pledged, not three or four? Two pledges on a gate leave only two men who could accept the other, and the other would need three.

Who decides which gate is better? Nobody. Which gate the four go to is not the subject of this book and is settled nowhere in it. An owner who may call either gate calls either; the arrangement is indifferent to which.

What do the sighted gates do? They keep the second demand. An owner calls only a gate that three men named from among the gates they know were sighted, and a gate enters that list only because someone sighted it. If all four sighted north, south is never on anyone's list, and nothing but north can ever be called.

Birds come late, are set aside, or sit in the pines for glass after glass. Can pledges tied on such a night ever send two men to different gates?

Chapter IV

Never Two Gates, in Any Wind

Why must the first demand hold in any wind? Because nobody can tell when the wind drops. If it held only in still air, the four would never know whether it held. So the arrangement is built so that the first demand needs nothing from the wind at all.

What does it rest on? Two facts. The first is small. In any turn only the owner calls, and he calls at most one gate, so no two men ever tie pledges on different gates for the same turn.

The second is the heart of it. Suppose some man goes, and take the earliest turn in which anyone went, say north in turn five. The owner of turn five had pledged from two men, so two men tied north, turn five. Could either of them lose his pledge on north? To lose it, he would have to untie it without tying a later north, and he unties only on hearing of a pledge on south at a turn as late as five. There was none in turn five, so it would have to be a later turn, say turn eight. But for south to be called in turn eight, three men had to be able to accept south in turn eight's asking glass, which means three men holding no pledge on north. Two of the four still held one. That leaves two.

Never Two Gates

However long the wind blows and however the birds are delayed, no two men ever go to different gates; and if all four sighted the same gate, no man goes to the other.

Proof — that no two men go to different gates, in any wind
  1. One gate per turn. Only a turn's owner calls, and he calls at most one gate in it. A pledge for turn k is tied only on taking in the call of turn k. So all pledges for turn k are on one gate.
  2. Suppose some man goes to a gate, and let turn k be the earliest turn in which an owner went; say he went north. He had pledged from two men, so two men tied north, turn k. Men who go on a going bird (described below) go to a gate some owner went to earlier, so it is enough to show that no owner in a later turn goes south.
  3. The two keep a pledge on north. Suppose not, and take the first untying glass in which one of the two unties a pledge on north for turn k or later without holding a later one. He unties it on learning of a pledge on south for some turn h at least as late. By step 1, h is not k, so h is later than k, and no later than this untying glass.
  4. A pledge on south for turn h means south was called in turn h, so three men could accept south in turn h's asking glass: three men with no pledge on north. But both of the two held a pledge on north from turn k until this untying glass, which is no earlier than turn h's own. There are four men; at most two held no pledge on north. This contradicts the three.
  5. So from turn k on, two men always hold a pledge on north, or have gone north and send nothing. In no later asking glass can three men name south, south is never called, no man ties a pledge on south for a later turn, and no owner goes south.
  6. The second demand. A gate is named in an asking glass only if it is among the sighted gates, and a gate enters that list only through someone's sighting. If all four sighted north, only north can be named, called or gone to.∎

Where does the wind come into the proof? Nowhere. It uses only the counting: two pledges that cannot be untied, and three men needed to accept the other gate, among four. Birds may arrive late, be set aside, or not come at all in the glass they were meant for; each of those only makes a call or a pledged fail to happen, and failing to happen is always safe. That is why the arrangement can run straight through the wind without anyone knowing when it ends.

Then why untie pledges at all? Because a pledge from a turn in which nobody went, because the owner's pledged birds were in the pines, would otherwise sit on its tent pole all night and forbid its holder to accept the other gate for ever. The untying glass lets a later call, which knew more, override it. What it can never override is a pledge held by the two men of a turn in which someone went, because no later call against that gate can gather three.

Nobody goes to a wrong gate, in any wind. But an arrangement where nobody ever goes would manage that too. Once the wind drops, does anyone go?

Chapter V

Going Once the Wind Drops

First, what happens when a man goes? Going is silence. A man who has struck his tent sends no more birds and ties no more pledges, and his quarter is open behind him. So a man who goes must first say so. Before he strikes his tent, he sends each of the other tents a going bird: going, north. A going bird carries no turn and is never set aside. A man who takes one in, at any glass, whenever it comes, sends going birds of his own to every tent, and then goes to the gate it names.

Why must every man who goes send his own going birds, not only the first? A man may be killed halfway through tying them, with two sent and one not. Whoever received one of his will send a full set. Since at most one man is killed, the birds of the others reach every tent in the end, and every man left alive goes, whenever the birds come.

Is this an addition to the paper? No, it is the paper's own device. The paper has a man who decides send the decision to everyone, lets anyone who receives it decide at once, and, where messages are delayed but never lost, lets a man who has sent it stop. On this hill no bird is ever lost.

So once one man goes, the third demand is settled. Does some man go once the wind has dropped?

While the wind blows, a turn can fail at every step. The asking birds are in the pines, so the owner hears from two men and calls nothing; or he calls, and the call reaches one man in time; or three men pledge, and their pledged birds come in the next glass and are set aside. Each failure leaves pledges tied that nobody went on. What clears them up? The untying glass, in still air. Every man then hears every live man's pledges within the glass, and any pledge on one gate that is no later than some pledge on the other is untied. What is left is on one gate at most.

Now take the next turn owned by a live man. In its asking glass every live man hears from every other; at least three are alive; the sighted gates have gone round, so every man's list holds the same gates; and all the pledges still tied are on one gate, which every live man can therefore accept. So all the live men name a common gate, the owner calls it, every live man pledges, and the owner has his two pledged before the answering glass runs out.

Going After the Wind

If the wind drops, every man who is not killed goes. If nobody had gone when it dropped, all go within twenty glasses of the drop. If someone had, they go when his going birds arrive, which is certain but bounded by no count of glasses.

Proof — that the four go once the wind has dropped
  1. From the glass after the wind drops, every bird let go by a live man is taken in within its own glass, so no bird between live men is set aside.
  2. After a still untying glass, pledges are on one gate at most. In it every live man hears all the pledges of every live man. If live men hold pledges on both gates, take the latest turn among them, say turn h on north; by the first step of Never Two Gates there is no pledge on south for turn h. Every pledge on south is for a turn no later than h, and its holder, hearing of north, turn h, unties it.
  3. Every man knows the same sighted gates. Every strip carries its sender's list, and in a still untying glass every live man hears from every other. After it, a gate that any live man knows was seen is known to all of them. A gate still pledged was called by some owner, who called it from a list, so it is among them.
  4. If some man had already gone when the wind dropped, his going birds are never lost, so they reach every tent in the end and every man left alive goes. A going bird let go while the wind blew may still be in the pines, so no count of glasses bounds this. So suppose no one had gone when the wind dropped. From then on a man who goes sends his going birds in still air, and they arrive within the glass.
  5. The wind drops during some turn. The untying glass of the turn after it is wholly still. Of the two turns after that, at least one is owned by a live man, since only one man may be dead. Take the first of them owned by a live man: it begins within three turns of the drop and ends within four, sixteen glasses.
  6. In its asking glass, each of the live men, at least three, names every gate on the common list that he can accept. If some gate is still pledged, every live man can accept it, since no pledge on the other gate remains; and it is on the list. If none is pledged, every live man can accept every gate on the list. Either way at least three men name a common gate, and the owner hears them all within the glass and calls it.
  7. Every live man takes in the call within the calling glass, pledges and sends pledged; the owner takes in at least two within the answering glass and goes, sending his going birds, within sixteen glasses of the wind's dropping.
  8. By the going birds, every man left alive then goes, and by Never Two Gates they all go to the same gate.∎

Where does twenty come from? It is the paper's: four glasses to a turn, and N + 1 = 5 turns after the wind drops. The proof above is a little tighter, but nothing depends on it. The paper's men earn it by never stopping: a man who has decided there goes on taking part. On the hill a man who goes is gone. If one goes while the wind blows, and another is dead, the two left cannot gather three names for a call, and they wait for his going birds, which will come, but at no glass anyone can count. Can anyone say, at some glass of the night, by now we will have gone? No: the bound counts glasses after an event no man sees, so nobody knows from which glass to count.

Where the allegory leaks

A night ends. The paper's rounds do not: it lets the wind blow for as long as it likes before dropping, and then promises a decision within a fixed number of rounds after. On the hill, if the wind drops late, dawn may come first. The model has no dawn, and nothing in this book turns on one: the first demand holds at every glass of the night, and the third is a promise about glasses after the drop, however many there are.

The arrangement bears one death among four. Could a cleverer one bear two?

Chapter VI

Two Against Two

No arrangement could. The reason is the wind again, now working against the four.

Suppose any two of the four might be killed. Can a pair of men who have heard nothing from the other pair wait for them? They cannot: the other two may be dead, and the third demand says the living must go. But the other two may be alive, with every bird between the pairs in the pines, and the wind will drop only after the living pair has gone.

More Than Half

If two of the four may be killed, no arrangement meets the three demands, even with glasses, even if the wind always drops, and even if all that is asked of the second demand is that four live men who sighted the same gate go to it.

Proof — that four men cannot bear two deaths
  1. Suppose some arrangement does. Divide the four into two pairs, Numbers 1 and 2 on one side, Numbers 3 and 4 on the other.
  2. The first night. All four sighted north. Numbers 3 and 4 are dead from dusk, and every bird between Numbers 1 and 2 is taken in within its glass. Two deaths are allowed, so by the third demand Numbers 1 and 2 go, within some number of glasses, say A. They go north. For if they went south, take a night the same for them in every respect, except that Numbers 3 and 4 are alive and sighted north, and the wind holds every bird from them for more than A glasses before dropping. Numbers 1 and 2 cannot tell that night from this one, so they go south, though all four were alive and sighted north.
  3. The second night. All four sighted south; Numbers 1 and 2 are dead from dusk; by the same argument Numbers 3 and 4 go south within some B glasses.
  4. The third night. Numbers 1 and 2 sighted north, Numbers 3 and 4 south. Nobody dies. Birds within each pair are taken in within their glass, and the wind holds every bird between the pairs for more than A and more than B glasses, and then drops. That is a night the wind allows.
  5. Numbers 1 and 2 cannot tell the third night from the first until more than A glasses have passed, and by then they have gone north. Numbers 3 and 4 cannot tell it from the second, and have gone south. Two men went to different gates, which contradicts the first demand.∎

Did the proof need the number four? Only that there are two sides, each small enough to be dead. Any number of men of whom half or more may die can be split in the same way. So the arrangement's ratio is the right one: it bears t deaths among N men when N is at least 2t + 1, and no arrangement bears more.

Is this a new argument? It is the camps' own. A dead man looks exactly like a slow bird*, and here the likeness is used once, for a fixed length of time, rather than for ever. That is the difference the wind's dropping makes. It cannot make silence mean death, but it limits how long the confusion can last, and an arrangement that waits for more than half can outlast it.

The mercenaries die but never lie. On the crown the same wind blows over men who may lie. What does it cost them?

Chapter VII

Liars and Seals

The mercenaries do not lie. On the crown some of the bandits do*, and on the later nights of the siege the same wind blows there, pinning the ravens between the posts for as long as it lasts. The paper answers for liars too, and its answer belongs to the crown, not to the camps. It is set down here because it is the same wind.

On the crown's first night there was no wind, and the flights were short. There, with sealed orders that no one can forge, the chief's word can be carried to any number of posts with any number of liars among them*. Does that survive the wind? It does not.

The table is the paper's first. The middle column is this book's: glasses that run alike and a wind that drops. In it, seals change nothing. Four posts bear one liar, sealed or not, and three posts bear none.

Why do seals stop helping? The wind again. On the crown's first night a post that heard nothing within the glass knew that nothing was sent, and could treat silence as a message. Under the wind a man can never wait for everyone, since one of them may be a man whose birds are in the pines and another a liar keeping quiet. He can wait only for all but t, and among the ones he hears from, t may be liars answering promptly. A seal stops a liar forging another man's word. It does not stop him answering promptly while an honest man's bird is still in a pine, and from his own mouth he may tell two men two different things.

Proof — that sealed or not, three posts cannot bear one liar under a wind that drops
  1. Take three posts, P, Q and R, of whom one may lie, and suppose some arrangement meets the three demands.
  2. The first night. All sighted hold. R is silent from dusk, which a liar may be. P and Q must act without him, within some number of glasses, and, as with the two pairs of men, they must choose hold.
  3. The second night. All sighted scatter. P is silent from dusk. Q and R choose scatter, within some number of glasses.
  4. The third night. P sighted hold, R scatter, and Q is the liar. To P he behaves exactly as on the first night, to R exactly as on the second, sealing every strip honestly with his own seal. The wind holds every bird between P and R until both have acted. P cannot tell this night from the first, nor R from the second, so they choose differently.∎

The crown has four men and bears one liar, exactly 3t + 1. On a windy night those four keep a loot book together, with seals and a chief's job that passes from man to man*. That is the crown's matter; this book is the camps'. The wind and this table belong to both.

A wind that drops is enough, with more than half alive. What has it not given the four?

Chapter VIII

What the Wind Does Not Say

Does anyone learn when the wind dropped? No. Nothing in the arrangement detects the calm. The four run the same turns in the wind and after it, and the proofs do the knowing on their behalf. A man who goes knows only that the two pledged birds came in time, not that the wind has dropped; a man who is still waiting cannot tell whether the wind is still up or his owner is dead.

Would the glasses alone have been enough? No. Glasses that run alike, turned together, with no bound on flights, would leave the four able to arrange nothing*. A glass that runs out with nothing at the perch tells a man only that a glass has run out. What makes the glasses count is the promise about the wind. Take either away, the common count or the dropping wind, and nothing can be arranged.

What if the glasses had not been turned together? The four here have one common moment, the last light on the crown. The paper also answers for men who have none, whose glasses may run at somewhat different rates. They can make a common count by birds alone: each man sends the others the count he has reached, and moves his own count on only when enough others show they have passed it, never so far that the dead or the slow could not catch up. This is a clock kept by ravens, and it needs the same more-than-half as the rest. Its proof is long, and the paper says so. The resiliency comes out the same.

What if nobody knew how long a still flight takes? Here every man knows the span of a still-air flight, and a glass is made to fit it. The paper also answers for men who know only that some such span exists. Then each round is given a little more time than the one before, so that in the end the rounds are longer than any flight, whatever it is, and from then on the arrangement works as before.

Is there a bound before the wind drops? No. The twenty glasses are counted from a moment nobody sees. How long the wind blows is up to the wind. On a night when it never drops, nobody can be made to go*. Nobody goes to a wrong gate either.

Does it matter that no bird is lost? It does, for stopping. The four can strike their tents and go because a going bird, once let go, always arrives. If birds could be lost while the wind blew, the paper observes that some man would have to go on sending for ever, however the arrangement was made, since no one could be sure his going bird had reached the others. On this hill that never arises.

Is the wind the only way out? No. There are other ways out at these tents, each changing one thing. Men who may each toss a coin, alone in his tent, go with probability one, on no night anyone can name*. Men each given a slate that in the end chalks the dead, and in the end spares one living man, can agree while a majority is alive*. Here nothing is given to the men at all; the change is in the wind. The three answers are different in kind. The coin needs no promise about time and gives none; the slate is granted, not built; the wind is a fact about the hill, and the arrangement makes use of it without ever observing it. The turn that passes round the tents in number order, fixed by the count and not by anyone's command, is also how men with such slates take turns to propose*.

What follows from going? Nothing. The four go in at one gate. What happens at the gate is not the subject of this book or any other: no assault is made, no wall is carried, and nobody surrenders.

The ideas, plainly

Fischer, Lynch and Paterson showed that consensus is impossible in an asynchronous system if even one process may crash, because a crashed process cannot be told from a slow one. Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer asked how much synchrony is enough. Their answer is partial synchrony: either a bound on message delay exists but is unknown, or it is known but holds only from some unknown global stabilization time (GST) on.

In both models consensus is possible, and they determine exactly how many faults can be tolerated: with crash or omission faults, more than half the processes must be correct (N ≥ 2t + 1); with Byzantine faults, more than two thirds (N ≥ 3t + 1), whether or not messages are signed. The lower bounds are partition arguments: if half may fail, the network can delay every message between two halves until each has decided alone.

The protocols run in rounds grouped into phases, each phase belonging to one process in rotation. A phase's owner proposes a value that enough processes find acceptable; processes lock it and acknowledge; the owner decides on enough acknowledgments; a lock-release round lets a later lock override an earlier one. Safety holds under any delay, because the processes that locked a decided value can never all be outvoted. Termination follows once delays are bounded, because the first phase owned by a correct process after stabilization succeeds.

The separation between safety in all conditions and progress in good conditions, and rotating phases with locks carried forward, became the shape of later consensus protocols.
