Agreeing When Messages Run Late

When messages can be delayed without limit, no protocol can guarantee that a group of computers will ever reach agreement. Yet real networks behave well most of the time: periods of chaos eventually give way to calm. Can we design a protocol that never makes a mistake no matter how late messages run, and is guaranteed to reach a decision once messages start arriving on time again—even though no computer can tell when the calm has arrived?

To see how, we return to the four Greek mercenaries camped at the foot of Mount Phyle on a stormy night when high winds delay their ravens for hours, and watch how they manage to agree whether to attack or hold once the wind finally drops.

After Cynthia Dwork, Nancy Lynch and Larry Stockmeyer, Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony, 1988

First, in machine terms

Safe always, live once the network settles

Suppose a group of computers must agree on one of two values, while messages between them can be delayed and one computer might crash at any moment.

If messages can be delayed indefinitely, no protocol can guarantee that the machines will ever reach a decision, because a machine that has crashed is indistinguishable from one whose messages are merely running late. Real networks, however, are well-behaved most of the time. Even when congestion or routing glitches delay packets for a while, the network eventually calms down. Suppose that up until some unknown moment, messages can take arbitrarily long, and from that moment on (Global Stabilization Time, or GST), every message arrives within a known time bound. This middle ground between pure chaos and strict timing is called partial synchrony.

  • Agreement under partial synchrony (Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer, 1988): we can build a protocol that never allows two machines to decide differently, no matter how badly messages are delayed, and that guarantees every working machine decides soon after message delays settle—even though no machine can tell when that moment has arrived.
  • A working majority is required: more than half of the machines must remain working. The protocol relies on this majority, and no protocol can succeed with fewer.

This design cleanly separates safety from the network's mood: bad timing can delay a decision, but can never cause a wrong one. We tell the story through four mercenaries, their ravens, and a night wind that eventually dies down.

The foot of Mount Phyle

Four tents, out of sight of one another

Four mercenaries are camped at the foot of Mount Phyle, one to a tent, hidden from one another by the folds of the hillside and communicating only by messenger raven.* Before dawn, they must either all attack the hill together, or all hold their positions.

Only ravensEvery raven eventually reaches its destination, and delivers its message strip exactly as written.
Nobody liesThe mercenaries are honest comrades—none of them ever tampers with a message or sends a false report.
One may dieAt most one of the four might be killed during the night, leaving his tent permanently silent. Nobody actually dies in tonight's story, but their plan must be prepared for the possibility that one of them might.
What the four want

All attack, or all hold

The four mercenaries must act as one: an assault by only part of the company will be overpowered on the slope, while holding with a man missing leaves a path down the hill unguarded.

At dusk, each mercenary scouted the section of the hill above his tent and formed an initial judgment—a sighting—of either attack or hold. Because they may have seen different things, they must exchange ravens to settle on a single common plan. A mercenary commits when he finally steps out of his tent—either to strike camp and climb the slope with the others, or to take up his post in the gully and hold. Once a man commits, his choice is final; whatever he privately thought inside his tent beforehand no longer matters. Any arrangement they use must satisfy three demands.

First demand

No two commit differently

Never does one man go out to attack while another stays to hold (consistency).

Second demand

A shared sighting wins

If all four men began with the same sighting at dusk, that is the plan they must commit to (unanimity).

Third demand

Every living man commits

Every man who has not been killed eventually steps out of his tent with a decision (termination).

As we saw in The Limits of Agreement, if ravens can be delayed without any bound all night long, no arrangement can guarantee all three demands at once.* The trouble comes from two facts working together: there is no upper limit on how long a raven can take, and a dead man's silence looks identical to a slow bird. If there were a known maximum flight time from the very start of the night, silence lasting longer than that limit would prove a comrade was dead.*

Tonight, the mercenaries do not have a guaranteed flight time from dusk—but they have something just strong enough, and it comes from the very force that delays their birds.

The only promise

The wind keeps the ravens in the folds

What makes the ravens late is the mountain wind. While it howls through the gullies, a raven released from a tent is forced down into the pines among the wild crows to wait out the gusts—for an hour or for most of the night, with no telling how long. Even after the wind dies down, a bird that took shelter earlier may linger in the branches as long as it pleases.

A bird released into still air, however, flies straight to its perch. The one promise of these nights is that the wind always drops eventually, and once it drops, it stays down for the rest of the night. From that moment on, every raven released into the calm air arrives within a short, predictable span of time that all four men know. That single promise is the entire difference between an impossible night and a solvable one. In the interactive below, Tent 1 releases a bird to Tent 2 during each time span. Notice that the mercenaries cannot tell when the wind has dropped—and watching the birds above does not reveal it either. A brief lull outside one tent says nothing about the ravines two ridges over, where gusts may still be pinning a raven in the pines. Worse, a temporary lull that is about to be broken by a fresh gale looks identical to the true end of the wind. No man ever knows for sure that the wind has died down across the whole mountain. Each man knows only that it eventually will—and without that guarantee, agreement would be impossible.*

So the four need a two-part guarantee: their arrangement must never let two men commit to different plans no matter how long the wind rages, and it must bring every living man to a decision soon after the air turns still. Because the promise speaks of a known span of time, each man alone in his tent first needs a way to measure that span.

A count every man shares

Glasses and late birds

Each mercenary measures time with sand. Every tent has an identical sandglass, and at the exact moment the last direct sunlight vanished from the summit of Mount Phyle—a sight visible from all four tents at once—every man turned his glass for the first time.

The instant a sandglass runs empty, its owner flips it again and adds one to his count of glasses (rounds). Because all four glasses were started together at sunset and run at the same rate, all four men are always on the same glass count. Real sandglasses drift slightly over a night, of course, but by far less than a raven's flight, which is close enough. That shared sunset does not violate the rules of the camp because no man sent it—nature provided one shared starting mark at dusk, and nothing like it will happen again until dawn. (The bandits up on the summit cannot use this trick: their posts face different slopes where the sun vanishes at different times, and their chief inside the ring wall sees only the sky overhead.)

One glass is sized so that a man has time to write and tie message strips for all four tents, release his ravens, and—if the air is still—have every raven reach its destination before the sand runs out. That is what the known flight time buys: once the wind has dropped, any bird released at the start of a glass is guaranteed to arrive before that same glass ends. What about birds delayed by the wind that straggle in during a later glass? A simple rule takes care of them.

Every strip

Carries its glass

Whenever a man writes a message strip, he marks it with the number of the glass in which he sent it.

A late bird

Is read, and set aside

If a raven arrives during a later glass than the one written on its strip, its message is ignored—except for learning which sightings were seen at dusk, its vote or call counts for nothing.

One exception

The parting bird

One special message—a parting bird announcing that a comrade has already committed—is accepted whenever it arrives. We will meet it shortly.

Setting late birds aside makes every glass completely self-contained. What a man does during glass 7 depends only on the birds sent and received during glass 7; stragglers from glass 3 can never arrive out of nowhere and muddle a later step. While the wind blows, of course, a glass's birds may arrive late and be set aside—which simply means that during windy glasses, some messages fail to show up in time.

Notice that the sandglasses help only because the wind eventually drops. Without that promise, flipping sandglasses all night would buy the mercenaries nothing at all:* if flights have no time bound, a glass that runs out with no bird at the perch tells a man nothing about whether his comrade is dead or the bird is merely slow. The glasses get their power from the eventual calm, even though no man can see when that calm begins.

Now that all four men share a synchronized count of glasses and a clean rule for late birds, how do they use those glasses to reach agreement?

The hill, as this telling uses it, stated in the paper's terms

(1) While the wind blows, a bird may take any amount of time, though no bird is ever permanently lost.
(2) The wind drops at some moment and stays down. From then on, any bird released at the start of a glass reaches its perch within that same glass. A bird released before the wind dropped may still be sitting in the pines.
(3) No man knows when the wind has dropped.
(4) The glasses were started together at sunset and run at the same rate, so every man shares the same glass count.
(5) Late birds: any message strip arriving in a later glass than the one in which it was written is ignored, except for parting birds (and the piggybacked set of initial sightings).
(6) At most one of the four mercenaries may be killed. No one lies.

Who may propose

Turns and pledges

To keep from talking over one another, the four men group their glasses into blocks of four. Each block of four glasses is one turn (phase): glasses 1 to 4 make turn one, glasses 5 to 8 make turn two, and so on through the night.

Each turn belongs to a single mercenary, rotating in tent order: turn one belongs to Tent 1, turn two to Tent 2, turn three to Tent 3, turn four to Tent 4, turn five back to Tent 1, and so on. That mercenary is the turn's owner (coordinator or proposer). Being the owner does not make him a commander: nobody elected him, and the rotation hands a turn to a dead man's tent just the same as a living one's. Even in his own turn, the owner cannot simply order what he likes—he may only propose a plan that at least three men (himself included) have just reported they are free to accept. When the fourth glass of the turn runs out, the turn ends and passes to the next tent, regardless of whether anything was settled. Inside his tent, each man tracks two pieces of state, governed by one rule for when an answer is acceptable.

What each man keeps

The sighted answers

The set of dusk sightings this man knows somebody in the camp saw (proper values). It begins with his own sighting; every strip he sends includes his list, and he adds any sighting he learns of from incoming strips. If all four saw hold at dusk, only hold ever appears on anyone's list.

What each man keeps

Pledges

A strip tied to his tent pole naming an answer and a turn—such as hold, turn five (a lock). Tying that pledge means the owner of turn five might commit to hold, so this tent promises not to help a later turn switch to attack unless the pledge is first untied. In windy weather, a man can end up holding pledges on both answers at once.

A word for it

To accept

A man is free to accept an answer only if he currently holds no pledge on the opposite answer.

Why does a call require three men who can accept, while committing requires only two pledges? Both numbers come from having four men when at most one may die. Because one comrade might be dead, the owner can never wait to hear from all four—three is the most he can count on in the asking glass. And once two men tie pledges for the owner's call in the answering glass, those two pledges are enough to lock out the opposite answer: out of four men in total, if two hold pledges for hold, at most two men are left without a pledge for hold—one short of the three needed for any later owner to call attack!

Notice too that the protocol does not care whether attack or hold is wiser. If three men can accept both answers, the owner is free to call either one. And the list of sighted answers automatically guarantees the second demand (unanimity): an owner can only call an answer that three men reported from their sighted lists, and an answer only enters those lists if someone actually saw it at dusk. If all four saw hold, attack never enters any list and can never be called.

Turn five ran smoothly because the air was still. While the wind blows, however, ravens arrive late and get set aside, leaving half-finished turns and scattered pledges behind. Next we must check why those windy turns can never trick two men into committing to opposite plans.

The four glasses of a turn, stated in the paper's terms

The asking glass. Every man (including the turn's owner) sends the owner the list of answers he can currently accept that also appear in his list of sighted answers.
The calling glass. If at least three men (the owner possibly among them) named the same answer in the asking glass, the owner calls it by sending pledge to hold, turn five (for instance) to every tent, including his own. If both answers qualify, he calls either one; if neither gets three, he calls nothing.
The answering glass. Any man who receives the owner's call during the calling glass ties a pledge for that answer and turn to his tent pole, unties any older pledge he held on that same answer, and sends pledged back to the owner. Any pledge he holds on the other answer stays tied for now. If the owner receives pledged from at least two men (including himself) during the answering glass, the owner commits to the called answer.
The untying glass. Every man broadcasts all the pledges currently tied to his tent pole (both answer and turn number) to every tent. If a man holds a pledge on one answer from turn h and learns of a pledge on the opposite answer from a turn h' that is at least as late (h' ≥ h), he unties his older pledge.

Safety asks nothing of the wind

Never two answers, in any wind

Because no mercenary can tell when the wind has dropped, the first demand—that no two men ever commit to opposite plans—cannot depend on still air. It must hold unconditionally, even in the worst gale.

Why does it hold? The proof rests on two simple facts. First, in any single turn, only that turn's owner can make a call, and he calls at most one answer—so two men can never tie pledges for opposite answers carrying the same turn number. Second, consider the earliest turn in which anyone commits—say Tent 1 commits to hold in turn five. To commit, Tent 1 had to receive pledged from at least two men in the answering glass, which means at least two men tied hold, turn five to their poles. Could either of those two men ever untie that pledge? By the untying rule, a man only unties hold, turn five if he hears of a pledge for attack from turn five or later. By our first fact, there is no pledge for attack from turn five. And for anyone to tie a pledge for attack in a later turn—say turn eight—its owner would first need three men in turn eight's asking glass who hold no pledge for hold. Since two of the four men are still holding hold, turn five, at most two men in the entire camp are free to accept attack. Turn eight's owner can never gather three, so attack can never be called!

Never Two Answers

However long the wind blows and however badly ravens are delayed, no two men ever commit to different answers. Moreover, if all four sighted the same answer at dusk, no man ever commits to the other.

Which specific men tied the pledge does not matter—only how many. An owner commits on at least two pledged birds, and any later call for the opposite answer requires three men free to accept it.

Notice that the wind plays no part in this safety argument. All the wind can do is delay ravens so that they arrive in a later glass and get set aside. That can prevent a call from reaching enough tents, or prevent pledged birds from reaching the owner in time—but failing to commit is always safe. And once any owner does receive two pledged birds in time, those two pledges are already tied on their tent poles, forming an unbreakable wall against the opposite answer.

What, then, is the untying glass for? Suppose in an earlier windy turn, the owner called attack, one man received the call and tied attack, turn two, and the other ravens were blown into the pines so nobody committed. Without a way to untie old pledges, that lone pledge from a failed turn would sit on its pole all night, forever forbidding that man from accepting hold. Worse, another man might similarly hold a lone pledge for hold from turn three, deadlocking the camp! The untying glass lets a newer pledge override an older one—clearing away debris from aborted turns—while still ensuring that once two men hold pledges from a turn where someone committed, no opposite call can ever be made to untie them.

So no man ever commits to a wrong answer, in any wind. Of course, an arrangement where everybody stayed in their tents forever would also never make a mistake. To satisfy the third demand, we must now see why every living man is guaranteed to commit once the wind drops.

Proof · that no two men commit to different answers, in any wind

Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.1(a). However messages are delayed, no two processors decide different values (consistency), and if all four started with the same value no processor decides another (strong unanimity).

  1. One answer per turn. Only a turn's owner calls, and the owner calls at most one answer in it. A pledge for turn k is tied only on taking in the call of turn k. So all pledges for turn k are on one answer.
  2. Suppose some man commits, and let turn k be the earliest turn in which an owner committed; say that owner committed to hold. The owner had pledged from two men, so two men tied hold, turn k. Men who commit on a parting bird (described below) commit to an answer some owner committed to earlier, so it is enough to show that no owner in a later turn commits to attack.
  3. The two keep a pledge to hold. Suppose not, and take the first untying glass in which one of the two unties a pledge to hold for turn k or later without having a later one. That man unties it on learning of a pledge to attack for some turn h at least as late. By step 1, h is not k, so h is later than k, and no later than this untying glass.
  4. A pledge to attack for turn h means attack was called in turn h, so three men could accept attack in turn h's asking glass: three men with no pledge to hold. But both of the two had a pledge to hold from turn k until this untying glass, which is no earlier than turn h's own. There are four men; at most two had no pledge to hold. This contradicts the three.
  5. So from turn k on, two men always have a pledge to hold, or have committed to hold and send nothing. In no later asking glass can three men name attack, attack is never called, no man ties a pledge to attack for a later turn, and no owner commits to attack.

The third demand

Committing once the wind drops

When a mercenary commits, he steps out of his tent to take his place on the slope or in the gully, leaving his ravens and sandglass behind. If he simply vanished without a word, the comrades left behind might lack thethree voices needed to complete a turn of their own. So before any man steps out of his tent, he releases one final set of ravens to the other three tents—his parting birds: parting, hold.

Unlike ordinary messages, a parting bird carries no glass number and is never set aside, no matter when it lands. Any mercenary who receives a parting bird immediately releases parting birds of his own to all the other tents and commits to the same answer. Why does every man relay a full set of parting birds, rather than relying on the first man's birds alone? Because the first man might be killed halfway through releasing his birds, after sending to one comrade but not the others. Having every recipient relay a full set guarantees that as long as at most one man dies—and because no raven is ever permanently lost—the parting birds will reach every living man, and all of them will commit. That reduces the entire third demand to a single question: once the wind drops, why is at least one man guaranteed to commit?

While the wind is blowing, a turn can stall in any of three places:

First place

The asking birds are in the pines

The turn's owner hears from only one or two men in the asking glass, and cannot make a call.

Second place

The call is late

The owner makes a call, but the wind delays his birds so that at most one man receives it in the calling glass.

Third place

The pledged birds are late

Several men receive the call and tie pledges, but their pledged replies arrive after the answering glass ends and are set aside.

Those stalled turns can leave conflicting pledges scattered across the camp—say Tent 2 holding an older pledge for attack and Tent 1 holding a newer pledge for hold. Now watch what happens as soon as the wind drops. In the very first untying glass that runs entirely in still air, every living man's broadcast reaches every other living man before the sand runs out. Every man sees the full set of active pledges across the camp, and anyone holding an older pledge on the losing answer unties it in favor of the latest turn's pledge. By the end of that single calm untying glass, all remaining pledges among the living men point to at most one answer—and every living man also shares the exact same list of sighted answers.

Now follow the very next turn owned by a living man. In its asking glass, all the living men (at least three of them) share the same sighted list, and since any remaining pledges are on at most one answer, there is at least one sighted answer that all of them are free to accept. All three report that answer to the owner in time; the owner calls it in the calling glass; all three receive the call and tie pledges in the answering glass; and their pledged birds fly back in still air before the glass runs out. The owner commits!

Committing After the Wind

Once the wind drops, every man who is not killed eventually commits. If nobody had yet committed when the wind dropped, everyone commits within at most twenty glasses (five turns) after the drop. If someone had already committed while the wind was blowing, the others commit as soon as his parting birds finish making their way out of the pines.

The timeline traces two possibilities after the wind drops: one where all four men are alive, and one where the owner of the first full turn after the untying glass happens to be dead, so the camp finishes on the very next turn.

Why does the paper state a bound of twenty glasses (five turns of four glasses), while our timeline finishes in at most sixteen? It is simply a looser, rounder bound in the paper's proof; nothing essential hangs on the difference. One subtle distinction does matter, though: in the paper's basic protocol, a machine that decides keeps running the rounds forever, whereas on our hill a mercenary who commits leaves his tent. If one mercenary committed while the wind was still blowing and another was killed, the two men left in their tents could never gather three voices for a call on their own—so they rely on the parting birds released before the first man left, which are guaranteed to arrive eventually, though no fixed count of glasses can say when they will leave the pines.

Even when nobody commits early, no mercenary can ever look at his sandglass at glass 40 and say, “By now we are guaranteed to have committed.” The twenty-glass countdown starts from the moment the wind drops, and because no man can see that moment, no man knows which glass started the countdown.

Our arrangement tolerates one death among four mercenaries. Could a cleverer arrangement tolerate two deaths out of four?

Where the allegory leaks

A real night ends at dawn, whereas the paper's rounds can continue indefinitely: the formal model allows the wind to blow for an arbitrarily large number of rounds before dropping, and promises a decision within a fixed number of rounds after GST. On a real mountain, if the wind only dropped five minutes before sunrise, dawn might arrive before the twenty glasses finished. In the mathematical model there is no dawn: safety holds at every round forever, and termination holds within a bounded number of rounds after GST, however late GST occurs.

If none is pledged, every live man can accept every answer on the list. Either way at least three men name a common answer, and the owner hears them all within the glass and calls it.
  7. Every live man takes in the call within the calling glass, pledges and sends pledged; the owner takes in at least two within the answering glass and commits, sending parting birds, within sixteen glasses of the wind's dropping.
  8. By the parting birds, every man left alive then commits, and by Never Two Answers they all commit to the same answer.∎
What the arrangement cannot bear

Two against two

No arrangement—no matter how clever—can tolerate two deaths out of four on a windy hill. Once again, the culprit is the wind, which can temporarily split the camp in half.

Suppose up to two men might die during the night. Then if Tents 1 and 2 hear nothing at all from Tents 3 and 4, they cannot wait forever—for all they know, Numbers 3 and 4 are dead, and the third demand requires the living to commit eventually. Likewise, if Tents 3 and 4 hear nothing from Tents 1 and 2, they too must eventually commit on their own. Now imagine a third night on which nobody is dead, but the wind blows between the two pairs of tents, holding every raven that crosses between {1, 2} and {3, 4} in the pines until after both pairs have given up waiting and committed—and only then does the wind drop! More Than Half

If two of the four men may die, no arrangement can meet all three demands—even with synchronized sandglasses, even though the wind always drops eventually, and even under the weakest unanimity rule (that when all four live and see the same sighting, they must commit to it).

On the third night, nobody died and the wind eventually dropped, just as promised. Yet because the wind lasted longer than the time each pair could afford to wait, Tents 1 and 2 experienced a night identical to Night 1 (where 3 and 4 were dead and everyone saw hold), while Tents 3 and 4 experienced a night identical to Night 2 (where 1 and 2 were dead and everyone saw attack). One pair committed to hold and the other to attack! This partition argument works against any group where half or more might fail: to survive t deaths under partial synchrony, a camp must have at least 2t + 1 members—a strict majority of living men.

Notice how this echoes the fundamental dilemma of the camp: a dead man looks just like a slow bird.* The difference is that in a purely asynchronous night, the wind never has to stop, whereas here the wind need only hold the cross-camp birds until both halves have committed. Only by requiring every decision to involve more than half the camp can we ensure that no two groups can ever decide in isolation from one another.

At the foot of the hill, men may die, but they never lie. Up on the summit, the same wind blows across the bandits' fortress—where a comrade may be a traitor. How many men does agreement require when the wind blows among liars?

The same wind, on the crown

Liars and seals

Up on the crown of Mount Phyle, the bandits face traitors in their own ranks,* and on windy nights the same gales sweep the summit, pinning their messenger rats in their burrows between the posts for hours at a time.

In Agreeing Among Liars, when there was no wind and every rat arrived on schedule, unforgeable wax seals were magic: they allowed any number of posts to agree despite any number of traitors.* Once the wind blows, however, wax seals lose that magic! Look at the middle wind setting—a wind that drops—when men may lie. Whether the men use unforgeable wax seals or not, tolerating one liar requires four posts; three posts can never tolerate even a single liar!

Why do wax seals stop reducing the number of posts needed as soon as the wind blows? On a calm night with guaranteed delivery times, silence is informative: if a post fails to relay a sealed message before the round ends, everyone knows that post is acting dishonestly. Under the wind, however, nobody can wait for all four posts, because one post might be a traitor staying silent. With three posts (P, Q, R) and one possible traitor, any two posts must be able to decide without waiting for the third. Then a traitor Q can play a double game: to P, Q claims to have seen hold and pretends R is silent; to R, Q claims to have seen scatter and pretends P is silent—stamping every message honestly with Q's own genuine seal! Meanwhile, the wind delays all messages directly between P and R until P commits to hold and R commits to scatter. A wax seal stops a liar from forging someone else's signature, but it cannot stop a liar from signing two contradictory stories of his own while an honest comrade's message is stuck in the wind.

That is why all the later books set on the summit—Keeping Order Among Liars and Changing Leaders Among Liars—use four posts to tolerate one traitor under the wind.*

Questions left over

What the wind does not say

The key to partial synchrony is that the protocol relies on the wind dropping without ever needing to detect that it has dropped. Eight natural questions follow from that insight.

Does anyone learn when the wind dropped?

No. Nothing in the protocol detects when the air has calmed, so no mercenary ever knows when GST has passed. They simply run the same four-glass turns during the wind and after it. When an owner finally receives two pledged birds in time and commits, he does not know whether the whole mountain is calm or whether his birds merely caught a lucky break—and a man still waiting in his tent cannot tell whether the wind is still blowing or the current turn's owner is dead.

Would the glasses alone have been enough?

No. Synchronized sandglasses without any bound on flight times leave the mercenaries right back in the impossibility of The Limits of Agreement.* If flights have no bound, a glass that runs out with no bird at the perch tells a man only that sand has fallen through a glass. Both ingredients are required: a way to measure rounds and an eventual bound on message delay.

What if the glasses had not been started together?

Here the four men shared sunset on the summit as a starting mark, and their glasses ran at the same rate. The paper also solves the harder case where processors start at different times and their local clocks drift at bounded rates. They build a shared round counter using messages alone: each processor broadcasts its current count and advances only when a majority have reached that step—a distributed clock kept by ravens.

What if nobody knew how long a still flight takes?

Here the men know how long a flight takes in still air, and size their sandglasses accordingly. The paper also solves the dual model of partial synchrony, where the air is calm from the start (a fixed bound Δ exists) but nobody knows how large Δ is! The fix is simple: make each round slightly longer than the previous one, so that eventually the rounds grow long enough to outlast any flight, and from that point on the protocol succeeds just as before.

Is there a bound before the wind drops?

None at all. The twenty-glass guarantee is measured from the unknown moment the wind drops, and how long the wind blows before then is entirely up to the weather. On a hypothetical night where the wind never dropped, nobody could be guaranteed to commit*—yet even then, safety would hold and nobody would ever commit to a conflicting plan.

Does it matter that no bird is lost?

It matters if men want to stop and leave their tents. Because parting birds are never lost, a mercenary who sends them and steps out of his tent knows they will eventually arrive. As the paper notes, if messages could be permanently dropped before GST, no deciding machine could ever safely halt, because it could never be sure its comrades had heard the decision.

Is the wind the only way out?

No—two other books at the foot of Mount Phyle escape the impossibility of agreement in different ways. If each man can flip a coin inside his tent (Agreeing by Chance), they reach agreement with probability 1 without needing the wind to drop.* And if each man has a failure-detector slate that eventually marks the dead while sparing at least one living comrade (Telling the Dead from the Slow), they can agree using the same rotating turn ownership we used here.*

What follows from committing?

In the story, once they commit, the four mercenaries either climb the slope together or hold the gullies together. What happens in the battle afterward lies outside the consensus problem: the algorithm's sole job is to ensure they act as one.

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Cynthia Dwork, Nancy Lynch and Larry Stockmeyer, Consensus in the Presence of Partial Synchrony. Journal of the ACM 35, 2 (April 1988), 288–323. Introduces the two models of partially synchronous communication and processors, the basic round model and Algorithms 1–3, the lower bounds on fault tolerance (Theorems 4.3 and 4.4), and distributed clock synchronization under partial synchrony.

The ideas. Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson proved that deterministic consensus is impossible in a completely asynchronous system if even a single process can crash, because a crashed process cannot be distinguished from a slow one. Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer asked how much timing synchrony is actually needed to bridge the gap. Their answer is partial synchrony: either a fixed upper bound Δ on message delay exists but is unknown to the processes, or the bound Δ is known in advance but holds only from some unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST) onward.

In both models, consensus becomes possible, and the paper establishes the exact fault-tolerance thresholds: with crash or omission faults, a strict majority of processes must remain correct (N ≥ 2t + 1); with Byzantine faults, more than two-thirds must remain correct (N ≥ 3t + 1), even when messages carry unforgeable cryptographic signatures. Both lower bounds rely on partition arguments: if too many processes can fail, the network can delay messages between two groups until each group is forced to decide on its own.

The protocols organize rounds into four-round phases, rotating phase ownership among the processes in turn. In each phase, the owner proposes a value that enough processes report as acceptable; processes lock that value and send acknowledgments; the owner decides upon receiving enough acknowledgments; and a lock-release round allows newer locks to supersede older ones from incomplete phases. Safety holds regardless of message delays because once a value is decided, the processes holding locks on it prevent any opposite value from gathering the quorum needed for a new proposal. Termination follows once message delays stabilize because the next phase owned by a correct process after GST is guaranteed to complete.

This clean separation—guaranteeing safety under arbitrary network delays while relying on eventual synchrony only for liveness, using rotating proposers and locks carried across phases—became the blueprint for modern consensus protocols from Paxos and Raft to Tendermint and HotStuff.
