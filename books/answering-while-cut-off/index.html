Answering While Cut Off

When one part of a system is cut off from the rest, should it keep answering and risk being wrong, or stay silent until it can be sure? Can it ever have both?

Bandits are reported on both stretches of the ring road beside the Vine, and no runner will go. The Vine's counter is still open and buyers still come to it. Each buyer must be answered, and every answer must fit the one order of sales the three counters owe their buyers. While the road is shut the Vine cannot do both, and no arrangement the houses could make would let it. What the houses can choose is which to give up, and for how long.

Chapter I

The Closed Road

The three trading houses of Arche, the Dolphin on the western shelf, the Vine on the northern terraces and the Anchor in the eastern bay, sit round the foot of Mount Phyle, and the mountain stands between every pair of them. The only way word passes from one house to another is the ring road: two single-file cuttings in the cliff, the upper walked sunwise and the lower against the sun, on which a runner carrying a slip can neither pass nor meet anyone. By the law of the road a slip goes by the direct stretch between two houses and never the long way round. There are three stretches: the Dolphin to the Vine, the Vine to the Anchor, and the Anchor to the Dolphin along the south.

The winter trade opens in gales. Spray washes over the cliff road, a runner may shelter under a ledge for a day or five, and while the gales last no one can say how long he will take. This book is set later in the season. The gales are over and the counters are open. The low cloud still hides the sun, so the sundials are still no use, but the cliff road is dry and the runners keep their pace. Once the storm has broken, a runner on any stretch reaches the far gate within three turns of the customs sandglass. The houses call that span a crossing, and they reckon with it: a slip sent down an open stretch is at the far gate within one crossing, or something is wrong with the road.

Sometimes something is. Every few seasons word comes to the runners that bandits are about on the road, and a stretch with bandits reported on it is a closed road. No runner will set out on it from either end, and no house asks one to. Nothing more happens than that. No bandit is seen, no runner is stopped, and no one on the coast can say how many there are, where they came from or whether they have anything to do with the band that holds the crown. The road is closed because of what is reported, and it opens again when the reports stop.

This book is about one such closing: bandits reported on both stretches beside the Vine, the Dolphin–Vine and the Vine–Anchor, and none on the south stretch. Both matter. With one stretch closed, the Vine could still send to the far house by giving its slip to the third house to pass on. With both closed, nothing the Vine writes reaches the Dolphin or the Anchor, and nothing they write reaches the Vine. The Dolphin and the Anchor still have the south stretch between them and trade with each other as before. The Vine is cut off.

A slip for a closed stretch is not carried at all. It does not wait at the gate to go when the road opens, since no one can say when that will be and business written for today is no use in a week; if the house still wants the thing said once the road is open, it writes a fresh slip. Nor is there a way round. The summit is held by the siege and no beacon of the coast is lit there. Reefs and tide-races stop small boats coasting from one cove to the next. The camps carry nothing between houses. And the goatherd who crosses the mountain by the smugglers' tracks* may come or may not, may take two hours or two days, and cannot be sent for. No house counts on him.

The Vine has not failed. Its gate is open, its clerks are at their books and its storehouse is full. Above all, buyers still come to its counter, from the terraces and the villages inland, and they do not care that the road is closed. They want to know what the house can sell them. The question of this book is what the Vine should tell them.

Chapter II

What a Counter Owes

Each house has a counter in its front room, where a buyer asks for jars and is told what the house can do. The three counters sell from one stock. When a shipload of Chian wine lands at the Anchor, a buyer at the Vine may have a jar of it as readily as a buyer on the Anchor's own quay, and it goes round to him by cart.

Chian comes in small cargoes and dear, so the houses keep it jar by jar. Each jar has its mark scratched on the shoulder and its own line in the book, and against the mark is written the name of the buyer it is promised to, or nothing, if it is still free. A buyer does one of two things at a counter. He asks after a jar, whether it is free or whose it is; or he buys it, and his name is written on its line. Either way he asks, and then he is answered.

The counters owe their buyers two things, and the houses have held to both for as long as the counters have stood.

The first is the rule of the counters. Each house keeps its own book, and a buyer at one counter cannot see the others. But the answers across all three must read as though there were one book and one clerk, taking the buyers one at a time. Put precisely:

The rule of the counters All the purchases and questions at all three counters can be set in a single order, the same for every counter, such that:
(1) every answer is the one a single clerk with a single book would have given, taking them in that order;
(2) each takes its place in the order at some moment between the buyer's asking and his answer; so that
(3) anything answered before another was asked comes before it.

The third clause is the one this book turns on. If a buyer at the Anchor has bought the last jar of Chian and been told it is his, and afterwards a buyer at the Vine asks whether that jar is free, the rule requires the answer no, it is promised. It does not matter how far apart the two counters are or whether any slip has passed between them.

Before and afterwards here are judged by the sun. No one on Arche can read it in the trading season, when the cloud lies low, and no house can learn what was done at another counter except by slip. The rule is not something a clerk checks. It is what the houses owe their buyers: that no one who could see all three counters at once, as the sun does, and no buyer who heard from over the mountain what was sold at another counter, would ever catch them out. It says what the houses must do, not how they could know they have done it.

The second thing the counters owe is simpler. Every buyer is answered. A buyer who asks at an open counter is told, sooner or later, whether the jar is free or that it is his. The houses set no limit on how long the answer may take; they ask only that it come. What does not count as an answer is being asked to wait until the road opens, since the road may stay closed for as long as the reports go on, and a buyer kept waiting for that has not been answered. A counter that stays shut has not answered anyone. And a counter that says “promised” of every jar, to be safe, has given an answer, but a wrong one whenever the jar is free: it has kept the second duty by breaking the first.

The ring road, as this book uses it (1) An open stretch: a slip arrives within one crossing, a known time. A clerk answers a slip within a known time of his own.
(2) A closed stretch: a slip for it is never carried. Any stretch may close, for as long as the reports last.
(3) Sandglasses: each house can measure how long it has waited. The glasses run alike at every house, but there is no hour: no house can tell what time it is at another.

This is the road of fair weather with bandits on it, not the road of the winter gales. In a gale no one knows how long a crossing will take, and a runner held under a ledge cannot be told from a road that is shut. Here the crossing has a bound and the houses know it. Chapter VIII returns to the gale.

Chapter III

Breweros' Claim

Breweros, who kept the counter at the Vine, was the first to say out loud what every clerk on the island half knew. He said it to a gathering of the three houses' clerks, as a claim and not a proof: of three things, answering by the rule, answering every buyer, and trading on a road that can close, a house may have any two, but not all three.

He named the choices by what each gives up, and for each he could point to something the houses already did.

Give upWhat it looks like on ArcheBreweros' way of doing it
The closed roadAll the selling done in one place, where no road runs between the clerks. A single house selling from a single book keeps the rule and answers everyone, because there is nothing to be cut off from.One house, one book.
Answering every buyerThe side that is cut off stops selling until the road is open. The Dolphin and the Anchor, the larger side, go on; the Vine's counter waits.Hold the jars back; shut the smaller side.
The ruleEvery counter answers from its own book, which may be behind. What went wrong is put right when the road opens.Answer from what you hold; settle the differences afterwards.

Breweros did not think the houses should always make the same choice. The three pulled in different directions and each was useful somewhere; he called it a spectrum. He also asked less than the houses do. He was content if almost every buyer was answered. The proof below asks for every buyer, for simplicity, and its authors noted that asking for almost every buyer changes nothing when the road can close anywhere, for any length of time.

Two scribes, Gilbertos of the Dolphin and Lynchaia of the Anchor, took the claim and made it exact. They fixed the rule as Chapter II states it, fixed what counts as an answer, and let the road close on any stretch for any length of time. Then they proved it.

Chapter IV

The Last Jar

The argument is short, and it is worth following with one jar.

One jar of Chian is left, and its line is empty in every house's book. Both stretches beside the Vine are closed. At the Anchor a buyer, Nikias, buys the jar; his name is written on its line and he is told it is his. The Anchor writes a slip for the Vine to say so, but no runner will take it. A day later, by the Vine's sandglass, a buyer at the Vine asks whether the jar is free.

The Vine must answer, since every buyer is answered. What can it go on? Its own book, whose line for the jar is empty, and everything that has reached it by slip, which is nothing, because nothing has reached it since the road closed.

Now take a second day on Arche, the same in every respect except that no one at the Anchor buys anything. The line is empty, the road is closed, and a day after the closing a buyer at the Vine asks whether the jar is free. Here the right answer is free, and the Vine must give it.

To the Vine, the two days are the same. Its book is the same, its sandglass has run the same, and nothing has come by the road in either. Whatever it would say on the second day it says on the first. On the first day it therefore says free. But on the first day the sale was answered before the question was asked, and the rule of the counters requires promised to Nikias. If the buyer at the Vine then buys the jar he has been told is free, the last jar has been promised to two buyers.

The Closed Road

On a ring road where any stretch may close, and even with a known crossing and sandglasses at every house, no arrangement of the houses can both answer every buyer and keep the rule of the counters.

Proof — that no arrangement both answers every buyer and keeps the rule
  1. Suppose some arrangement did both. Divide the houses into two sides that share no house: the Vine on one, the Dolphin and the Anchor on the other. Close both stretches between them for the whole of what follows, so that no slip crosses in either direction.
  2. The first run. The line of the last jar of Chian is empty. A buyer at the Anchor buys the jar. Since every buyer is answered, he is answered, and on the rule's reckoning the jar is now his. Nothing else is asked anywhere. Call the time this takes T, by the sandglass.
  3. The second run. The line is empty. Nothing is asked anywhere for a span of at least T. Then a buyer at the Vine asks whether the jar is free. He is answered. No sale was ever made, so by the rule the answer is free.
  4. Put them together. Let the first run happen on the Dolphin–Anchor side and the second on the Vine's, at the same time. The Dolphin and the Anchor cannot tell this combined run from the first run, since nothing reaches them from the Vine in either, so the sale is made and answered as before. The long quiet span at the start of the second run means the Vine's question is asked after the sale was answered.
  5. At the Vine, this combined run cannot be told from the second run alone. The Vine began with the same book, its sandglass has run the same, no buyer came to it until the question, and nothing has arrived by the road, because both stretches are closed in both runs. The Vine acts only on what it has, so it answers free.
  6. But in the combined run the sale was answered before the question was asked, so by the rule's third clause the sale comes first in the single order, and a single clerk would have answered promised. The rule is broken, which contradicts the supposition.∎

Notice what the proof used and what it did not. It used one sale and one later question, nothing more. It gave the houses every help Arche can offer: a known crossing and sandglasses that run alike. They are no help, because what the Vine lacks is not time but news, and no amount of waiting brings news down a closed road. And it did not need the Vine to be alone. With more houses on each side the argument is the same.

Nor does the proof say that the Vine answered badly. On the second day free was exactly right. The trouble is that the Vine must say the same thing on both days, and the two days want different answers.

Chapter V

Keeping Any Two

The other half of Breweros' claim is that any two of the three can be had, and it is the easier half. One arrangement does most of the work.

Let one house keep the book for each cargo. The Chian landed at the Anchor, so the Anchor keeps its lines, and the other counters keep no lines of their own for it. A buyer who asks or buys at the Vine is not answered from the Vine's book; the Vine writes a slip to the Anchor, the Anchor writes the name on the line or reads it and sends back the answer, and only then does the Vine answer the buyer.

The keeper's arrangement (1) For each cargo, one house keeps the only book of its lines.
(2) A buyer at any other counter is answered only when the keeper's answer has come back by the road.
(3) The keeper takes slips one at a time, in the order they reach it.
Proof — that the keeper's arrangement keeps the rule
  1. Order all the purchases and questions by the moment the keeper handled them. This is one order, and it is the same whichever counter the buyer stood at.
  2. Every answer is the keeper's answer, given from the one book in that order, so each is what a single clerk with a single book would have said.
  3. The keeper handles a buyer's business after the buyer asked, since the slip was written only then, and before the buyer is answered, since the answer waits for the keeper. So each takes its place between asking and answer, and anything answered before another was asked was handled by the keeper first.∎

With that arrangement the three choices look like this.

The rule, while the road can close. Keep the keeper's arrangement and nothing else. When the road beside the Vine closes, the Vine's slip is not carried and its buyer is not answered; the Vine's counter, in effect, stands shut. Every answer that is given keeps the rule. And when the road is open, every buyer is answered, so the houses give up nothing they could have kept. This is Breweros' hold the jars back. A counter that simply refused every buyer would keep the rule too, and trivially; the keeper's arrangement is better, since it fails only when it has to.

The rule and every buyer answered, while no road closes. The same arrangement again. If no stretch ever closes, every slip reaches the keeper and every answer comes back.

Every buyer answered, while the road can close. Let each counter answer from its own book, whatever state it is in. Every buyer is answered at once. The rule is given up, and in the worst case a counter that has heard nothing since the season opened tells every buyer that every jar is free.

Put that way, the third choice sounds like carelessness. It need not be. The next two chapters show that a counter answering from its own book can still owe its buyers something exact.

Chapter VI

The Sandglass at the Gate

The houses do not want to choose once and for all. Almost all the time the road is open, and then the keeper's arrangement gives them both the rule and an answer for everyone. They want to lose the rule only while the road is actually closed, and keep answering throughout.

On Arche they can, because the road has a crossing. A slip to the keeper and its answer take at most two crossings on the road and the time the keeper's clerk takes to answer it. So each counter turns a gate-glass when it sends a slip to the keeper, a sandglass that runs for exactly that long.

The rule of the gate-glass (1) A buyer asks or buys. The counter sends a slip to the keeper and turns the gate-glass.
(2) If the keeper's answer comes back before the glass runs out, the buyer is given that answer.
(3) If the glass runs out first, the counter concludes the road is closed and answers from its own book. A question is told the latest name the keeper has sent for that line, unless the counter has itself since sold the jar, in which case it is told that sale. A purchase is told yours.

When the road is open, no glass ever runs out, since every answer comes back within two crossings and a clerk's reply, and the gate-glass arrangement is the keeper's arrangement: it keeps the rule. When the road is closed, the glass runs out, and the counter answers anyway. Every buyer is answered, always.

The houses therefore have half of what they wanted: every buyer is answered, and in any season in which no slip is lost, every answer keeps the rule. The Closed Road theorem is not broken. On the first day of Chapter IV the Vine's glass runs out and it says free, and the rule is broken, exactly as the theorem says it must be for any arrangement that answers every buyer. What the gate-glass buys is that the rule is broken only in a season when the road has actually been shut.

It buys nothing after the road opens. If the Vine sold a jar while cut off and told its buyer yours, the keeper never heard of it, and nothing in the gate-glass tells it. Long after the road is open again, the Anchor's buyers are told the jar is free. The promise is about whole seasons, not about the days after a closing. The next chapter repairs that.

It is worth being clear about what the gate-glass relies on. It works because on an open stretch a slip always arrives within a crossing, so a glass that runs out means the road is closed and nothing else. Chapter VIII shows what becomes of it when that is not so.

Chapter VII

How Stale, and for How Long

The gate-glass promises the rule in a season with no slip lost, and nothing at all about what happens after a closing. A buyer at the Vine may reasonably ask more. The road has been open for a week; is the Vine's book still a month behind? Gilbertos and Lynchaia wrote down a promise the houses can keep, and it has a span of time in it. Call that span t, fixed in advance and measured by the sandglass.

The promise of the span t For each jar, all the purchases and questions about it can be set in one order P in which:
(1) all purchases are ordered among themselves, and every question is placed relative to every purchase;
(2) every question is answered with the name of the last purchase before it in P, or free if there is none;
(3) business at any one counter keeps its own order in P;
(4) if no slip was ever lost, P keeps the rule of the counters: anything answered before another was asked comes before it;
(5) and whatever happens, if something was answered, and then the road was open for a span longer than t with no slip lost, and only then was something else asked, the first comes before the second.

The last clause is the new one. It says that a closing does its damage only while it lasts and for a span t after. Business done before an open span of t is seen by every counter after it. How long the road stays closed is up to the bandits, or the reports of them; the houses promise nothing about that. They promise only how long they take to catch up.

The keeper's arrangement needs only two additions to keep this promise.

The keeper's arrangement with a span At every counter, always: every slip from the keeper about a line, whether an answer, an acknowledgement or a notice, carries the line's name and number, and the counter keeps the highest-numbered one it has had. Every slip a counter sends to the keeper is preceded by its unacknowledged sales, in the order it made them; the single-file road delivers them first.
At a counter, a question: send to the keeper and turn the gate-glass. If the keeper answers in time, give that answer. If not, and the counter has itself sold the jar in a sale the keeper has not yet acknowledged, answer with the latest such sale. Otherwise answer with the highest-numbered name it holds for that line.
At a counter, a purchase: send to the keeper and turn the gate-glass. Tell the buyer yours when the keeper acknowledges it, or when the glass runs out, whichever is first. Until the keeper acknowledges it, send it again, with any other unacknowledged sales in the order they were made, often enough that on an open road it reaches the keeper within half of t.
At the keeper: each purchase that reaches it is written on the jar's line and takes the next number. Its acknowledgement carries the number. The keeper sends the name and number to every counter, and sends them again to any counter that has not acknowledged them, often enough that on an open road they arrive within half of t.
The Span

The keeper's arrangement with a span answers every buyer, keeps the rule of the counters in any season in which no slip is lost, and in every season keeps the promise of the span t.

Proof — that the keeper's arrangement with a span keeps the promise
  1. The order. Take one jar. Order its purchases by the numbers the keeper gave them; a sale that never reaches the keeper goes after all the numbered ones, in the order it was made. Place each question immediately after the purchase whose name it was given, or at the start if it was told free. Then every question is answered with the last purchase before it, which is clauses (1) and (2).
  2. Each counter's own order. A counter sends its sales in the order it made them, so the keeper numbers them in that order. Every slip a counter sends the keeper comes behind its unacknowledged sales on the single-file road, so the keeper has numbered them before it answers that counter's question. A counter keeps the highest number it has had from any slip of the keeper's and never answers with a lower one, and its own unacknowledged sales are newer than anything the keeper has sent it. So each question at a counter lands after that counter's earlier business. That is clause (3).
  3. No slip lost. Then no glass ever runs out, every answer is the keeper's, and this is the keeper's arrangement of Chapter V, which keeps the rule. That is clause (4).
  4. A purchase, then an open span. Suppose a purchase at the Vine was answered, then the road was open for longer than t. During that span the Vine sends the sale again until the keeper has it, and the keeper sends it with its number until every counter has it. Each takes at most half of t, so both are done within the span. So any purchase begun after the span gets a higher number, and any question begun after it is answered with this sale or a later one; neither comes before it.
  5. A question, then an open span. Suppose a question at the Vine was answered, then the road was open for longer than t. The name it was given came from the keeper, or from a sale of the Vine's own. Either way, within the span that sale is numbered by the keeper and sent to every counter, and the same argument as the last step places everything begun after the span later in P. That is clause (5).∎

The paper's own proof of this is a few lines long. It does not say how a question at a counter should treat that counter's own sales that the keeper has not yet acknowledged, and the second step above supplies it. This is not the hard part of the paper; the hard part was seeing what could be promised at all.

Look at what the diagram does not show: the Anchor, all through the closing, went on selling Chian from the lines it kept. If its buyer took the same jar as the Vine's buyer, the keeper numbers both sales when the Vine's arrives, and the line ends with whichever name was numbered later. The promise of the span is kept: both sales are in the order, and every question after the span is answered with the later name. But one jar has been promised to two buyers, and one house will have to make that good. No promise about when the houses learn of it can undo it. This is why answering from a stale book is, on Arche, something the houses do knowingly and only when the road is shut, and never their ordinary way of trading.

Chapter VIII

What the Closed Road Does Not Say

In a gale, even the gate-glass fails. Everything in Chapters VI and VII relied on the crossing: a glass that runs out means the road is closed. In the winter gales there is no crossing. A runner may shelter under a ledge for a day or five, and a house that has waited any length of time cannot tell a closed road from a slow runner. Gilbertos and Lynchaia proved first, in that world, the same theorem as Chapter IV. Then they proved something stronger: in a gale, no arrangement can answer every buyer and keep the rule even on the days when no slip is lost.

The Gale

If crossings have no bound, no arrangement that answers every buyer can keep the rule of the counters even in those runs where every slip arrives.

Proof — that in a gale, rule-keeping cannot even be promised when nothing is lost
  1. Suppose an arrangement answers every buyer in every run, and keeps the rule in every run in which no slip is lost.
  2. By the Closed Road theorem, which holds in a gale as on a fair day, there is some run in which a slip is lost and the rule is broken. The rule is broken at a definite answer, given after only so much has happened.
  3. Take the run only up to that answer. Every slip that was lost in it, let it instead be still on the road, under a ledge, and let it arrive afterwards; in a gale no house can tell the difference, since no delay is too long. Continue the run so that every slip arrives.
  4. This is a run in which no slip is lost. Up to the bad answer it is the same run as before, so the bad answer is still given, and the rule is broken in a run where nothing was lost. That contradicts the supposition.∎

The mercenaries at the foot of Mount Phyle know that choice. On windy nights, no man goes to a gate while anyone might still go to the other: what must never go wrong is kept, and while the wind blows they may wait a long time*. That is agreement rather than the rule of the counters, and a different theorem, but it is the same kind of choice as Breweros' hold the jars back. In the gale, the gate-glass has nothing to offer. Only once the storm has broken does a glass that runs out mean anything.

The harbour dues are not touched. The dues a ship owes at Arche are fixed by the island's old law and cut into a slab at every gate, and they never change, so any counter, cut off or not, can tell a shipmaster what he owes and be right. The Closed Road theorem is about lines that change as jars are sold, where one counter needs news of the others. A thing that needs no news from anywhere is outside it.

It is not the camps' impossibility. Below the crown, the mercenaries cannot be sure of both deciding and deciding alike when one of them may fall silent*. The closed road is a relative of that, and the two have the same shape: something that must never go wrong, the rule, and something that must in the end happen, an answer, which cannot both be promised when the road is unreliable. But neither result gives the other. The camps lose no ravens and have one camp that may stop. The houses have no house that stops, and lose slips instead.

It is not about a failed house. A house that has stopped altogether owes no one an answer. Breweros himself said that a single house cut off is much like one that has failed, and so it is, except that the Vine's buyers are standing at its counter. That is why the question has to be put to it at all. The proof never needed the Vine to be alone: were there more houses, any division of them into two sides would give the same argument.

It does not say the houses must make one choice. The houses divide the choice, and Gilbertos and Lynchaia, writing about the claim many years later, said that many real arrangements do. The houses can give up the rule for one cargo and not for another: a question about a jar of oil from a good pressing may be answered from any book, since hundreds more stand in the storehouse, while the last jars of a small cargo of Chian wait for the keeper. They can divide by what is asked: a question may be answered from the book while a purchase waits. They can move the boundary as a cargo sells, selling freely from the book while there are many jars and asking the keeper as the last few go. Each of these gives up a little of both, and each is still bound by Chapter IV wherever it answers a buyer from a book that may be behind.

The ideas, plainly

In 2000 Eric Brewer conjectured that a shared-data system can have at most two of three properties: consistency, availability, and tolerance of network partitions. Gilbert and Lynch made the conjecture precise and proved it. Consistency is atomicity, or linearizability: the system behaves as if there were one copy of the data, handling operations one at a time, so a read that begins after a write has completed must see it. Availability means that every request received by a working node eventually gets a response. Partition tolerance means that these hold even though the network may lose any messages, including every message between two groups of nodes.

The proof is a single indistinguishability argument. Cut the nodes into two groups and lose every message between them. A write completes in one group; later, a read arrives in the other. That group has seen nothing, so it cannot tell this execution from one in which no write happened, and it must return the old value. If it answers at all, it is wrong. Timers and a known delivery bound do not help, because the missing information cannot arrive.

Any two of the three properties can be had. A single designated node holding the data gives consistency always and availability whenever there is no partition. Answering from local data gives availability always and gives up consistency. With timers and a delivery bound, a node can time out and answer from local data only when messages are actually lost, so the service is consistent in any execution without a partition, and Delayed-t consistency bounds how long stale answers can persist once the partition heals. Without a delivery bound, a node cannot tell a lost message from a slow one, and even this is impossible.

CAP is one case of a wider fact about unreliable systems: a safety property (nothing bad happens) and a liveness property (something good eventually happens) often cannot both be guaranteed. Real systems divide the trade-off: by data, by operation, or by how much slack remains, choosing consistency where a mistake is costly and availability where it is not.

The Papers Behind This Book

  • Eric A. Brewer, Towards Robust Distributed Systems. Invited talk, 19th ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), Portland, Oregon, July 2000. — The conjecture: of consistency, availability and tolerance to network partitions, a shared-data system can have at most two; the three cases and their examples.
  • Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch, Brewer's Conjecture and the Feasibility of Consistent, Available, Partition-Tolerant Web Services.
