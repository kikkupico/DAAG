Answering While Cut Off

When one part of a system is cut off from the rest, should it keep answering and risk being wrong, or stay silent until it can be sure? Can it ever have both?

The four mercenaries at the foot of the hill keep a standing gate, north or south, which any of them may move and any may ask after. On these nights a raven can be lost. When every bird between one tent and the rest is lost, the man in that tent cannot both answer at once and answer as the others would. No arrangement lets him. What the four can choose is which to give up, and for how long.

Chapter I

Lost Birds

Bandits hold the crown of Mount Phyle. At the foot, at the four quarters, four mercenaries keep four small tents, one man to a tent, under nobody's command, and no man can see another*. The tents are numbered 1 to 4, and each man goes by his tent's number, Number 1 to Number 4. They must go in at one gate, north or south. Word passes only by raven, and nobody lies.

Until they go, the four keep a standing gate: the gate they mean to go in at, as things stand. At dusk it is north. Any man who sees something on his side of the hill may move it to the other gate, and any man may ask which gate stands. A move is done when the man who made it is told it stands; a question is done when he is told north or south. Nobody goes anywhere in this book, and nothing follows from which gate stands.

The one change from the other nights at the camps is this: a raven can be lost. A hawk may take it in the folds, and it never arrives. Nobody sees a bird taken; a man waiting on a bird learns it was lost only when longer than any flight has passed and it has not come, and even then he learns nothing of what it carried. On some nights every bird between one tent and the other three is lost, for as long as the hawks keep to that fold. That tent is cut off.

On the night this book is about, tent 4 is cut off. Number 4 is alive and well and in his tent. He wants to know which gate stands, and he wants to know now. What should he be told?

Chapter II

What the Four Owe Each Other

The four owe each other two things.

The first is that the standing gate behave as one gate, though each tent keeps its own note of it. Every move and every question, at all four tents, must fit one order in which a single record would have given every answer, each taking effect at a moment within its own span, and anything finished before another began coming first*. Call it answering as one. If Number 2 has moved the gate to south and been told it stands, a man who asks afterwards must hear south, or a later move.

The second is that every man is answered. A man who asks, or moves the gate, is answered in the end, from what his tent holds. No limit is set on how long that may take, only that it come. Being told to wait until the hawks leave is not an answer, since they may stay all night. And a tent that says north to every question, to be safe, has answered, but wrongly whenever south stands.

The camps, as this book uses them (1) On these nights there is no wind. A bird that arrives does so within a known span, a flight; a man answers a bird within a known span of his own.
(2) Any bird may be lost, and between a cut-off tent and the rest every bird is lost, for as long as that lasts.
(3) Each tent has a sandglass. The glasses run alike, so a man can measure how long he has waited, but no man can tell what another's glass shows.
Gilbert & Lynch's partially synchronous model with arbitrary message loss, §4.1: a known bound tmsg on delivery of messages that are not lost, and local timers at the same rate.

Line (1) is the calm of the windy nights after the wind has dropped*, here from the start. Chapter VII turns the wind back on.

Chapter III

The Cut-Off Tent

It was first put as a claim, and only later proved: of three things, answering as one, answering every man, and carrying on while birds can be lost, the four may have any two, but not all three.

The proof is short. At dusk the standing gate is north, and tent 4 is cut off. At tent 2, Number 2 moves the gate to south and is told it stands. His birds to tent 4 are lost. Later, Number 4 asks which gate stands.

He must be answered. What can he go on? His own note, which says north, and whatever birds have reached him, which is none. Now take a second night, the same in every respect except that nobody moves the gate. Tent 4 is cut off, and later Number 4 asks. Here the right answer is north.

To Number 4 the two nights are the same. His note is the same, his glass has run the same, and no bird has come in either. Whatever he says on the second night he says on the first. So on the first he says north, though Number 2's move was finished before he asked, and answering as one requires south.

The Cut-Off Tent

When birds can be lost, no arrangement of the four can both answer every man and answer as one, even with a known flight and glasses in every tent.

Proof — that no arrangement both answers every man and answers as one
  1. Suppose some arrangement did both. Put tent 4 on one side and the other three on the other, and lose every bird between them for the whole of what follows.
  2. The first night. The gate stands at north. Number 2 moves it to south. Since every man is answered, he is told it stands. Nothing else happens anywhere. Call the time this takes T, by the glass.
  3. The second night. The gate stands at north. Nothing happens anywhere for at least T. Then Number 4 asks which gate stands, and is answered. Nobody moved it, so answering as one gives north.
  4. Put them together. Let the first night happen among the three and the second at tent 4, at the same time. The three cannot tell this from the first night, since nothing reaches them from tent 4 in either, so Number 2's move is made and answered as before. Because of the quiet span, Number 4 asks after his move was finished.
  5. Number 4 cannot tell this from the second night. He began with the same note, his glass has run the same, and no bird has reached him in either. So he answers north.
  6. But Number 2's move was finished before Number 4 asked, so it comes first in any order that answers as one, and a single record would have said south. That contradicts the supposition.∎

The proof used one move and one later question, and gave the four every help the camps can offer: a known flight and glasses that run alike. They are no help, because what Number 4 lacks is not time but news, and no amount of waiting brings a lost bird. Nor did the proof need tent 4 alone; with any division of the four into two sides the argument is the same. And it does not say Number 4 answered badly. On the second night north is exactly right. He must say the same thing on both nights, and the two nights want different answers.

Chapter IV

Keeping Any Two

Any two of the three can be had, and one arrangement does most of the work. Let one tent keep the standing gate: say tent 1. Number 1 holds the only true note. A man at any other tent who moves the gate or asks sends a bird to tent 1 and waits; Number 1 changes his note or reads it, and sends the answer back; only then is the man answered.

The keeper's arrangement (1) One tent keeps the only note of the standing gate.
(2) A man at any other tent is answered only when the keeper's answer has come back.
(3) The keeper deals with birds one at a time, in the order he takes them in.
The centralized algorithm, §3.2.1–3.2.2.
Proof — that the keeper's arrangement answers as one
  1. Order all moves and questions by the moment the keeper dealt with them. This is one order, the same whichever tent the man was at.
  2. Every answer is the keeper's, from his one note in that order, so each is what a single record would say.
  3. The keeper deals with a man's business after it began, since the bird was sent then, and before it ends, since the answer waits for the keeper. So each takes effect within its span, and anything finished before another began was dealt with first.∎

With that arrangement the three choices look like this.

Answering as one, while birds can be lost. The keeper's arrangement and nothing else. When tent 4 is cut off, Number 4's bird does not arrive and he is not answered: his tent, in effect, falls silent. Every answer that is given is right. When no bird is lost, every man is answered.

Answering as one and answering every man, while no bird is lost. The same arrangement: every bird reaches the keeper and every answer comes back.

Answering every man, while birds can be lost. Let each man answer from his own note, whatever it says. Every man is answered at once, and answering as one is given up. In the worst case a man who has heard nothing since dusk says north all night.

Chapter V

Waiting a Glass

Most nights no fold has hawks in it, and then the keeper's arrangement gives both answering as one and an answer for every man. The four want to lose answering as one only while birds are actually being lost, and to answer every man throughout. Because a bird that arrives does so within a known flight, they can.

Waiting a glass (1) A man who moves the gate or asks sends a bird to the keeper and turns a glass that runs for two flights and the keeper's time to answer.
(2) If the keeper's answer comes before the glass runs out, he takes it.
(3) If the glass runs out first, he answers from his own note: a question gets the latest gate the keeper has sent him, unless he has himself moved the gate since, in which case it gets his own move; a move is told it stands.
A timeout of 2tmsg + tlocal, §4.3.

When no bird is lost, no glass runs out, and this is the keeper's arrangement: it answers as one. When a bird is lost, the glass runs out and the man answers anyway. Every man is always answered. So on any night on which no bird is lost, every answer is as one.

The Cut-Off Tent is not broken. On the first night of Chapter III, Number 4's glass runs out and he says north, exactly as that result says any arrangement answering every man must. What waiting a glass buys is that this happens only on nights when birds are actually lost.

It buys nothing afterwards. If Number 4 moved the gate while cut off, the keeper never heard of it, and nothing here tells him. Long after the hawks have gone, the others are told a gate Number 4 has moved away from. The promise is about whole nights, not about the glasses after a loss. The next chapter repairs that.

Chapter VI

How Stale, and for How Long

A man may reasonably ask more. The hawks left long ago; is his note still hours behind? There is a promise the four can keep, and it has a span of time in it. Call that span t, fixed in advance and measured by the glass.

The promise of the span t All the moves and questions can be set in one order P in which:
(1) all moves are ordered among themselves, and every question is placed relative to every move;
(2) every question is answered with the gate of the last move before it in P, or north if there is none;
(3) each man's own business keeps its order in P;
(4) if no bird was ever lost, P answers as one;
(5) and whatever happens, if something was finished, and then a span longer than t passed with no bird lost, and only then something else began, the first comes before the second.
Delayed-t consistency, Definition 3.

The last clause is the new one. A loss does its damage only while it lasts and for a span t after. How long the hawks stay, nobody can promise. The four promise only how long they take to catch up.

Birds at the camps come in any order, so a man cannot count on an earlier bird arriving first. Instead every bird he sends the keeper carries, before anything else, all the moves he has made that the keeper has not yet acknowledged, in the order he made them.

The keeper's arrangement with a span At every tent, always: every bird from the keeper carries the gate and its number, and a man keeps the highest-numbered one he has had. Every bird a man sends the keeper carries all his unacknowledged moves, in order.
A question: send to the keeper and wait a glass. If the keeper answers in time, give that answer. If not, give the latest of his own unacknowledged moves, or if there is none, the highest-numbered gate he holds.
A move: send to the keeper and wait a glass. It stands when the keeper acknowledges it or the glass runs out, whichever is first. Until acknowledged, send it again, often enough that with no bird lost it reaches the keeper within half of t.
At the keeper: each move that reaches him, taken in its sender's order, changes his note and takes the next number. He sends the gate and number to every tent, again and again to any that has not acknowledged it, often enough that with no bird lost they arrive within half of t.
The modified centralized algorithm, Theorem 4.
The Span

The keeper's arrangement with a span answers every man, answers as one on any night on which no bird is lost, and on every night keeps the promise of the span t.

Proof — that the keeper's arrangement with a span keeps the promise
  1. The order. Order the moves by the keeper's numbers; a move that never reaches him goes after all the numbered ones, in the order it was made. Place each question immediately after the move whose gate it was given, or at the start if it was given north from dusk. Then every question is answered with the last move before it: clauses (1) and (2).
  2. Each man's own order. A man's moves reach the keeper in the order he made them, since each bird carries all the earlier unacknowledged ones first; so the keeper numbers them in that order, and has numbered them before he answers that man's question. A man never answers with a lower number than one he holds, and his own unacknowledged moves are newer than anything the keeper has sent him. So each man's questions land after his earlier business: clause (3).
  3. No bird lost. Then no glass runs out, every answer is the keeper's, and this is the keeper's arrangement of Chapter IV, which answers as one: clause (4).
  4. A move, then a span. Suppose a move by Number 4 was finished, then more than t passed with no bird lost. In that span his move reaches the keeper and the keeper's number for it reaches every tent, each within half of t. So any move begun after the span gets a higher number, and any question begun after it is answered with this move or a later one.
  5. A question, then a span. The gate a finished question was given came from the keeper or from a move of the asker's own. Either way, within the span that move is numbered and sent to every tent, and the last step places everything begun after the span later in P: clause (5).∎

The paper's own proof of this is a few lines long, and says nothing about how a man should treat his own moves the keeper has not yet acknowledged; the second step supplies it.

What the diagram does not show: all through the loss, the three went on moving and asking through the keeper. If Number 2 moved the gate to north while Number 4, cut off, moved it to south, the keeper numbers both when Number 4's bird arrives, and the gate ends at whichever was numbered later. The promise is kept. But for a while two men were each told a different gate stood, and no promise about when they learn of it undoes that.

Chapter VII

What Being Cut Off Does Not Say

In a wind, even waiting a glass fails. Everything in Chapters V and VI relied on the flight: a glass that runs out means a bird was lost. On a windy night there is no known flight; a bird may sit in the pines for any time, and a man who has waited any length of time* cannot tell a lost bird from a slow one. Then something stronger is true.

The Wind

If flights have no bound, no arrangement that answers every man can answer as one even on the nights when no bird is lost.

Proof — that in a wind, answering as one cannot even be promised when nothing is lost
  1. Suppose an arrangement answers every man on every night, and answers as one on every night on which no bird is lost.
  2. By the Cut-Off Tent, which holds in a wind as well, there is a night on which a bird is lost and a wrong answer is given. It is given after only so much has happened.
  3. Take that night up to the wrong answer. Let every bird that was lost instead be still in the pines, arriving afterwards; in a wind nobody can tell the difference. Continue the night so that every bird arrives.
  4. On this night no bird is lost, and up to the wrong answer it is the same night, so the wrong answer is still given. That contradicts the supposition.∎

It is not the camps' impossibility. With one man who may fall silent and no longest flight, the four cannot be sure of ever going*. That has the same shape as this book's result: something that must never go wrong, and something that must in the end happen, which cannot both be promised. But neither result gives the other. There, no bird is lost and one man may stop. Here, no man stops and birds are lost instead.

It is not about a dead man. A man who has stopped owes no one an answer. Number 4 is alive and wants to know. That is why the question has to be put to his tent at all.

A thing that needs no news is outside it. Which number each tent bears never changes, and any man, cut off or not, can say so and be right. The result is about a gate that can be moved, where one tent needs news of the others.

The four need not choose once for all. They may answer as one for the standing gate and answer at once for something that matters less, or answer questions from their own notes while moves wait for the keeper. Each such division gives up a little of both, and each is still bound by Chapter III wherever a man answers from a note that may be behind.

The ideas, plainly

In 2000 Eric Brewer conjectured that a shared-data system can have at most two of three properties: consistency, availability, and tolerance of network partitions. Gilbert and Lynch made the conjecture precise and proved it. Consistency is atomicity, or linearizability: the system behaves as if there were one copy of the data. Availability means every request received by a working node eventually gets a response. Partition tolerance means these hold even though the network may lose any messages, including every message between two groups of nodes.

The proof is a single indistinguishability argument. Cut the nodes into two groups and lose every message between them. A write completes in one group; later, a read arrives in the other. That group cannot tell this execution from one with no write, so if it answers at all, it is wrong. Timers and a known delivery bound do not help, because the missing information cannot arrive.

Any two of the three can be had. A single designated node gives consistency always and availability whenever there is no partition. With timers and a delivery bound, a node can time out and answer locally only when messages are actually lost, and Delayed-t consistency bounds how long stale answers persist once the partition heals. Without a delivery bound, even that is impossible.

CAP is one case of a wider fact about unreliable systems: a safety property and a liveness property often cannot both be guaranteed. Real systems divide the trade-off by data or by operation.
