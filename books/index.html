Distributed Algorithms of Ancient Greece

An exploration of 31 papers in distributed computing employing the polarising ancient Greece allegory introduced by Leslie Lamport in his seminal paper, The Part-Time Parliament.

Arche · The Ring Road

Causality, State & Partial Synchrony

Book I · Ordering Without Clocks

If no two machines agree on the time, how can they agree on what happened first? And when two things happen far apart, how can anyone tell whether one led to the other or neither knew of the other?

We find out by exploring how ancient Greek trading houses kept the order of trades when sundials became useless on cloudy days.
Lamport 1978 · Fidge 1988 · Mattern 1988

Book II · Consistent Snapshots

How do you record the state of a whole system when it never stops moving and no one can see all of it at once? And what can such a record reliably tell you?

We find out by exploring how ancient Greek trading houses counted every jar they owned, including those in transit, without interrupting ongoing trades.
Chandy & Lamport 1985

Book III · Consensus in Partial Synchrony

If messages can be delayed without limit, agreement cannot be guaranteed; yet real networks behave well most of the time. Can a protocol stay safe through any delay, and still decide once things settle?

We find out by exploring how three ancient Greek trading houses decided whether their merchant fleet should sail together while winter gales delayed the messengers between them.
Dwork, Lynch & Stockmeyer 1988

Behaving as One Copy Not yet written

When data is copied onto several machines, what does it mean for them to behave as if there were only one copy? And who could ever tell the difference?

We find out by exploring how three ancient Greek trading houses sold from one stock of jars without ever promising the last jar to two buyers.
Herlihy & Wing 1990

Consistency or Availability Not yet written

When a network splits, can a replicated service keep answering every request, and still answer as if there were one copy?

We find out by exploring what ancient Greek trading houses did when bandits on the road cut one house off from the others.
Brewer 2000 · Gilbert & Lynch 2002

Arche · Mount Phyle

Consensus & Faults

Book IV · The Limits of Agreement

Can machines always agree when some of them may fail? What if one may stop silently while messages take any time at all, and what if messages are prompt but some machines lie?

We find out by exploring how mercenaries at the foot of a hill, and the bandits holding its summit, each had to agree on a plan without being able to see one another.
Fischer, Lynch & Paterson 1985 · Lamport, Shostak & Pease 1982 · Pease, Shostak & Lamport 1980

Consensus with Unreliable Failure Detectors Not yet written

If a crashed machine looks exactly like a slow one, agreement is impossible. How good must our guesses about who has crashed be before agreement becomes possible again?

We find out by exploring how Greek mercenary camps kept a board of which comrades had fallen silent, knowing any mark on it might be wrong.
Chandra & Toueg 1996

Replication Despite Liars Not yet written

Can a replicated service keep working when some of its machines lie, the leader included, and messages take arbitrarily long?

We find out by exploring how four Greek mercenary camps kept their orders in one sequence when one of them may have been bought.
Castro & Liskov 1999

Paxos

Consensus & Replicated State Machines

Consensus by Overlapping Majorities Not yet written

How can a group of machines agree on a sequence of commands when any of them may wander off at any time, and no one is in charge for long?

Lamport answered this himself, with the part-time legislators of the Greek island of Paxos. We present his paper as he wrote it, with pictures.
Lamport 1998 · Lamport 2001

Consensus in Practice Not yet written

An algorithm can be proven correct on paper and still fail in production. What does it take to keep consensus running for years on real machines?

We find out by exploring the repairs an ancient Greek parliament needed to keep sitting for years: damaged ledgers, changing membership, answering citizens quickly, and more.
Chandra, Griesemer & Redstone 2007

Skene

Leader-Driven Consensus

Leader-Driven Replication Not yet written

Most practical systems put one leader in charge. How do you replace a leader without losing anything it promised, and can such an algorithm be made easy to understand?

We find out by exploring how the legislators of a Greek island governed from a theatre, one voice on the stage at a time.
Oki & Liskov 1988 · Liskov & Cowling 2012 · Ongaro & Ousterhout 2014

Homonoia

Replication Without Assembly

Spreading Updates by Gossip Not yet written

How can an update reach every machine in a large system without anyone organising the spread?

We find out by exploring how Greek scholars passed on new learning like rumours in a colonnade, and compared whole collections once a year to catch what the rumours missed.
Demers et al. 1987 · Saito & Shapiro 2005

Always-Writable Storage Not yet written

How can a store accept every write even when some of its machines are down? And how often will it then hand back stale data?

We find out by exploring a ring of ancient Greek libraries that keep taking in new copies even when some of them are closed.
DeCandia et al. 2007 · Bailis et al. 2012

Update Anywhere, Order Later Not yet written

Why does letting every replica accept updates and reconcile them later break down as a system grows, and what can replace it?

We find out by exploring an ancient Greek scriptorium where scribes use their changes at once, drafted in wax, and copy them fair in ink only when their order has been settled.
Gray, Helland, O'Neil & Shasha 1996 · Terry et al. 1995

Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types Not yet written

Can some data be merged in any order, any number of times, and still end the same everywhere, so that no coordination is ever needed?

We find out by exploring how Greek scholars on remote terraces kept a shared count with voting pebbles.
Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero & Zawirski 2011 · the same authors' comprehensive study, 2011

Promises Without Coordination Not yet written

Without coordinating on every write, what can a system still promise? That no one sees a reply before the message it answers? That no one sees a change half made? That two people never take the last item?

We find out by exploring how libraries sorted their letters and served their readers while the couriers were stopped.
Lloyd et al. 2011 · Bailis et al. 2014

The Council Ground

When Coordination Is Needed

Coordination and Monotonicity Not yet written

Which problems can be solved without machines ever waiting for one another, and which cannot?

We find out by exploring a Greek hall with two doors: one for questions that need an assembly to answer, and one for those that do not.
Hellerstein 2010 · Ameloot, Neven & Van den Bussche 2011
