Telling the Absent from the Slow

In an asynchronous network, a computer that has crashed looks identical to one whose messages are merely running slow, and that single ambiguity makes agreement impossible. What if each machine had an imperfect detector that guessed which machines had crashed? How wrong can those guesses be before agreement becomes impossible again?

To find out, we visit the Chamber of Paxos, where scribes chalk an attendance slate at each door noting which part-time legislators have stepped out into the olive groves, and discover how little those slates need to get right for the parliament to pass a decree.

To find out, we follow the algorithms step by step. Four processes each keep a list of the processes they suspect have crashed, and we discover how little those lists need to get right for the system to agree.

After Tushar Deepak Chandra and Sam Toueg, Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems, 1996

Suppose each legislator had an imperfect guide to tell them when to stop waiting. That is the single addition made here. At each door to the Chamber stands a chalked attendance slate (a pinakion, acting as a failure detector) with a row for each legislator. Whenever a colleague is suspected of having departed, their row on the slate is chalked; if the suspicion passes and they are seen entering the colonnade, the chalk mark is wiped out again. A legislator waiting for a note from a colleague simply waits until either the messenger arrives or that colleague's row is chalked on their slate.Suppose each process had an imperfect hint telling it when to stop waiting. That is the single addition made here. Each process gets a failure detector (a suspect list) with an entry for each of the other processes. Whenever a process is suspected of having crashed, it is on the list; if the suspicion passes, it can be taken off again. A process waiting for a message from another simply waits until either the message arrives or that process is on its suspect list.

Crucially, the slates at different doors need not agree with one another, and they are often wrong: a scribe may chalk a present colleague whose runner is merely slow in the heat, or leave an absent member's row blank for a while. How reliable these slates have to be before the four legislators can pass a decree is what remains to be settled.Crucially, the suspect lists at different processes need not agree with one another, and they are often wrong: a list may include a live process whose message is merely slow, or omit a crashed one for a while. How reliable these lists have to be before the four processes can reach agreement is what remains to be settled.

Two ways to go wrong

How a slate may be wrongHow a detector may be wrong

Any attendance slate can make only two kinds of mistakes: it can fail to chalk an absent legislator, or it can falsely chalk a present colleague. So we judge a slate by two promises, how well it chalks the absent (completeness), and how well it spares the present (accuracy).Any failure detector can make only two kinds of mistakes: it can fail to suspect a crashed process, or it can falsely suspect a live one. So we judge a detector by two promises, how well it suspects the crashed (completeness), and how well it spares the live (accuracy).

A warning

Setting The Chamber of Paxos

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Tushar Deepak Chandra and Sam Toueg, Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems. Journal of the ACM 43, 2 (March 1996), 225–267. Defines the eight classes of failure detectors, the reduction from weak to strong completeness, the consensus algorithms for S and ◇S, the majority lower bound for ◇P, and the equivalence of consensus and atomic broadcast.

The ideas, plainly. Deterministic consensus is impossible in an asynchronous system if even a single process can crash, because a crashed process cannot be distinguished from a slow one. Chandra and Toueg factor that obstacle into an abstract module at each process—an unreliable failure detector that outputs a set of processes currently suspected to have crashed. Detectors are specified purely by two axiomatic properties: completeness (crashed processes are eventually permanently suspected, either by every correct process or by at least one correct process) and accuracy (correct processes are spared from false suspicion, either all of them or at least one of them, and either perpetually from the start or only eventually).

Because processes can gossip their suspect lists and clear any process from which they receive a message, weak completeness reduces to strong completeness while preserving accuracy—leaving four core classes (P, S, ◇P, ◇S). With class S (some correct process is never suspected), a four-phase relay protocol solves consensus regardless of how many processes crash (f < n). With class ◇S (eventually some correct process is never suspected), a rotating-coordinator protocol solves consensus whenever a strict majority of processes are correct (f < n/2); safety holds unconditionally even if the detector misbehaves forever, and termination occurs as soon as the detector stabilizes. A partition argument shows that f < n/2 is necessary even for ◇P.

Failure detectors separate the why of timing from the how of agreement: in a partially synchronous system, heartbeats with adaptive timeouts implement ◇P, while the consensus protocol itself can be written and proved without mentioning clocks or message delay bounds at all.
