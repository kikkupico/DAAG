Pilot · Questions · Arche · Mount Phyle

The Limits of Agreement

Can machines always agree when some of them may fail? What if one may stop silently while messages take any time at all, and what if messages are prompt but some machines lie?

One hill, two parties, one question apiece. The book takes turns between them: a chapter below with the mercenaries, then a chapter above with the bandits. Each chapter leaves a question, and the next chapter of the same party answers it.

Chapter I

The Hill

Bandits hold the crown of Mount Phyle with their loot: the chief, Number 1, on the peak, and three of his men at posts in the crags below the rim, none within sight or earshot of another. Four mercenaries, hired to clear them out, are camped at the foot, one to a tent, out of sight of one another and under nobody's command*. Both parties send word only by raven. Each has one question to settle, with two answers.

The two questions

Below. Four men cannot come at both gates — two at each is no assault, and a good way to be killed in pairs. So all four must go in at the same gate: north or south.

Above. The bandits must hold the hill and keep the loot, or scatter and abandon it, and must do the same thing. Half holding while half scatter is worse than either.

Which answer is better, in either case, is settled nowhere in this book. Nor is any night being narrated: what is described is an arrangement, and nothing comes out.

The two parties differ in exactly one respect each. Below, no man ever misreports anything, but any man may die, and nobody can say how long a bird will take. Above, nobody dies and the flights are short and bounded, but some men do not report what they were told.

Which is the worse trouble to arrange against: a friend who may be dead, or a friend who may be lying? The book answers only at its end.

Chapter II · Below

What the Mercenaries Want

Take the foot of the hill first. The tents are numbered 1 to 4, and each man goes by his tent's number. Each formed an opinion at dusk from what he could see of his own quarter: north gate or south. What do they want of what they do with those opinions? Three things.

The three demands

First. No two of them go to different gates.

Second. Both gates must stay genuinely possible. Agreeing beforehand to go north whatever anyone saw would satisfy the first demand perfectly and make the evening pointless. What they do must depend on what they saw.

Third. Somebody must eventually go.

Why the second? Because without it they could agree beforehand to go north whatever anyone saw, which satisfies the first demand perfectly and makes the evening pointless. Why the third? Because an arrangement in which nobody ever goes satisfies the first two for free.

Going is a definite act, not a state of mind. A man goes by striking his tent and going round the slope to one gate; his quarter is then open behind him, and he will not be back before dawn. So he may think what he likes for as long as he likes, and none of it counts; then he does one thing, and it counts for good.

One more condition, easy to pass over: they may not draw lots. Each acts on what has reached him and nothing else, so that given the same sightings and the same arrivals in the same order, each does the same thing every time.

Nobody lies below. So why not simply wait until every man has reported, and then all apply the same rule? What could possibly go wrong with that?

Chapter III · Above

What the Bandits Want

Now the crown. Each bandit sees one part of the hill and forms his own account of what should be done: hold, or scatter. The four accounts must get into four heads unaltered. Why is that hard? Because a liar can send one thing to one man and something else to another.

So take the accounts one at a time. One man, the chief for this round, sends his order to the other three, the guards, and two things are wanted of that sending.

The two conditions

The first. Every honest guard obeys the same order.

The second. If the chief is honest, every honest guard obeys the order he sent.

If the chief is honest, the second gives the first for nothing. But what if the chief is the liar? Then the second says nothing at all, while the first still stands: honest men must end up together even when the man they listened to told them different things. That asymmetry is the whole problem.

A liar may do anything: send different orders to different men, send nothing, keep a lie up consistently, or agree his lies with another liar. Who the liars are is never learned, by anyone on the hill or by the reader.

And the chief is a job, not a rank. Number 1 holds the peak, but in these arrangements whoever's account is going round is the chief for that round. Run the thing four times, once for each man's account, and every honest man ends holding the same four accounts, after which any rule they all apply gives them all the same answer.

The chief might lie. Then why doesn't each guard simply ask the others what the chief told them, and compare?

Chapter IV · Below

A Tent That Does Not Answer

The second chapter asked why the mercenaries don't wait for every report. To see why not, look at the birds below. Four things are true of them, and all four are needed.

They arrive. On these nights no bird is lost.

They take as long as they take. The gullies are wooded and full of the hill's own crows, and a bird may join them for an hour or for the night. There is no longest flight. No man can say if nothing has come from tent 1 by now, nothing is coming, because no such time exists.

They come in no particular order. Two birds sent an hour apart may arrive either way round.

Nothing is forged. A tube from tent 2 was tied there and says what that man wrote.

And a man may always walk to his perch and find nothing there. That is not news; it changes nothing, and he can do it all night.

Now add death. Any one of the four may be killed. It has not happened, and nobody is killed in this book; what matters is that it may. A dead man's tent simply falls silent. Nobody succeeds him and no bird reports it.

The difficulty, entire

A man waiting on a tent that has not answered is in one of two situations. Either the man there is dead and no bird will ever come; or he is perfectly well, the bird went out hours ago, and it is sitting in a pine two spurs away.

Nothing distinguishes them — no observation, no instrument, no length of waiting. Both look like an empty perch. And since there is no longest flight, waiting longer never turns one into the other. It only makes the wait longer.

That answers the question. A man who waits for every report waits for ever whenever one man is dead, and he can never learn that one is. So an arrangement must let each man act, at some point, without hearing from everyone. At most one of the four may fall silent: nobody could promise it, but it is the most generous thing the argument can grant, and it turns out to be enough to do the damage.

Then somewhere in the night the matter gets settled, without everyone having been heard. When is that moment, and could anyone on the hill tell that it had come?

Chapter V · Above

The Ravens Above

The third chapter asked why each guard doesn't ask the others what the chief told him. On the crown he can, because the birds up here are different. The hops are short, direct and clear of the gullies, and no bird has ever taken longer than a sandglass takes to run. At dusk, before the posts were manned, the four turned their glasses together at one word, and each has turned his since the moment it ran out. One running is a glass: within it, every man who is going to send, sends.

What the birds are assumed to give, above the wall The first. A slip sent arrives, and arrives as written.
The second. The bird shows whose post released it, so no man can be impersonated.
The third. Silence is detectable: if nothing comes from a post within the glass, nothing was sent.

The third assumption is the one that costs something, and it is bought with the short flight and the glasses, exactly what the mercenaries below do not have. Silence is also given a meaning, or a man could block everything by sending nothing: no slip within the glass counts as hold. Hold is the default, not the wiser answer, only the definite one.

What none of this gives is any check on content. A plain slip says whatever its writer chose to write, and a guard reporting what the chief sent him may write anything he likes.

So guards can compare notes, and silence means something. Take the smallest party, a chief and two guards, one of the three a liar. Is comparing notes enough to save the two honest men?

Chapter VI · Below

Standings and Arrivals

The fourth chapter asked when, in the night, the matter is settled. To say that exactly, the argument needs a few words ordinary language lacks.

In this bookWhat it means
a standingHow the night stands, entire: what each of the four men holds in his head, together with every tube aloft and not yet taken in. A configuration.
an arrivalOne named man taking in one named tube. It is the only kind of thing that happens; a man thinks only when something arrives. An event.
an empty-handed arrivalGoing to the perch and finding nothing. Always possible, changes nothing, counts as that man's turn. A receive returning the null marker.
a course; a fair courseA list of arrivals in order. It is fair if it could really happen: no tube sent to a man still answering stays aloft for ever, and at most one man falls silent. A schedule and its run; an admissible run.
two-gated / one-gatedA standing is two-gated if a fair course from it still ends at the north gate and another still ends at the south. One-gated if only one remains. Bivalent / univalent.

So the question becomes: at what standing does the night stop being two-gated? Note what two-gated is not. It is not a state of mind: every man may be entirely resolved and the standing two-gated all the same. It reports only that which gate the night ends at still depends on the order in which birds arrive. And once any man has gone, the standing is one-gated.

Here is the first tool. It is the dullest fact in the book, and the most useful.

The lemma of separate folds

Take any standing and two courses that could run from it, no man appearing in both. Then they do not interfere: run either first and the night arrives at the same standing.

Why is it true? An arrival is one man taking in one tube and perhaps writing more. Everything in that belongs to him or to the air, and nothing to anybody else. If one course is the business of tents 1 and 2 and the other of tents 3 and 4, each is weather to the other.

Proof — that courses touching no man in common commute
  1. A standing is what is in each man's head together with the tubes aloft. An arrival changes two parts of it: the one man who takes the tube in, and the air — one tube removed, perhaps others added.
  2. Let σ₁ touch a set of men A and σ₂ a set B, disjoint. Running σ₁ alters only men in A, which σ₂ never reads; and conversely.
  3. Neither disables the other through the air: tubes are addressed, so those σ₂ consumes are addressed into B, and σ₁ removes only tubes addressed into A. Both orders are therefore applicable.
  4. In either order, the men of A have taken exactly the arrivals of σ₁ and those of B exactly the arrivals of σ₂, each in its own order, and the air has had the same tubes removed and added. So the standings reached are the same.∎

Now the question has a sharper form. At dusk, when every man has his sighting and no bird has flown, is the night already settled by the sightings alone?

Chapter VII · Above

Three Cannot Be Brought to Agree

The fifth chapter asked whether comparing notes saves two honest guards against a liar among three. It does not, and nothing can.

Put an honest guard in two situations. In the first, the chief is honest and sends hold to both guards; the other guard is the liar and reports he sent me scatter. The second condition binds our man: the chief is honest, so he must hold. In the second situation the chief is the liar and sends hold to our man and scatter to the other; the other guard is honest and reports, truthfully, he sent me scatter.

What does our man hold in each? Exactly the same two slips. He cannot tell the situations apart, so he holds in both. But the same argument, run on the other guard, has him scattering in the second, and there both guards are honest and have gone two ways.

That argument is persuasive and not a proof; reasoning of just this shape has settled things that turned out false. The result is correct, and the rigorous proof is in Pease, Shostak and Lamport 1980.

Proof — that three cannot be brought to agree, as far as such an argument goes
  1. Suppose an arrangement works for three men with one liar: all honest men obey the same order, and an honest chief is obeyed.
  2. A. Chief honest, sending hold to both guards; the second guard lies, reporting he sent me scatter.
  3. B. Chief the liar, sending hold to the first guard and scatter to the second; the second guard is honest and reports the same words.
  4. The first guard receives the same two things in A and B and nothing else, so he does the same in both. In A the chief is honest, so he must hold; hence he holds in B.
  5. C, the mirror of A: chief honest, sending scatter to both; the first guard lies. By the same argument the second guard scatters in C, and B looks like C to him, so he scatters in B.
  6. In B both guards are honest and have gone two ways, against the first condition.∎

Three men cannot bear one liar. How many can? What does a liar cost?

Chapter VIII · Below

The Dusk That Settles Nothing

The sixth chapter asked whether the night is settled at dusk by the sightings alone. For some way the sightings may fall, it is not.

Line up the sixteen possible beginnings so that each differs from the next in one man's sighting, all-north at one end and all-south at the other. Suppose every beginning were already settled. The second demand forces the ends opposite ways, so somewhere along the row two neighbours are settled opposite ways while differing in one man's sighting. Call him p.

Take a fair course from the north-settled neighbour in which p never stirs; he may be the one silent man. By the third demand it ends at the north gate. Run the same course from the other beginning. The two differ only in what p saw, and p has told nobody, so no other man can tell them apart. It ends north there too, though that beginning was settled south.

The dusk that settles nothing

However the four have arranged their affairs, there is a way for the sightings to fall that leaves the night two-gated.

Proof — that some beginning is two-gated
  1. Suppose every beginning were one-gated, each already fated by the sightings alone.
  2. Both fates occur: the second demand forces north where all four saw north, and south where all four saw south.
  3. Call two beginnings neighbours when they differ in one man's sighting. Walking from a north-fated beginning to a south-fated one by single changes, there must be neighbours B₀ and B₁ fated north and south, differing only in the sighting of one man p.
  4. Take a fair course from B₀ in which p never stirs; this is permitted, since at most one man may be silent. Being fair, it ends at the north gate.
  5. Run the same course from B₁. Since p never acts and his sighting is the only difference, every other man sees exactly the same arrivals in the same order, so the course ends at the north gate here too.
  6. But B₁ was fated south, and a fated beginning admits no course to the other gate. Hence some beginning is two-gated.∎

What did the work? Not a death: nobody died. The other three could not tell a silent man from a slow one, and so could not tell two different worlds apart.

The night can begin open. But it cannot stay open for ever, can it? Surely some arrival, sooner or later, must be the one that closes it?

Chapter IX · Above

Four Men to a Liar

The seventh chapter asked what a liar costs. The answer: more than three men for each. One liar wants four men, two want seven, three want ten. The crown has four, so it bears one liar and no more.

Why? Suppose some method brought three men or fewer to a liar safely through. Then three real men, one a liar, could each privately play a third of that method's men, none playing more parts than the method can bear liars. The method would keep its promise among the parts, and each real man would do what his parts did: three real men would agree against one liar, which the seventh chapter showed cannot happen.

Would it help to ask for less, only that they act at about the same time? No: rough agreement can be turned back into exact agreement, so the one is as hard as the other.

Proof — that fewer than four men to a liar cannot be made to work
  1. Suppose a method brings n men safely through m liars with n ≤ 3m. Call its men parts.
  2. Set three real men, one a liar, to play the parts: the real chief plays the parts' chief and at most m − 1 of its guards, and each real guard plays at most m of its guards. Since n ≤ 3m, they divide so.
  3. Word between two parts played by one man passes in his head; word between parts played by different men goes by bird, and by Chapter V arrives as sent and correctly attributed.
  4. At most one real man lies, and he plays at most m parts, so at most m parts lie — which the method was assumed to bear. It therefore keeps both conditions among the parts.
  5. By the first condition, all the parts played by one honest real man obey one order, which he obeys; by the second, if the real chief is honest then every honest part obeys his order. So the three real men satisfy both conditions against one liar, which Chapter VII denies.∎

Above the bound there is a method, the Repeating. It needs one auxiliary, the greater count: any rule that takes the accounts a guard holds and returns one, such that if most of them say the same thing, that is what it returns.

The Repeating, bearing m liars (1) The chief sends his order to every guard.
(2) Each guard takes what he received, or hold if nothing came, and — unless m is zero — acts as chief himself in the Repeating bearing m − 1 liars, putting that order about to the other guards.
(3) Each guard now holds his own account and one from each other guard, and obeys the greater count of them.

On this peak, bearing one liar, that is two glasses. If the chief is honest, each honest guard holds hold, hold and whatever the liar said, and the greater count is hold. If the chief lies, all three guards are honest, each repeats faithfully, and all three hold the same three accounts and reach the same answer. Nobody worked out who lied; he was outvoted, which is cheaper.

What the Repeating gives

Bearing m liars, it meets both conditions whenever there are more than 3m men and at most m of them lie.

Proof — that the Repeating meets both conditions above the bound
  1. Lemma. Bearing m liars, the method meets the second condition if there are more than 2k + m men and at most k liars. By induction on m: for m = 0 an honest chief's order arrives as sent. Assume it for m − 1.
  2. An honest chief sends v to all n − 1 guards. Each honest guard puts v about among n − 1 men bearing m − 1 liars, and n − 1 > 2k + (m − 1), so by the hypothesis every honest guard receives v from every honest guard.
  3. Since n − 1 > 2k, most guards are honest, so v is most of what each honest guard holds and the greater count returns it.
  4. The theorem, by induction on m. If the chief is honest, the lemma with k = m gives the second condition, and the first follows from it.
  5. If the chief lies, at most m − 1 guards lie, and there are more than 3m − 1 > 3(m − 1) guards, so the hypothesis applies to their repeatings: for each guard, any two honest guards end holding the same account of what he put about.
  6. So any two honest guards hold the same accounts in the same places, and the same greater count of them. The first condition holds; the second is vacuous, the chief being a liar.∎

Every further liar costs three more men and another glass of reports about reports. The trouble was that a plain slip says whatever its writer chose. What if a slip could not be altered?

Chapter X · Below

Deferring the Telling Arrival

The eighth chapter asked whether some arrival must eventually close the night. The answer is no: whichever arrival would have settled the matter, there is a way to take it and be open still.

Fix a two-gated standing and an arrival e that can happen at it. We are not going to prevent e; the birds are reliable. The question is only whether it must settle anything when it comes. Suppose every standing reached by holding e back and then allowing it were settled. Then somewhere two of them, one arrival f apart, are settled opposite ways. If f is another man's business, the lemma of separate folds says e and f commute, and one standing would be settled both ways. If f is the same man's business, let him go quiet, as a man does whose birds are in the pines, and let the other three finish; the lemma again shows both gates reachable from a standing that had finished. Neither case survives.

Deferring the telling arrival

From any two-gated standing, and for any arrival that can happen at it, the night can go on — without that arrival for a while, then with it — and arrive two-gated still.

No single tube is ever the one that has to settle the matter.

Proof — that deferring an arrival always leaves the night two-gated
  1. Let C be two-gated and e an arrival applicable there. Write 𝒞 for the standings reachable from C without allowing e, and 𝒟 for those got by allowing e at each standing of 𝒞. Suppose no member of 𝒟 is two-gated.
  2. Both settlements occur in 𝒟. C is two-gated, so some course from it ends north and another ends south. If such a course never allows e, its standings lie in 𝒞 and allowing e at the end gives a member of 𝒟 settled the same way; if it does allow e, the standing just after is already in 𝒟.
  3. So 𝒞 holds neighbours that split. 𝒞 is connected, so walking from the standing whose image is settled north to the one whose image is settled south gives C₀ and C₁ = f(C₀), one arrival apart, whose images D₀ and D₁ are settled opposite ways.
  4. Case one: f is another man's arrival. By the lemma of separate folds e and f commute, so D₁ = f(D₀). But everything reachable from the north-settled D₀ is settled north, and D₁ is settled south.
  5. Case two: f is the same man's arrival, say p's. Take a course σ from C₀ in which p never stirs and which does finish; permitted, since p may be the one silent man. Call the standing it reaches A.
  6. σ has no man in common with e or f, so by the lemma it may be run from D₀ and from D₁ as well, reaching e(A) and f then e applied to A — settled north and settled south respectively. So A is two-gated, though σ finished there.
  7. Both cases are impossible, so 𝒟 holds a two-gated standing.∎

The night can begin open, and no single arrival need close it. What kind of night do those two facts make, if a scheduler of birds used them without rest?

Chapter XI · Above

The Seal Rings

The ninth chapter asked what happens if a slip cannot be altered. Each man on the peak has a signet cut with his own number. A slip sent under seal is written in wax and stamped, and a man passing it on stamps his own beside, so hold : 1 : 2 is the chief's order as relayed by Number 2.

What a seal is assumed to do (a) An honest man's seal cannot be counterfeited, and tampering shows.
(b) Anyone can tell whose seal is whose.

Nothing is assumed about a liar's seal: liars may lend each other rings. And a seal cannot compel: a man can refuse to pass a slip on; he simply cannot alter it.

Each guard keeps a sheaf of the orders he has properly received, and a fixed standing rule turns a sheaf into one order, with an empty sheaf giving hold.

The Sealed Repeating, bearing m liars (1) The chief seals his order and sends it to every guard.
(2) A guard given a properly sealed order not yet in his sheaf puts it in; and if fewer than m guards' seals stand on it — the chief's not counted — he adds his own and sends it to every guard not already named on it.
(3) When no further slip can come, he obeys the standing rule applied to his sheaf.

Three men are now enough. If the chief lies, sealing hold to one guard and scatter to the other, each guard passes his across, both sheaves hold the same two orders, and the same standing rule sends both the same way. And each guard now holds two orders under the chief's seal, which could not both exist if the chief were honest: the chief has convicted himself, of lying and of nothing else, and only because he was careless enough to seal two orders.

How many liars can be borne? Any number, whatever the size of the party. Only the question gives out: with fewer than two men more than there are liars, too few honest men remain for agreement among them to mean anything. Where plain slips bought each liar with three more men, seals buy him with one more glass.

Proof — that the Sealed Repeating works against any number of liars
  1. Second condition. An honest chief seals one order and sends it to all, and no one can counterfeit his seal, so every honest guard's sheaf holds exactly that order and the standing rule returns it.
  2. First condition. Take the chief to be a liar. It is enough that if an honest guard i has an order in his sheaf, every honest guard j has it too; equal sheaves give equal answers.
  3. If i received it under the chief's seal alone, he sealed it and sent it to every other guard, so j has it.
  4. If it reached i carrying k guards' seals and j is among them, j had it already.
  5. If j is not among them and k < m, then i seals it and sends it to every guard not named on it, j included.
  6. If j is not among them and k = m: the chief is a liar, so at most m − 1 guards lie, so one of the m named on the slip is honest — and he, when he first received the order, sent it to every guard not then named on it, j among them.
  7. Nowhere were the men counted. The bound of Chapter IX belongs to unsealed slips, not to lying, and the seals dissolve it.∎
Chapter XII · Below

What Cannot Be Promised

The tenth chapter asked what kind of night the two facts make. Keep the four in a queue. Go to the man at its head; if a tube is waiting for him let him take in the one longest aloft, and if none is waiting let him walk to his perch and back. Choose the stretch before that arrival by the deferral, so that the night is two-gated after it. Send him to the back of the queue and go to the next.

Run that for ever. No man is starved of his turn; every tube is taken in at last. The night is entirely fair, and at every stage of it nothing has been settled.

What cannot be promised

There is no arrangement the four could have made — none at all, however ingenious — which guarantees that anybody ever goes to a gate.

Four misreadings are worth closing off. Nobody dies in it. The one permitted death was spent on the argument, not the hill; what limits them is the possibility of a death, which looks exactly like a slow bird. Nothing goes wrong. Nobody goes to a wrong gate, because nobody goes anywhere. One would have been enough. The demand was only that somebody go, and even that fails. It is not about this hill. Any number of men from two upward, any two answers: same conclusion.

What would have to change? Not the slowness of the birds, nor their disorder. The trouble is two things together: no longest flight, and a dead man looking exactly like a slow bird. Give them a longest flight, and six hours of silence means something. Or give them some report of who has died, one that in the end names every dead man and in the end stops naming at least one living one, and it is enough while more than half live. Or let every death happen before dusk, and a majority can manage.

None of these is on offer at the foot of the hill. The first of them is on offer at the top.

Chapter XIII

The Two Halves

The first chapter asked which trouble is worse, a friend who may be dead or a friend who may be lying. Set the two ends of the hill side by side. Above, agreement is had in a few glasses: four men bear one liar by plain slip, and under seal any number of liars is borne. Below, no arrangement whatever guarantees that anybody ever moves. The difference is not that the bandits are cleverer. It is that they have glasses to bound the flights between them, and the mercenaries do not.

And each end is short of exactly one distinction, the same shape of trouble twice over.

The two things that cannot be settled

Below. A man with an empty perch cannot tell whether the man in that tent is dead or whether the bird is in a pine. So he can never safely stop waiting, and nothing is ever concluded.

Above. A guard holding two accounts of one order that disagree cannot tell whether the chief lied to one of them or the peer is lying to him. So no single man's word can ever be trusted, and nobody ever learns who lied.

In both cases what is lost is smaller than it looks. Below, nothing goes wrong: the men are never in disagreement, only never finished. Above, nothing is discovered: the men agree, and are wrong together as readily as right together. The arrangements were not built to tell anybody who failed. They were built so that nobody needs to be told.

So which trouble is worse? The one without a clock. Lies can be outvoted when silence means something; a death cannot be told from a delay when nothing ever does.

The ideas, below. No deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system in which even one process may crash. The proof turns on bivalence: a configuration from which both decisions are still reachable. There is always a bivalent initial configuration, and from any bivalent configuration the step that would resolve the indecision can be deferred and then taken without resolving it — so a scheduler can build an admissible run that never decides. Three details carry the weight: the run the proof constructs contains no failure at all, the single permitted fault being spent on the argument; agreement and validity are never violated, only termination is lost; and the result bites even though the requirement is merely that some process decide. The obstruction is not slowness but the inability to distinguish a crashed process from a slow one. Relax it — bound the delays, even only eventually, or supply an imperfect oracle about who has failed — and consensus becomes solvable again; the paper's own §4 relaxes it by letting processes die only before the protocol begins, and gives a protocol that works whenever a majority are sound.

The ideas, above. A commander must get an order to n − 1 lieutenants so that IC1 all loyal lieutenants obey the same order and IC2 they obey his if he is loyal. IC2 gives IC1 only when the commander is loyal, and he may not be: that is the problem. With oral messages no solution exists for three generals against one traitor, because a loyal lieutenant cannot distinguish a lying commander from a lying peer; more generally nothing works below 3m + 1 generals, shown by having three generals each simulate a third of a hypothetical smaller solution, and settling for approximate agreement does not help, since it can be turned back into exact agreement. Above the bound, OM(m) works: the commander sends his value, each lieutenant relays what he got by OM(m − 1), and each takes the majority — one extra round per traitor tolerated. Unforgeable signatures change everything: SM(m) tolerates any number of traitors with no requirement at all on the number of generals, and three generals suffice against one. No assumption is made about a traitor's signature, so signatures prove what the loyal did and nothing about the rest, and a traitor can still withhold a message. What no algorithm gives is identification, the sole exception being a commander who signs two contradictory orders. And all of it rests on A3 — detecting absence — which in practice means bounded message time and clocks kept synchronised, itself a problem as hard as this one.
