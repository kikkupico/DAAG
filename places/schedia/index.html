Schedia and the Tholos

A city with laws of its own, governed from a round hall like the Chamber's, with a podium at its centre.

Read this first, and the book set in Schedia can be read on its own. It describes the island of Paxos, the city on its eastern arm, and the Tholos where its law is made.

↓ Scroll. Things unfold.

The Island of Paxos

Paxos is shaped like a Y. Two arms reach north and face each other across a bay: the western arm holds the Chamber of the Paxon Parliament, the eastern arm the city of Schedia and its Tholos. The arms join at a fork, where the hall of two doors stands, and below the fork the stem widens into the body of the island, the scholars' coast. The island road runs up the stem, forks, and runs out along both arms.

Paxos can read the sun. Unlike Arche in its trading season, the island tells time by day, and the Chamber has a meridian line. Word goes by messenger and by courier along the island's roads, and takes as long as it takes.

A City of Its Own

Schedia is a small city on the eastern arm, above a sheltered cove on the bay. Its name means raft. It was founded by settlers from a different mother city, who came by sea and brought their founders' laws with them, and the Parliament's decrees have never run on the eastern arm. That was ordinary Greek practice: the small island of Amorgos held three independent cities, each settled from elsewhere and each with its own laws.

So Paxos has two cities and two law scrolls. Each covers its own city, and the two never contradict each other. There is no quarrel between them for either to settle; Schedia simply keeps its law another way.

The Tholos

Schedia is governed from its Tholos, a round hall on a terrace above the town that faces the Chamber across the water. It is the same kind of hall. Its acoustics are just as poor, so nobody can be heard across it and everything goes by messenger, and its legislators serve part time, coming and going by its one door. The council has five legislators, Okios, Liskovia, Kleon, Melissa and Theron, each with a copy of the law scroll at a bench of their own. The Tholos has one thing the Chamber lacks: a podium at the centre.

Nobody can be heard from the podium either. It is an office, not a pulpit. Only the legislator who holds it may propose; the others keep their law scrolls on the benches round the drum and write down what the podium's messengers bring. Citizens at the doors send their petitions to the podium, and a petition that reaches a bench instead is sent back with the name of the holder. The podium stands where every bench is the same distance away, so its messengers run out from the centre, where in the Chamber they cross the floor in every direction.

A board hangs on the podium with a number and the name of the legislator who raised it, and every note the podium sends carries that number. When the podium falls silent, any legislator who notices may raise a higher board. Its new holder first gathers what a majority of the benches hold, so that nothing a majority knew is lost. A holder who stepped out and missed the new board can go on sending notes under the old one, but settles nothing: any majority they reach includes a legislator who has taken the new board and refuses the old number.

The law is kept in wax, not ink. Each legislator's tablets stay on their bench and are smoothed when they go home; only the bench's name and the last board it served under are cut into its stone. A legislator who steps out comes back to their tablets. One who goes home comes back to smooth wax and must be brought up to date from the others. If a majority went home at once, the law would be lost.

The name is Athenian. In Athens the Tholos was the round building of the prytaneis, the committee on duty in turn, with one member presiding.

What each part of this place has in common with the real Greek world, so that a reader can tell what is borrowed from what is made up. Close means the real thing is much like it; loose means it is borrowed and changed; invented means the setting made it up; anachronism means the real thing is of another age.
