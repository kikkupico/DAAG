Libraries Asking Whether to Wait

Libraries that each hold some of the island's facts and hear of the rest by courier, late and out of order, and a hall that sorts their questions into those that can be answered alone and those that must wait for everyone.

Read this first, and the book set in the hall can be read on its own. It says who brings questions to the hall, what carries the facts between them and what the couriers carry. The hall itself, at the fork of the island, comes last.

The Libraries and Their Questions

The questions brought to the hall come from the libraries of the scholars' coast. Each library holds some of the island's facts, which copies have been made, which works cite which, and none holds them all. A librarian answers from what his library holds and from what couriers have brought him.

Some librarians keep a roster, a list of every library on the coast. Others keep none, and a librarian with no roster cannot tell, until a letter arrives, whether he is alone on the island or one of many.

The assemblies stand at the other end of the question: the Parliament in the Chamber and the council in the Tholos, which settle things only once they have heard from a majority.

Couriers

Facts travel from the library that holds them to the libraries that need them by courier, and they arrive late, in batches or out of order. A courier may be stopped on the road, and while he is stopped nobody can say whether more is coming.

Nothing else carries word. No library can look into another's shelves, and no bell or beacon tells the coast that every courier has arrived.

Two Kinds of Question

Some questions only grow. Has at least one copy of this work been made? can be answered yes as soon as a single letter says so, and once it is yes it is yes for ever, whatever news comes later. A library can answer such a question from the letters it holds, with no roster and no waiting. A question of that kind needs no assembly, and it leaves by the scholars' door.

Other questions rest on absence. Is this every copy that has been made? or Has no one claimed this? can be answered only by hearing from everyone, since a single late courier could carry the letter that overturns the answer. A question of that kind needs everyone to wait on everyone, and it leaves by the door of the assemblies.

So what the couriers carry decides which door a question takes.

What a courier carriesWhat it says
A letter of factThat a copy of a work has been made, or that one work cites another. It only adds; no later letter takes it back.
A slip from a libraryMy shelf is empty, sent by each library whose shelf is. A librarian counting such slips needs a roster to know when the last has come.
Add and Strike noticesA work added to a reader's list, or struck from it, in any order.
A closing noticeThe list is closed, and here are the notices that came before it: a manifest. A library holding it waits only for the notices it names.

The Hall

Paxos is shaped like a Y. Two arms reach north and face each other across a bay: the western arm holds the Chamber of the Paxon Parliament, the eastern arm the city of Schedia and its Tholos. Below the fork the stem widens into the scholars' coast. The island road runs up the stem, forks, and runs out along both arms.

The hall of two doors stands at the fork, on common ground that belongs to neither city, as the Messon on Lesbos was the common sanctuary of that island's cities. It has two doors and no more. One faces north, up the bay between the arms, towards the Chamber and the Tholos, the two bodies on Paxos that assemble. The other faces south, down the stem, towards the scholars' coast, which does not.

The hall does not rule on anything. It sorts. Every question brought to it leaves by the door its own shape decides. Putting both cities and the scholars on one island makes the difference a walk: the scholars could take any question to an assembly, and the hall shows which questions need one.
