Distributed Algorithms of Ancient Greece

25 landmark papers in distributed computing explained using the same ancient Greece allegory employed by Leslie Lamport in his seminal paper, “The Part-Time Parliament (Paxos)”.

Every book reads two ways. Allegory tells the papers as stories of ancient Greece; Computing tells the same pages in the papers' own terms, with processes, messages and their figures. The Part-Time Parliament

Any two majorities share a legislator.Any two majorities intersect.

How can a group of machines agree on a sequence of commands when any of them may wander off at any time, and no one is in charge for long?

Lamport answered this himself, with the part-time legislators of the Greek island of Paxos. We present his paper as he wrote it, with interactive figures. It is the paper that inspired this entire project. Lamport ends it with the parliament's fall; the other books set that aside, and in them the parliament is still sitting.

What cannot be done

Four walls

Some books end at a proof that something cannot be done. Each wall comes down to two cases that look alike: what one post sees, the same on both nightsone process's view, the same in both runs.

The first wall

No plan is sure to finish.FLP.

When birds may take any time and one man may fall silent, no fixed plan can be sure that anyone ever commits. The night is never decided wrongly; it may simply never be decided. Three things get the camp through: a wind that drops, a coin in each tent, a slate of suspects.

Deterministic consensus in an asynchronous system with one crash cannot guarantee termination, though safety is never lost. Three approaches circumvent it: partial synchrony, randomization, failure detectors.

The second wall

Half cannot speak for all.Quorum bounds.

If half the camp may fall silent, a wind can hold every bird between the halves until each half commits alone, so more than half must live, with coins or slates alike; only a slate that never chalks one living man escapes. If men may lie, three cannot bear one liar: four can bear one, seven two. Seals lift that count only while silence can be timed; in the wind it takes four to bear one liar, sealed or not.

With crash faults, partial synchrony, randomization and eventually accurate detectors all need n ≥ 2t + 1 (with S, any number may crash). Byzantine agreement needs n ≥ 3m + 1 with oral messages; signatures remove the bound only in synchronous rounds; under partial synchrony n ≥ 3t + 1, signed or not.

From post 2:At p₂: a lying chiefa faulty commander looks like a lying peera faulty lieutenant

The third wall

Answer, or be right.CAP.

The last two crosses come from a wall first met at the foot. A tent cut off by hawks cannot both answer every question and answer as one. With a known flight, waiting a glass keeps the copies as one on every night no bird is lost; when birds may also be late without limit, not even then. So no librarian alone can stop two readers taking the last copy, or promise the newest edition.

The two unavailable guarantees come from CAP. Under message loss, a register cannot be both linearizable and available; with bounded delays, consistency survives in loss-free runs; asynchronously, not even then. Preventing lost update or write skew, and any recency guarantee, are therefore unavailable.

The fourth wall

An absence needs everyone.CALM.

A question that only grows needs no waiting and no roster; a question that rests on none, every or only must hear from everyone first. That is why an old notice of withdrawal can be thrown away only when every library has been heard from, why no table alone keeps a count above zero, and why nothing on the coast that only grows ever waits for an assembly.

Exactly the non-monotone computations, such as negation, aggregation, global invariants and garbage collection, need coordination; monotone ones run coordination-free, and so stay available under partition.

The series in one breath

Places that share no clock and talk only by messenger can still act as one. They order what happens by counting slips, not by reading the sun. They decide by waiting for most of them, never all, since any two majorities share a member, and every new attempt carries forward what most of them last held. Each liar costs three more men, unless seals expose him while time can be kept. No fixed plan is sure to finish when a silent man may only be slow, and no post cut off from the rest can both answer and be right; but whatever only grows can be merged in any order, with no one waiting at all.

Processes that share no clock and communicate only by messages can still behave as one. Logical and vector clocks order events by causality, and linearizability defines what behaving as one copy means. Consensus waits for a quorum, never for all, because any two majorities intersect, and every new ballot, view or term carries forward the latest value a quorum accepted. Byzantine faults need n ≥ 3f + 1, unless signatures and synchronous rounds remove the bound. FLP rules out guaranteed termination for deterministic consensus with one crash in an asynchronous system, and CAP rules out availability with linearizability under partition; but monotone computations, such as CRDTs, need no coordination at all.
