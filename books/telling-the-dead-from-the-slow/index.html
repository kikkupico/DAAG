Telling the Dead from the Slow

In an asynchronous network, a computer that has crashed looks identical to one whose messages are merely running slow—and that single ambiguity makes agreement impossible. What if each machine had an imperfect detector that guessed which machines had crashed? How wrong can those guesses be before agreement becomes impossible again?

To find out, we return to the four Greek mercenaries at the foot of Mount Phyle, where each man keeps a chalk slate of the tents he suspects have fallen silent—and discover how little those slates need to get right for the camp to agree.

After Tushar Deepak Chandra and Sam Toueg, Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems, 1996

First, in machine terms

A guess about who has crashed

When messages can be delayed without bound, a crashed machine looks identical to a slow one—so a machine waiting for a reply can never know whether to keep waiting or move on.

What if we equip each machine with a failure detector—a local module that maintains a list of machines it currently suspects have crashed? Crucially, the detector is allowed to be unreliable: it can falsely suspect a slow working machine, and then remove that suspicion later when a message arrives. Tushar Deepak Chandra and Sam Toueg (1996) asked a foundational question: instead of baking clock and network timing bounds into our algorithms, how little accuracy does a failure detector need to make consensus solvable?

  • If at least one working machine is never falsely suspected (Class S): consensus can be solved regardless of how many machines crash (even if all but one crash).
  • If at least one working machine is only eventually spared from false suspicion (Class ◇S): consensus can be solved as long as a strict majority of the machines remain working—and falsehoods before that eventual moment can only delay a decision, never cause a wrong one.

By judging a detector purely by the properties of its suspect lists—not by how it measures time—we can write clean, time-free consensus algorithms. We tell the story with four mercenaries, their ravens, and a chalk slate at each tent.

The foot of Mount Phyle

Four tents, out of sight of one another

Four mercenaries are camped at the foot of Mount Phyle, one to a tent, hidden from one another by the folds of the hillside and under nobody's command.* Bandits hold the summit above them.

Only ravensThe four communicate only by raven. Every raven eventually arrives, but there is no upper bound on how long a flight may take.
Nobody liesThe mercenaries are honest comrades: no one ever falsifies a message, so whatever a raven carries is true.
Any man may dieAny of the four might be killed during the night, leaving his tent permanently silent without any raven to announce his death.
Three demands, one change

A slate at every tent

Before dawn, all four mercenaries must either attack the hill together or hold their ground together. Each man formed an initial sighting at dusk, and committing is final: once a man steps out of his tent to attack or hold, he leaves his ravens behind for the night.

Whatever arrangement they use to settle on a single plan must satisfy three demands:

First demand

No two commit differently

No two of the four ever commit to different answers (agreement).

Second demand

Only a sighted answer

A mercenary may only commit to an answer that at least one of the four actually sighted at dusk (validity).

Third demand

Every living man commits

Every man who has not been killed eventually commits to a plan (termination).

As we saw in The Limits of Agreement, if ravens can take arbitrarily long and nothing more is given, no arrangement can satisfy all three demands.* The stumbling block is a silent tent: a mercenary waiting for a raven from Tent 4 cannot tell whether Number 4 is dead or his bird is merely delayed in the pines.

Without help, both choices fail: waiting forever leaves the camp paralyzed if Number 4 is dead, while giving up early risks acting without a living comrade whose bird was just about to land.

What if each mercenary had an imperfect guide to tell him when to stop waiting? That is the single addition in this book. Beside each tent stands a slate (a failure detector) with a row for each of the other tents. Whenever a tent is suspected of being dead, its row on the slate is chalked; if the suspicion passes, the chalk mark can be rubbed out again. A mercenary waiting for a raven from a tent simply waits until either the raven lands or that tent's row is chalked on his slate.

Crucially, the slates at different tents need not agree with one another, and they are often wrong: a slate may chalk a living comrade whose bird is merely slow, or leave a dead man's row blank for a while. How reliable do these slates have to be before the four mercenaries can reach agreement?

Two ways to go wrong

How a slate may be wrong

Any slate can make only two kinds of mistakes: it can fail to chalk a dead man, or it can falsely chalk a living man. So we judge a slate by two promises—how well it chalks the dead (completeness), and how well it spares the living (accuracy).

Try keeping a slate yourself below. You are at Tent 1, and some tents have sent no raven yet. Chalk the tents you suspect are dead, then reveal the night to see which of the two mistakes you made.

Mark a slate you are at tent 1

The dead

Chalking the dead

Every man who is killed is eventually chalked permanently (completeness)—either on every living man's slate (strong), or on at least one living man's slate (weak).

The living

Sparing the living

Living men are not falsely chalked (accuracy)—either all living men or at least one living man, and either from dusk onward or only eventually.

A warning

Chalking the dead is worth nothing alone

A slate that simply chalks every tent from dusk onward permanently chalks every dead man—yet conveys zero information!

That third card is worth pausing over. If all you ask of a slate is that it eventually chalks the dead, you can build one effortlessly: just chalk every tent at dusk and never rub the marks out! A mercenary who relied on such a slate would never wait for anyone. What gives a slate real power is pairing completeness with some guarantee of sparing the living.

Four strengths of sparing

Eight kinds of slate

We can ask a slate to spare the living in four strengths, from strictest to loosest. Combined with the two ways of chalking the dead (by every living man, or by at least one living man), they form a grid of eight kinds of slates:

  • Never chalked (Strong accuracy). No living man is ever chalked by anyone at any point in the night.
  • One never chalked (Weak accuracy). There is at least one man who lives through the night whom nobody ever chalks, from dusk onward.
  • In the end, never chalked (Eventual strong accuracy). Mistakes may happen early on, but there comes a point in the night after which no living man is chalked by anyone.
  • In the end, one never chalked (Eventual weak accuracy). Mistakes may happen early on, and even later most living men may still be falsely chalked—all that is promised is that from some unknown moment onward, at least one living man is never chalked by anyone.

Copying marks

Why is "some living man chalks the dead" just as good as "every living man chalks the dead"? Because the mercenaries can share their chalk marks by raven!

Suppose each man keeps a second slate—his copy—and repeatedly sends ravens to every other tent listing the marks on his original slate. Whenever a mercenary receives such a raven from Tent q, he does two things on his copy: he adds all the marks that Tent q reported, and he rubs out any mark against Tent q itself (since Tent q was obviously alive to send the bird!).

Try this below. Tent 4 is dead, and among the three living tents only Tent 1's original slate has chalked Tent 4, while Tents 1 and 2 also falsely chalk living comrades at first. Send rounds of ravens and watch how every living man's copy comes to chalk Tent 4 while sparing the living.

Rounds of birds tent 4 is dead

See why copying works so cleanly: once Tent 4 dies, it stops sending ravens, so after its last bird in flight lands, nobody ever rubs Tent 4 out of a copy. Meanwhile, the one living man whose original slate permanently chalked Tent 4 keeps broadcasting that mark, so every living man's copy permanently chalks Tent 4! At the same time, copying preserves whichever accuracy guarantee the original slates had: if nobody's original slate ever chalks Tent 2, no copy ever will; and if all original slates eventually stop chalking Tent 2, then because Tent 2 is alive and keeps sending ravens, Tent 2's own birds rub out any leftover false marks against Tent 2 on everyone's copy.

That means any weak slate (W or ◇W) can be automatically upgraded into a strong one (S or ◇S). And since a perfect slate (P) is already a special case of a strong slate (S), and ◇P is a special case of ◇S, we only need to design consensus protocols for two classes of slates: strong (S) and in the end strong (◇S)!

Let us start with a strong (S) slate, where at least one living man is never chalked from dusk onward—though nobody knows which man he is.

However many die

One man never chalked

Suppose the mercenaries have strong (S) slates: every dead man is eventually chalked on every slate, and there is at least one living man—say Number 2—whom nobody ever chalks from dusk onward. Even though nobody knows that Tent 2 is the unchalked one, the four can reach agreement no matter how many of them die—even if three of the four are killed!

How? Each mercenary keeps a four-row sheet of sightings, one row per tent, initially containing only his own sighting in his own row and blanks (`—`) for the other three. The protocol runs in three short stages to guarantee that every surviving mercenary ends up with the exact same sheet:

1 · Spreading (3 rounds)

Relay newly learned sightings

In each of 3 rounds (n − 1 rounds for n tents), every man sends all tents any sightings he learned for the first time in the previous round (in round 1, his own sighting). He waits for a bird from every tent that is not currently chalked on his slate, and adds any new sightings to his sheet.

2 · Comparing (1 round)

Cross out missing rows

Each man sends his entire four-row sheet to every tent and waits for sheets from all unchalked tents. If a row is blank in any sheet he receives, he blanks that row in his own sheet too (taking the intersection of the sheets).

3 · Committing

Pick the first filled row

Each man looks down his finished sheet in order (row 1, row 2, row 3, row 4) and commits to the answer in the first row that is not blank.

Scroll through the night below. Tent 4 is killed in round 1 after its bird reaches only Tent 3, and the slates falsely chalk living comrades in rounds 1 and 2—except for Tent 2, which is never chalked by anyone.

1 · At dusk

Each sheet holds one row

At dusk, each man's sheet holds only his own sighting (A for attack or H for hold). Tent 2 will never be chalked by anyone tonight, though nobody knows that. Tent 4 is about to be killed.

2 · Round one

A bird reaches only one tent

Tent 4's round-1 bird reaches only Tent 3 before Number 4 is killed. Meanwhile, Tent 1 falsely chalks Tent 3 (and moves on without waiting for Tent 3's bird), and Tent 3 falsely chalks Tent 1. Only Tent 3 has learned Tent 4's sighting.

3 · Round two

Chalk hides a sighting

In round 2, both Tents 1 and 2 falsely chalk Tent 3 and do not wait for its bird—so Tent 4's sighting, which only Tent 3 was relaying in round 2, fails to reach Tents 1 and 2! Tent 3 waits for everyone and learns Tent 1's sighting.

4 · Round three

Nobody is chalked

In round 3, nothing new is learned. At the end of the three spreading rounds, Tents 1 and 2 have rows 1, 2, and 3 filled, while Tent 3 has all four rows filled—a discrepancy!

5 · Comparing

One row is blanked

Now comes the comparing round: everyone broadcasts their full sheet. Because nobody ever chalks Tent 2, everyone waits for Tent 2's sheet—and since row 4 is blank on Tent 2's sheet, Tent 3 blanks row 4 on its own sheet! Every surviving sheet now matches Tent 2's sheet.

6 · Committing

All three commit alike

Each surviving man commits to the first non-blank row on his sheet: row 1, attack. All three agree on a genuine dusk sighting, despite a death and repeated false chalk marks!

Why does every surviving sheet end up identical to Tent 2's sheet? Two halves make the magic work. First, because nobody ever chalks Tent 2, every living man waits for Tent 2's raven in every single round. That guarantees that by the end of the 3 spreading rounds, every sighting that reached Tent 2 has also reached every other surviving man (if Tent 2 learned a sighting in round 1 or 2, Tent 2 relayed it in the next round and everyone waited for Tent 2's bird; if Tent 2 only learned it in round 3, that sighting had already passed through the other three tents along a chain of length 3!). Thus, after spreading, every surviving sheet contains at least all the rows filled in Tent 2's sheet.

Second, in the comparing round, every surviving man again waits for Tent 2's sheet and crosses out any row that is blank in Tent 2's sheet—while no row that is filled in Tent 2's sheet is ever crossed out, because every surviving sheet already has that row filled! Thus, after comparing, every surviving sheet is identical to Tent 2's sheet, and at least row 2 (Tent 2's own sighting) is guaranteed to be non-blank.

A strong slate (Class S) is remarkably powerful, since it tolerates any number of deaths. Requiring that one living man is never falsely chalked from the very start of the night, however, is a tall order when winds blow early in the evening. What if the slates only stop chalking that one living man eventually (Class ◇S)?

Slates right only in the end

In the end, one man spared

With an in the end strong (◇S) slate, every living mercenary might be falsely chalked by everyone else for the first several hours of the night. Only after some unknown point in time is a single living man left unchalked. Under this weak guarantee, the four mercenaries can still reach agreement—provided more than half of them remain alive (at most one death out of four).

Why can't we use the four-round sheet protocol here? Because that protocol relied on having an unchalked man during rounds 1 through 4, and with ◇S the slates might still be chalking everyone during those rounds! Instead, the mercenaries run an open-ended sequence of rounds with a rotating turn owner (Tent 1 in round 1, Tent 2 in round 2, Tent 3 in round 3, Tent 4 in round 4, Tent 1 in round 5, and so on), just as they did on the windy nights of Agreeing When Messages Run Late.* Each mercenary maintains a current estimate (starting as his dusk sighting) together with the round number in which he last adopted an owner's proposal. Each round has four steps:

  • (1) Every man sends the current round's owner his current estimate and the round number in which he last adopted it.
  • (2) The round's owner waits to receive estimates from three men (a majority of the four, including himself), picks the estimate with the most recent round number, and sends that estimate to every tent as his proposal.
  • (3) Every man waits until either the owner's proposal arrives or his slate chalks the owner. If the proposal arrives first, he updates his estimate to the owner's proposal, records the current round number as its timestamp, and replies agreed (ack) to the owner. If his slate chalks the owner first, he replies not agreed (nack) without changing his estimate.
  • (4) The owner waits for three replies. If all three say agreed, the owner broadcasts a parting bird announcing that the estimate is decided! Otherwise, everyone simply moves on to the next round.
  • Committing. Any man who receives a parting bird relays it to every other tent and commits to the answer it names.

Step through the rounds below. During the first two rounds, the slates falsely chalk living owners so the rounds abort safely; from round 3 onward, one living man is spared.

Rounds by turns choose a night

Choose a night.

Why can two men never commit to different answers, no matter how badly the slates lie? Suppose round r is the earliest round in which an owner receives three agreed replies—say for hold. Because those three men replied agreed in round r, all three of them updated their estimate to hold and stamped it with round r. Now consider any later round's owner: in Step 2, that owner must wait for estimates from three of the four tents. Because any two groups of three out of four must overlap in at least two tents, the new owner is guaranteed to hear from at least one of the three men who adopted hold in round r! And because no estimate before round r can have a timestamp as high as r, the most recently stamped estimate in round r + 1 (and by induction in every round after r) must be hold. Once three men say agreed in any round, the estimate is permanently locked for the rest of the night!

Why does every living man eventually commit? Because at most one man dies, at least three men are alive to send estimates in Step 1 and replies in Step 3, and nobody ever gets stuck waiting on a dead owner in Step 3 because every dead man is eventually chalked on every slate. Then, once the moment arrives after which one living man (say Tent q) is never chalked by anyone, wait until the turn rotation reaches Tent q's round: Tent q gathers three estimates, sends its proposal to everyone, nobody chalks Tent q so all living men wait for the proposal and reply agreed, and Tent q receives three agreed replies and sends out the parting bird!

Notice the beauty of this separation: while the slates are making mistakes early in the night, false chalk marks can cause a round to abort with not agreed, costing time—but an aborted round never sends a parting bird and never breaks safety.

Why did ◇S require more than half the camp to stay alive, whereas S tolerated up to three deaths out of four? Could a cleverer algorithm for ◇S—or even for an in the end perfect (◇P) slate—tolerate two deaths out of four?

Would every arrangement need it?

Why more than half, then

Requiring more than half the camp to survive is not a quirk of the rotating-turn algorithm—it is a hard law for every algorithm whose slates are only eventually accurate, even if the slates are in the end perfect (◇P).

Why? Because an "eventual" guarantee allows the slates to be wrong for as long as it takes two isolated halves of the camp to commit on their own! Step through the three nights below to see the trap.

Three nights pairs: tents 1 and 2, tents 3 and 4

Each line is one tent, with time moving outward from the center. A hollow dot is a man who has not committed; a filled dot is one who has (blue for hold, red for attack). A cross marks a dead man.

On the third night, nobody is dead, but every raven flying between {1, 2} and {3, 4} is delayed in the pines while each pair's slates falsely chalk the other pair. Because the protocol must tolerate two deaths, Tents 1 and 2 must eventually commit to hold (say by time A), and Tents 3 and 4 must eventually commit to attack (say by time B). Only after both pairs have committed do the delayed ravens arrive and the slates rub out all their chalk marks—satisfying the definition of in the end perfect (◇P)! Yet the camp has already split in two.

Notice the exact contrast with a strong (S) slate: with S, at least one living man is never chalked from dusk onward, preventing the camp from ever splitting into two halves that completely chalk each other. Once accuracy holds only eventually, every decision must wait for a strict majority of the camp so that any two deciding groups are forced to overlap.

Proof · that slates in the end perfect cannot carry the four past two deaths

Theorem 5. Consensus cannot be solved using a failure detector of class ◇P if f ≥ n/2, where f is the number of processes that may crash. Here n = 4 and f = 2; the three nights are the runs R₀, R₁ and RA, and A and B are times defined in the steps.

  1. Suppose some arrangement does. Divide the four into pairs: Numbers 1 and 2, Numbers 3 and 4.
  2. The first night. All sighted hold. Numbers 3 and 4 are dead from dusk, and the other two chalk them all night. That slate is in the end perfect. Numbers 1 and 2 commit, within some time A, and to hold, since only hold was sighted.
  3. The second night. The same with pairs and answers swapped: Numbers 3 and 4 commit to attack within some time B.
  4. The third night. Numbers 1 and 2 sighted hold, Numbers 3 and 4 attack. Nobody dies. Every bird between the pairs sits in the pines until after A and B, and until then each pair chalks the other; after that, all birds arrive and all chalk is rubbed out. That slate is in the end perfect too.
  5. Until then each pair sees exactly what it saw on its own night, and commits: one pair to hold, the other to attack. That contradicts the first demand.∎
Granted, not earned

What the slate does not say

Where does a reliable slate come from? In a completely asynchronous network where ravens have no bound on flight time at all, no mercenary can build even an in-the-end-weak (◇W) slate—for if he could, the camp could solve consensus on a purely asynchronous night, contradicting The Limits of Agreement!*

A failure detector is therefore a clean way of packaging a timing assumption about the outside world into a purely logical interface: the algorithm asks only what the slate's marks eventually get right, without caring how the slate was built.

How to build an in-the-end-perfect (◇P) slate on a windy night. On a night where the wind eventually drops (Agreeing When Messages Run Late),* each mercenary can easily maintain a ◇P slate using a sandglass. Every man periodically sends heartbeat ravens to every other tent. If a man's sandglass runs out with no bird from Tent q, he chalks Tent q on his slate. If a delayed bird from Tent q lands later on, he rubs out the chalk mark against Tent q and increases the timeout he allows Tent q next time! Try watching one tent with this rule below, first when the watched man is alive and then when he is killed.

Each cell is a time step moving left to right. Blue cells mark the windy part of the night when birds can run arbitrarily late. A dot is an arriving bird, a cross is a chalk mark against the watched tent, and a loop is a mark rubbed out (which increases the timeout for that tent).

While the wind blows, slow birds cause false chalk marks that lengthen the timeout; once the wind drops, the timeout is big enough that a living man is never chalked again.

See how the adaptive timeout works: every time a slow bird causes a false chalk mark, rubbing it out makes the watcher more patient with that tent in the future. Once the wind drops, a living man's birds arrive within a bounded time, so after at most a few more adjustments the watcher's timeout exceeds that bound and he never falsely chalks that living comrade again! Meanwhile, a dead man stops sending birds altogether, so his timeout eventually expires for the last time and he stays chalked forever.

Three final observations complete the picture:

If the slate breaks every promise

Safety still holds

In the rotating-turn algorithm for ◇S, even if the slates go haywire forever—chalking the living endlessly or never chalking the dead—two men will still never commit to different answers. Bad slates can only stall termination, never break agreement.

The weakest possible slate

In the end weak (◇W)

Because copying upgrades ◇W to ◇S, an in-the-end-weak slate (with a living majority) is sufficient to solve consensus. In a companion paper that same year, Chandra, Hadzilacos, and Toueg proved that ◇W is also necessary—the weakest failure detector that can solve consensus!

Agreeing on a sequence of orders

Atomic broadcast

Chandra and Toueg also showed that agreeing on a single choice (consensus) and agreeing on the exact order of a stream of messages (atomic broadcast) are equivalent in asynchronous systems: either problem can be built directly out of a solution to the other, using the exact same slates.

A failure detector can make mistakes repeatedly and still make consensus solvable: if one correct process is never suspected, consensus survives any number of crashes; if that holds only eventually, consensus survives as long as a majority of processes remain alive.

Instead of reasoning directly about clocks and network delays, failure detectors isolate the exact information needed to tell the dead from the slow.

The paper behind this book. Tushar Deepak Chandra and Sam Toueg, Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems. Journal of the ACM 43, 2 (March 1996), 225–267. Defines the eight classes of failure detectors, the reduction from weak to strong completeness, the consensus algorithms for S and ◇S, the majority lower bound for ◇P, and the equivalence of consensus and atomic broadcast.

The ideas, plainly. Deterministic consensus is impossible in an asynchronous system if even a single process can crash, because a crashed process cannot be distinguished from a slow one. Chandra and Toueg factor that obstacle into an abstract module at each process—an unreliable failure detector that outputs a set of processes currently suspected to have crashed. Detectors are specified purely by two axiomatic properties: completeness (crashed processes are eventually permanently suspected, either by every correct process or by at least one correct process) and accuracy (correct processes are spared from false suspicion, either all of them or at least one of them, and either perpetually from the start or only eventually).

Because processes can gossip their suspect lists and clear any process from which they receive a message, weak completeness reduces to strong completeness while preserving accuracy—leaving four core classes (P, S, ◇P, ◇S). With class S (some correct process is never suspected), a four-phase relay protocol solves consensus regardless of how many processes crash (f < n). With class ◇S (eventually some correct process is never suspected), a rotating-coordinator protocol solves consensus whenever a strict majority of processes are correct (f < n/2); safety holds unconditionally even if the detector misbehaves forever, and termination occurs as soon as the detector stabilizes. A partition argument shows that f < n/2 is necessary even for ◇P.

Failure detectors separate the why of timing from the how of agreement: in a partially synchronous system, heartbeats with adaptive timeouts implement ◇P, while the consensus protocol itself can be written and proved without mentioning clocks or message delay bounds at all.
