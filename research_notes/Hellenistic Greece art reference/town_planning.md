# Hellenistic Greek Town Planning and Urban Layout

Method note: fetched summaries were thin. Cited findings are what sources returned. Material from general scholarly background is placed under Inferences and marked "(background, not re-verified)". Dimensions are mostly omitted per brief; where a source gave one it is used only to convey relative scale. Period flags: C = Classical, H = Hellenistic, R = Roman.

## 1. Grid planning (Hippodamian), insulae, street hierarchy, terraced hillside cities

### Takeaway
Hellenistic new foundations and rebuilds use an orthogonal grid with a few broad main streets crossing many narrow ones, producing long narrow house blocks. On slopes the grid is kept and absorbed by terracing, with the narrow streets turning into stairs.

### Cited Findings
- Hippodamus of Miletus (5th c. BC, so a Classical figure) is the credited father of the grid ("broad, straight streets cutting one another at right angles"); Piraeus, Thurii and Rhodes are tied to him, and his principles are said to influence Halicarnassus, Alexandria, Antioch — [Wikipedia: Hippodamus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippodamus_of_Miletus)
- Priene (rebuilt 4th c. BC, occupied through the Hellenistic period): rectangular grid on a steep slope, aligned to compass points; six broad E-W streets (about 6 m) crossed by fifteen narrow N-S streets (about 3 m), i.e. main streets roughly twice the width of cross streets — [Wikipedia: Priene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priene); [orientation per search summary of UNESCO tentative list](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6348/)
- Priene: about 80 insulae each holding about 8 houses; about one third of houses had an indoor latrine; population 4,000-6,000; the site climbs the Mycale escarpment with extensive terracing and staircases; agora is the commercial centre with bouleuterion and prytaneion beside it; theatre, gymnasium and Athena sanctuary sit on higher terraces; aqueduct from mountain springs plus visible drains — [Wikipedia: Priene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priene)
- Pergamon (H capital): built on a high mesa; hairpin-turning streets, broad streets plus narrow connectors; upper acropolis holds altar, theatre ("one of the steepest"), Athena sanctuary, library, palace area; lower city has a three-terrace gymnasium, Hera/Demeter sanctuary, several agoras, houses on SE and W slopes; the agora lies on the lowest terrace framed by stoas — [Wikipedia: Pergamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pergamon); [Wikipedia: Hellenistic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_art)
- Hellenistic building plans "conformed to the natural setting" (Pergamon, Antioch) rather than levelling it — [Wikipedia: Hellenistic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_art)
- Kos town was founded 366 BC (C) in a Hippodamian grid after synoecism from Astypalaia; very large rectangular agora; terraced Asklepieion; stadium and gymnasium; Ptolemaic-influenced in H — [Wikipedia: Kos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kos)
- Rhodes (founded 408 BC, C) is credited with a Hippodamian water-supply and sewer network; Colossus stood near the harbour entrance (broadly accepted but its exact site is disputed) until the 226 BC earthquake — [Wikipedia: Rhodes city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes_(city))
- Alexandria (founded 331 BC): Hippodamian grid by Dinocrates of Rhodes with underground canals under streets; two colonnaded avenues crossing near the Sema; Canopic Street E-W; Heptastadion causeway creates the Great Harbour (east) and Eunostos with the Kibotos basin (west); quarters Rhakotis (Egyptian), Brucheum (Greek/royal), Jewish quarter; Pharos lighthouse, Museum/Library, Serapeum — [Wikipedia: Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria). Note: the Caesareum and obelisks listed there are Roman-period (R).
- Vitruvius (Roman-period text, Augustan, describing Hellenistic practice): streets and alleys should be laid on lines between wind quarters so winds are broken at the street mouths — [Vitruvius I.6 via Perseus](http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=vitr.+1&lang=original)
- Delos (Athenian-controlled free port 166 BC onward, H/Roman transition): grid-like regularity in northern districts with covered drains; the Theatre Quarter grew "ungoverned, if not confused" along winding Theatre Street; Stadium Quarter has colonnaded streets; rich and modest houses mixed in every quarter — [EFA: Delos, ville et habitat](https://www.efa.gr/delos-la-ville-et-l-habitat-3/?lang=en)
- Halicarnassus (Mausolus, 4th c. BC, C): paved streets and squares, theatre, fortified palace commanding harbour and inland hills, walls and watchtowers — [Wikipedia: Halicarnassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halicarnassus)
- Miletus rebuilt on a Hippodamian grid; multiple harbours (the detail on its four harbours and Lion Harbour was not returned by the fetch) — [Wikipedia: Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miletus)

### Inferences
- Visual rule for artists: not every Aegean town is a grid. New foundations and rebuilt cities (Priene, Kos, Rhodes, Alexandria, Miletus) are gridded; old island towns and organic growth (Delos Theatre Quarter) wind. Mixed is plausible. (inferred from findings above)
- On steep sites the long axis of blocks follows the contour and cross streets become stairways (Priene). (inferred)
- Background, not re-verified: Priene's blocks are about 1.5-2 plethra by 1 plethron, i.e. long and narrow; Miletus's grid is subdivided into districts with separate agoras (north and south), and the Lion Harbour with its two lion statues sits at the centre; Rhodes was famously described as theatre-shaped, rising in tiers from its harbours, with a hilltop acropolis (Monte Smith) holding temples of Apollo, Zeus-Athena and a stadium/theatre/gymnasium group; Alexandria's 'streets' were laid so the Etesian winds ventilate them.

### Gaps
- Exact block dimensions and street-width tables deliberately not collected. Miletus harbour detail and Rhodes acropolis details not retrieved from a citable source.

## 2. Public core: agora, stoas, altars, statues, fountains, sanctuaries, theatre, gymnasium

### Takeaway
The agora is a rectangular or trapezoidal open court framed by stoas (porches with shops or offices behind), cluttered with altars, statues on bases, trees and fountains. Theatres are cut into hill slopes with a view, gymnasia sit on flat edge-of-town ground or a terrace, and the main sanctuary takes the best-visible high terrace.

### Cited Findings
- Agora: colonnades and stoas surrounding a space "decorated with fountains, trees, and statues"; merchants kept stalls and shops under arcades; craftspeople clustered nearby — [Wikipedia: Agora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agora)
- Ancient Greek towns had an agora surrounded by a stoa, public fountains for household water, bouleuterion, theatres typically on hillsides, gymnasia, palaestrae, stadia; public buildings were sited to relate to each other architecturally — [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture)
- Priene: council house and prytaneion next to the agora; theatre, gymnasium and Athena sanctuary on higher terraces — [Wikipedia: Priene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priene)
- Pergamon: theatre steeply cut into the acropolis flank with a long narrow terrace and altar/temple beyond; gymnasium in three terraces below — [Wikipedia: Pergamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pergamon)
- Delos theatre (3rd c. BC) about 5,000 seats, surrounded by houses, a large hotel and small sanctuaries, with a notable cistern — [search summary of Delos sources](https://ancient-greece.org/archaeology/delos/)
- Kos agora described as one of the biggest in the ancient world, a very elongated rectangle — [Wikipedia: Kos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kos)

### Inferences
- Background, not re-verified: stoas are single or two-aisle porches with a Doric outer colonnade and often Ionic inner columns; shops or rooms open off the back; two-storey stoas at Pergamon and Athens (Stoa of Attalos, H). Agoras were kept free of carts at times, with horoi (boundary stones) marking the edge. Altars (often of Zeus Agoraios or Twelve Gods) stand in the open court; honorific statues and monument columns stand on bases along stoa fronts. Fountain houses (a covered basin with a columned front) sit at street corners or on the agora edge. Gymnasium: a peristyle palaestra with a long covered running track (xystos) and a bath, often near a river or at the city edge. Hellenistic stoas often make a continuous framed edge with a street running behind.

## 3. Harbours, walls, towers, gates, water, drains, paving, zoning, necropoleis

### Takeaway
Ports are held by moles and tower-flanked entrances; walls follow the ridge with towers at intervals and defended gates; water comes by aqueduct, cistern and fountain; cemeteries line the roads outside the gates.

### Cited Findings
- Vitruvius on harbours: use natural headlands where possible, build colonnades or shipsheds around them, set towers on both sides from which chains can be drawn across the mouth; where no natural shelter exists, build moles or embankments to form an enclosing harbour — [Vitruvius V.12 via Perseus search result](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Ten_Books_on_Architecture/Book_V) (Roman author, Hellenistic practice)
- Halicarnassus: harbour dredged and dredged sand used to build breakwaters before the channel — [Wikipedia: Halicarnassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halicarnassus)
- Alexandria: Heptastadion causeway forms two harbours; Pharos lighthouse (see section 1) — [Wikipedia: Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandria)
- Pergamon water: nine pipes bringing a large daily volume, including the Madradag aqueduct over 40 km (pressurised pipeline across a valley, famous as an engineering feat) — [Wikipedia: Pergamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pergamon)
- Priene: mountain-spring aqueduct, street drains still visible, many houses with latrines — [Wikipedia: Priene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priene)
- Delos: covered drains in northern districts; theatre cistern (see sections 1-2); ground-floor shops on main thoroughfares — [EFA](https://www.efa.gr/delos-la-ville-et-l-habitat-3/?lang=en)
- Paved streets and squares: Halicarnassus; Priene and Paestum described as "regular grid of paved streets" — [Wikipedia: Halicarnassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halicarnassus); [Wikipedia: Ancient Greek architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture)
- Hellenistic Delos blind-walled courtyard houses, peristyles for the wealthy, multi-storey, local gneiss/granite/marble; mixed wealth in each quarter — [EFA](https://www.efa.gr/delos-la-ville-et-l-habitat-3/?lang=en)

### Inferences
- Background, not re-verified: walls are ashlar (isodomic) or polygonal masonry over a socle with mudbrick or stone above; square or semi-circular towers project from the curtain at intervals, with catapult platforms in H; gates are set in re-entrant ("tenaille") or flanked-by-tower arrangements; the circuit rides the ridge and encloses far more than is built (Messene, Aigosthena, Priene's walls reaching up to the acropolis ridge). Closed military harbours (kothon style) had narrow entrances, shipsheds (neosoikoi) with sloping slips in rows, and chain-tower mouths (Rhodes, Carthage-type; Piraeus Zea and Mounychia in C). Cisterns (bottle or rectangular, plastered) are standard on dry islands; cisterns under or beside houses on Delos. Potteries and metalworking tend to sit near the edge or near water, downwind of the agora; commercial-harbour warehouses and shops cluster at the waterfront. Necropoleis line roads leaving the gates, with stelai, small naiskoi and monumental tombs, outside the walls. Roman: monumental colonnaded streets, aqueduct arcades, arches—avoid these in a Hellenistic scene. Colonnaded main streets in Alexandria and Antioch are partly H, partly R (uncertain).

### Gaps
- Gate and tower typology, zoning of craft quarters, necropolis placement: no source fetched; flagged as unverified background. Paving materials not documented.

## 4. Rural land: chora, farmsteads, terraces, towers, roads, boundary stones, rural sanctuaries

### Takeaway
The countryside is a patchwork of terraced slopes with isolated tower-farms (court, house, tower) on islands as well as mainland, linked by tracks, with sanctuaries and boundary markers dotted around.

### Cited Findings
- Basic farm elements: tower (pyrgos), courtyard (aule), house (oikia), in varying combinations; tower complexes in Sounion studied as "country estates" of the wealthy; similar rural patterns on Keos, Thasos, Melos — [Search result summarising Young/Attica and Oxbow](https://www.oxbowbooks.com/9781785706400/the-ancient-greek-farmstead/); [Island Towers: Thasos, BSA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-of-the-british-school-at-athens/article/abs/island-towers-the-case-of-thasos/1A51F6EA8964B6F9A4C4E01E9FE4277B)
- Thasos towers: some as danger signals to sailors, some agricultural strongholds, some strongrooms attached to village dwellings — same source
- Agricultural terraces documented in Attica (Atene deme), Delos and Keos in the Classical and Hellenistic periods — [Academia: Agricultural Terraces](https://www.academia.edu/29593340/Agricultural_Terraces_in_Classical_and_Hellenistic_Greece)

### Inferences
- Background, not re-verified: boundary stones (horoi) mark estates and sanctuary precincts; rural shrines of Pan, Nymphs, Hermes, Demeter and a hilltop Zeus are common; the main road leaves via a gate with tombs; threshing floors, presses and cisterns are part of the farm. Towers are used for refuge in raids (piracy in H Aegean).

### Gaps
- Farm sizes, road types and rural sanctuary typologies not sourced.

## 5. Citadels, forts, refuge places on islands

### Takeaway
Islands combine a harbour town with a hill-top fort or refuge, plus watch-towers around the coast.

### Cited Findings
- Halicarnassus had a fortified palace/citadel commanding both harbour and inland hills, with watchtowers — [Wikipedia: Halicarnassus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halicarnassus)
- Thasos coastal towers serving signalling, refuge and storage (see section 4)

### Inferences
- Background, not re-verified: island fort/refuge sites include walled summits (e.g. Koressia on Keos, Zagora in the earlier period) and small Ptolemaic or Antigonid garrison forts (e.g. Koressos, Itanos, Thera's Ptolemaic base on the Mesa Vouno ridge). I could not confirm specific examples here.
