Four Camps, Two Gates

An account of a siege in which four commanders, who could send word to one another only by raven and could not tell a dead man from a slow bird, were never obliged to decide anything at all.

Chapter I

The Hill and the Four Hollows

The hill stands by itself in a rolling plain, and there is nothing else high within sight of it. It is a cone, four hundred paces of height and rather more than a thousand across at the foot, and it is steep: a man climbing it leans forward the whole way. Eight long spurs run down its flanks like the folds of a heavy cloak, and between each spur and the next there is a gully, deep and wooded and quite separate from its neighbours. Holm oak and Aleppo pine cover the lower two thirds. Above that the trees give out into lentisk and broom, and above that again there is nothing but grey limestone scree, so that the top of the hill is pale where all the rest of it is black.

On the flat crown, which is a little over sixty paces across, there is an old wall of rough polygonal masonry, waist-thick and rather more than twice the height of a man. It is not in good repair. Several stretches have slumped, and the men who hold it now have patched those stretches with piled stone and cut timber. There are two ways through it. On the north side a narrow slot passes under a single flat stone lintel, and a man goes through it alone and not two abreast. On the south side there is a wider opening whose stone surround failed long ago and has been repaired with squared timber posts, a timber head beam and raking braces set back against the rock inside. Anyone who has looked at the two gates for a quarter of an hour can tell them apart at any distance at which they can be seen at all.

Bandits hold the crown. Four camps of soldiers lie at the foot of the hill, at the mouths of four gullies, roughly at the quarter points: north-east, south-east, south-west, north-west. Each camp is a ring of sharpened stakes round a shallow hollow, with two rows of leather tents inside it, a larger tent at the middle for the commander, and beside that a wicker loft up on stilts where the ravens are kept.

The camps do not signal. They light no fires, they burn nothing that smokes, and they sound no horns. The reason is plain enough: a fire on the lower slope would be seen from the crown, and what the bandits would learn from it is not merely that soldiers are there — they know that — but where each body of them sits and, worse, that the four bodies are in conversation. So the camps cook cold, and after dark the only light permitted anywhere below the wall is a small bronze lamp under a hood, indoors, in a commander's tent, with the flap down.

Nor can the camps see one another. Between any two of them stand two spurs and the gully between, and the spurs are higher than the camps by more than a hundred paces of hillside. A man at the mouth of one gully who looks along the ground toward his neighbour a thousand paces away sees black pine and a wooded ridge and nothing else at all. There is exactly one thing every camp can see that every other camp can also see, and it is the wall on the crown — and not even the wall, strictly, but its topmost courses, a ragged strip of moonlit stone a couple of feet deep that clears the shoulder of the hill and no more. Above that strip, on a clear night, there is a faint warmth in the sky where the bandits' own fire burns inside their own wall. They have no reason to hide it. They have been seen.

What remains, then, is the raven. Each camp keeps a loft, and each loft is divided into compartments, and the little door of each compartment is painted with the blazon of the camp whose birds are in it — an owl, a bull, a boar, a trident. A bird taken from the compartment marked with the bull will, sooner or later, arrive at the camp of the bull. That is the whole of the arrangement, and it is the only arrangement there is.

One more thing should be said before anything else, because everything in this book turns on it. A single ram, brought up a single gully by a single camp, will not carry either gate. The bandits are not many, but the crown is small and the wall, however patched, is a wall; what one camp can achieve alone at either gate is nothing. If the gate is to go, the rams must come to the same gate. Which is to say: the four camps do not need to agree about the weather, or about the bandits, or about anything else in the world. They need to agree about one thing, and there are two answers to it, and the answers are north and south.

Chapter II

What the Scouts Brought Back

Each camp sent its own scouts up at dusk, before the light went, and each camp's scouts came back with their own tablet. The tablets are wax on a hinged board and they carry the same kind of marks: a hasty stylus sketch of the two gates in their stretch of wall, and beside each gate a few tally strokes for what the scout thought worth counting — men on the parapet above it, the state of the repair, how the ground runs in the last thirty paces of the approach.

The four tablets do not agree, and there is no reason why they should. A scout who came up the north-east gully saw the north gate from close and the south gate only at an angle; a scout out of the south-west gully saw the reverse. One of them found the timber bracing of the south gate to be new and sound, and marked against it; another found the same bracing and thought it would burn. So each camp, having read its own tablet by lamplight with the flap down, has an opinion. Not a plan, and not a decision: an opinion, one of two, north or south.

Three things are wanted of the four camps, and it is worth setting them down flatly, because the rest of this book is about the third of them.

The three demands

First. No two camps may carry their rams to different gates. A siege in which two rams go north and two go south is worse than a siege in which none goes anywhere; it is four halves of an assault and no assault.

Second. Both gates must remain genuinely possible. It will not do for the commanders to settle the matter in advance by agreeing always to go north whatever the scouts bring back. That satisfies the first demand perfectly and it makes the scouts pointless. What the camps do must depend on what they learned.

Third. The rams must eventually go somewhere. A siege that never commits is not a siege; it is four camps sitting in the dark.

The first demand is the important one and it is never in danger anywhere in this book. The second is easily met. The third is the one that cannot be had.

Committing is a definite act and not a state of mind, and this matters. A camp commits by taking its ram out from under the hide shed, knocking the beam apart into sections, loading the sections and the bronze-shod head and the ropes onto the ox cart, and standing the cart at the mouth of the gully that leads to one gate and not the other. Once that is done it is done. A dismantled ram on a loaded cart is not a ram, and the hours it would take to make it one again are hours the camp does not have with men on the slope. So a commander may think what he likes for as long as he likes, and change his mind as often as he likes, and none of it counts; and then he gives one order and it counts permanently.

Note also what the commanders may not do. They may not draw lots. There is no shortage of pebbles on that hill and it would be the easiest thing in the world for the four of them to have agreed beforehand that in case of doubt each would put his hand in a bag; but they did not, and the arrangement they did make has nothing of chance in it. Each commander acts on what has reached him and on nothing else. Given the same tablets and the same arrivals in the same order, each of them does the same thing every time.

Chapter III

The Ravens and the Folds

A message goes like this. The keeper — an old man with a leather gauntlet laced onto his left arm from wrist to elbow, and a coloured cord at his belt so that anyone can see at a glance whose keeper he is — takes a strip of papyrus, rolls it, puts it in a small bronze tube, stops the tube with wax, and lashes the tube to a bird's leg with waxed cord. He takes the bird from the compartment whose door carries the blazon of the camp it is going to. Then he lets it go, and it climbs away over the trees, and that is the end of the keeper's part in it.

Four things are true of the birds, and all four of them are needed.

They arrive. No raven of that hill has ever been lost for good. Every bird that is sent is eventually taken in at the camp it was sent to, and the keepers know it, and they are right. This is worth dwelling on, because a great deal of trouble in the world comes from messages that simply vanish, and none of the trouble in this book does. Nothing here is ever lost.

They take as long as they take. The hill is folded, and the folds are wooded, and the woods are full of the hill's own crows and ravens, which is part of the difficulty: a bird carrying a capsule out of the north-east gully goes up over the spur, finds a hundred of its own kind quarrelling in the pines on the far side, and joins them for an hour or for the rest of the night. It will go on. Nobody knows when. And here is the point that matters: there is no longest flight. No keeper has a figure for it. No commander can say "if the bird from the boar has not come by such a time, it is not coming", because no such time exists and everyone knows it does not. The camps have no water-clocks and would not trust one if they had; the sky tells them nothing; and a bird that has been eleven hours in the folds is not more overdue than a bird that has been one, only later.

They arrive in no particular order. Two birds sent from the same loft an hour apart may be taken in in either order, and often are. A commander who receives word that seems to answer a question he has not yet been asked is not surprised by it.

Nothing forged, nothing false. A capsule that arrives at the camp of the owl with the cord of the bull's keeper on it was tied by the bull's keeper and says what the bull's commander told him to say. There is no counterfeiting anywhere in this book, no bird trained to lie, no capsule opened on the way and put back. Whatever else the camps do not know, they never doubt the word they actually hold. (What happens on a hill where they can doubt it is the business of the other book of this setting, and it is a different hill only in the sense that it is the same hill from the top.)

One more fact, small and disproportionately important. A keeper who walks over to the loft to see whether anything has come may find nothing there. That is not an event and it is not news. He comes back with an empty gauntlet, and the world is exactly as it was, and he will go again later. A camp can do this all night. Going to look and finding nothing is always available, always allowed, and never tells anybody anything.

Chapter IV

A Camp That Does Not Answer

The commanders know one further thing about their situation, and they knew it before they came. Any one of them may be killed at any moment — in his tent, at the loft, on the path, in the middle of dictating a capsule with the keeper standing there waiting for the rest of the sentence. It has not happened. There is no assassin in this account; nobody is sent and nobody is shown, and the reader who is waiting for one is waiting for a different book. What matters is not that it happens but that it may, and that the four of them have arranged their affairs on that footing.

They have arranged them badly, or rather they have arranged them as well as anyone could. A camp whose commander is dead falls silent, and stays silent. No one succeeds him: the second man in a camp of that size is a competent hoplite who has never been told what the four commanders were deciding or why, and he keeps the palisade and waits for orders that do not come. No word goes out to the other camps. Nobody sends a raven to say that the owl's commander is dead, because nobody in the owl's camp knows that the other three are waiting on him, and because the man who would have given that order is the man in question. A dead commander produces exactly one observable effect in the world, and it is the absence of anything.

Now put the two chapters together, which is all this one does.

The difficulty, entire

A commander waiting on a camp that has not answered is in one of two situations. In the first, the commander of that camp is dead and no bird will ever come. In the second, the commander of that camp is perfectly well, the bird was sent hours ago, and it is sitting in a pine two spurs away among a hundred of its own kind.

There is nothing — no observation, no instrument, no length of waiting, no clever arrangement of the birds — by which he can tell which. The two situations produce the same world: an unanswered row on a tally board and a keeper with an empty gauntlet. And because there is no longest flight, waiting longer never converts one into the other. It only makes the wait longer.

One point of arithmetic, and it is the last of the arrangements. At most one camp may fall silent. The commanders have not agreed to this; it is not a rule they made but a fact of the siege they are in, and it is a generous fact. If two or three of the four could go silent the reader would rightly expect trouble, and there would be a duller book in it. What follows is worse than that, because it needs only the possibility of one.

Chapter V

Standings and Arrivals

From here on the account needs words for things that ordinary language has no words for, and it is better to set them down at once than to keep explaining them. There are six, and they are all as plain as I can make them. The terms the mathematicians use for the same things are given beside each, and the reader who prefers those should use them.

In this bookWhat it means
a standingHow the siege stands, entire: what each of the four camps holds in its head and on its tally board at this moment, together with every capsule that is aloft and not yet taken in. A configuration.
an arrivalOne named camp taking in one named capsule. That is the only kind of thing that ever happens; a camp thinks only when something arrives. An event — the receipt of a message by a process.
an empty-handed arrivalA keeper going to the loft and coming back with nothing. Always possible, changes nothing, and counts as a camp's turn. A receive that returns the null marker.
a courseA list of arrivals in order: this camp takes in that capsule, then this one takes in that one, and so on, for as long as you like or for ever. Run the list from a standing and you get the siege that follows it. A schedule, and its run.
a fair courseA course that could really happen on that hill. Two conditions: no capsule sent to a camp that is still answering stays aloft for ever, and at most one camp falls silent. An admissible run.
two-gated / one-gatedA standing is two-gated if, from where the siege now is, there is a fair course ending with the rams at the north gate and a fair course ending with them at the south. It is one-gated if only one of the two is still reachable. Bivalent and univalent.

Two remarks about two-gated, because it is the word the rest of the book rests on and it is easy to hear it as something softer than it is.

It is not a fact about anybody's state of mind. No commander is undecided in a two-gated standing; the commanders may be entirely resolved, each of them, and the standing be two-gated all the same. What is two-gated is the siege, and what the word reports is that the future is not yet closed: there are still two ways this can come out, depending only on the order in which birds happen to arrive.

And it is not the same as nothing having happened. A standing far along in the night, with three tally boards crowded with pegs and a great deal settled, may be as thoroughly two-gated as the standing at dusk. Two-gated says nothing about how much is known. It says that the hill has not yet been made to choose.

A standing in which some camp has already committed is, of course, one-gated, and by the first demand every other camp that ever commits must commit the same way. The moment the first cart is stood at the mouth of a gully, the question is closed for good. That is what makes the first cart interesting.

Chapter VI

The Lemma of Separate Folds

The first of the three things that have to be established is the least interesting to look at and the most useful to have. It is about two camps that have nothing to do with each other for a while.

The lemma of separate folds

Take any standing. Take two courses that could be run from it, and suppose that no camp appears in both — every camp that stirs in the first is quiet in the second, and the other way about. Then the two courses do not interfere. You may run the first and then the second, or the second and then the first, and the siege ends up in precisely the same standing either way.

The reason is nothing more than the shape of the arrangement, and it is worth saying slowly because it is the only thing in the book that is genuinely obvious.

What does an arrival consist of? A named camp takes in a named capsule, reads it, adds a peg to its board, and perhaps ties a capsule or two of its own and lets the birds go. Everything in that sentence belongs either to the camp or to the air. Nothing belongs to any other camp. The commander of the boar does not consult the commander of the bull before pushing in a peg; he could not if he wished to. So if the first course is a business of the owl and the bull, and the second is a business of the boar and the trident, then running the first alters nothing the second depends on, and running the second alters nothing the first depends on. Each is, to the other, weather.

The one thing to be careful of is the air. Could the first course consume a capsule the second needs? It could not: a capsule is addressed, and the capsules the second course consumes are addressed to camps that take no part in the first. And could the first course's birds get in the second's way? There is no way in which a bird can be in another bird's way. The folds of that hill are large and the ravens are many.

So the two courses commute, and the picture is a diamond: one standing at the top, two at the sides, and the same standing at the bottom whichever way round you go.

Proof — that courses touching no camp in common commute
  1. A standing consists of what is in each commander's head and on his board, together with the capsules aloft. An arrival changes exactly two parts of it: the state of the one camp that takes the capsule in, and the air — one capsule removed, possibly some new ones added.
  2. Let σ₁ touch a set of camps A and σ₂ a set B, with A ∩ B empty. Running σ₁ alters only camps in A, so it alters nothing σ₂ reads, since σ₂ reads only camps in B. Symmetrically for the other order.
  3. Neither can disable the other through the air. A capsule is addressed. The capsules σ₂ consumes are addressed to camps in B, and σ₁ removes from the air only capsules addressed to camps in A. So every capsule σ₂ needs is still aloft after σ₁ has run, and conversely.
  4. Hence both orders are applicable from C. In either order the camps of A have taken exactly the arrivals of σ₁ and the camps of B exactly those of σ₂, each in its own order; and the air has had exactly the same capsules removed and the same added.
  5. Therefore both orders reach the same standing.∎
Chapter VII

The Scouts' Beginning

The second thing to establish is that the siege can begin badly — that there is a way for the scouts to come back down which leaves the hill genuinely open, with both gates still reachable and nothing in the four tablets to settle it. One might hope not. One might hope that the tablets always add up to something, and that a wise arrangement could always read the answer off them. They do not, and it cannot, and here is why.

Line up the possible beginnings. There are sixteen of them, since each of four camps comes back either for north or for south, and we can lay them out so that each differs from the next in exactly one camp's opinion: all four for north at one end, all four for south at the other, and a chain of beginnings between them in which we flip one camp at a time.

Suppose now, for the sake of argument, that no beginning is two-gated — that every one of the sixteen already settles the matter. The second demand tells us that the two ends of the chain settle it differently: a hill whose scouts all say north must be able to go north, and a hill whose scouts all say south must be able to go south, or the scouts are ornamental. So somewhere along that chain there are two beginnings side by side, one fated to go north and the next fated to go south, and they differ in the opinion of one camp only. Call that camp Q.

Here is the trouble. Consider a fair course starting from the northward beginning in which Q takes no part at all — never receives, never sends, never stirs. That is a permissible course: at most one camp may fall silent, and this once it is Q. By the third demand that course must eventually commit, and being a course from the northward beginning it commits north.

Now run the very same course from the southward beginning. It is the same list of arrivals, and every camp that appears in it — which is to say, all of them but Q — holds exactly what it held before, since the two beginnings differ only in what Q's scouts said, and Q has said nothing to anybody. The three other camps cannot tell the two beginnings apart. Not one of them has any means of telling: the only thing in the world that distinguishes the two is a wax tablet in Q's tent, and Q is silent. So they do what they did before, in the order they did it, and the siege commits north.

But that beginning was supposed to be fated south. So it was not fated after all: from it, north is reachable too. It was two-gated the whole time, which is what we had assumed no beginning was.

The scouts' beginning

There is a way for the scouts to come back which leaves the siege two-gated. However the four commanders have arranged their affairs, the hill can start open.

Notice what did the work. Not the death of Q's commander — nobody died. What did the work was that the other three could not tell a silent camp from a slow one, and so could not tell two different worlds apart. The permission for one camp to be silent was never used to break anything. It was used to build an argument.

Proof — that some beginning is two-gated
  1. Suppose every beginning were one-gated — each of them already fated, from the scouts' reports alone, to end at one particular gate.
  2. Both fates occur. Where every scout reported the north gate weakest, the second demand forces north; where every scout reported south, it forces south. So there is a north-fated beginning and a south-fated one.
  3. Call two beginnings neighbours when they differ in exactly one camp's report. Any beginning can be walked to any other by changing one report at a time, so somewhere along the walk from the north-fated to the south-fated beginning there are neighbours B₀ and B₁, fated north and south respectively, differing only in the report of one camp — call it p.
  4. Consider a course from B₀ in which p never stirs. It is permitted: at most one camp may be silent, and the course may let p be the one. Being fair, it reaches the north gate.
  5. Run the very same course from B₁. Since p never acts and p's report is the only difference, no other camp can tell the two beginnings apart at any point; every other camp sees exactly the same arrivals in the same order. So this course reaches the north gate from B₁ as well.
  6. But B₁ was fated south, and a fated beginning admits no course to the other gate. Contradiction.
  7. Hence some beginning is two-gated.∎
Chapter VIII

Deferring the Telling Arrival

So the hill can start open. The third thing to establish is that it can be kept open — that from any open standing, whichever arrival would have closed the matter, there is a way to take that arrival and still be open afterwards.

Fix a two-gated standing S, and fix some particular arrival e that can happen at it: some named camp taking in some named capsule that is aloft and addressed to it. We are not going to prevent e. The birds are reliable and prevention is not on offer; e will happen sooner or later and we know it. The question is only whether it must close the matter when it does.

Consider every standing reachable from S without letting e happen yet — all the ways the night could go on while that one capsule stays in the keeper's loft unopened or that one bird stays in its pine. Call that collection the deferral. Now take each standing in the deferral and let e happen at it. What we must show is that at least one of the results is still two-gated.

Suppose not: suppose e, applied anywhere in the deferral, always closes the matter. Since S itself is two-gated, the deferral contains a course leading toward north and a course leading toward south, so among the closed standings we get after e there are some fated north and some fated south. Walk from one to the other and you must, at some point, find two standings a single step apart whose results after e are fated differently: one step, one arrival, and the whole matter changes sides. Call that single step f.

Now there are only two possibilities, and neither survives.

If f belongs to a different camp from e. Then by the lemma of separate folds they commute, and the diamond closes: doing e then f lands in the same standing as doing f then e. But one of those routes was supposed to end fated north and the other fated south, and they have ended in the same standing. A standing cannot be fated both ways. So f cannot belong to another camp.

If f belongs to the same camp as e. Then let that camp go silent for a while — not die; go quiet, as a camp does whose birds are in the pines — and let the other three carry on to a finish. They can: a course in which one camp takes no part is fair, and by the third demand it must commit. Say it commits north. Now go back and take the same course, but let the quiet camp perform e at the start of it, and then run everything else exactly as before. By the lemma of separate folds the others notice nothing — e was that camp's own business — and they commit north again. Do it once more with f then e at the start, and they commit south, for that was what f was chosen to do. So from one standing, north and south are both reachable: it is two-gated. But we reached that standing by a course that was supposed to have committed, and a committed standing is closed. Again the assumption breaks.

Deferring the telling arrival

Let the siege stand two-gated, and let e be any arrival that can happen at it. Then the night can go on — without e for a while, and then with it — and arrive at a standing that is two-gated still.

Which is to say: no single capsule is ever the one that has to settle the matter. Each can be taken in, read, and pegged to a board, and the hill be no more decided afterwards than before.

Proof — that deferring an arrival always leaves the hill two-gated
  1. Let the siege stand two-gated at C, and let e be an arrival applicable there. Write 𝒞 for every standing reachable from C without ever allowing e, and 𝒟 for the standings got by allowing e at each of them.
  2. Suppose, for contradiction, that no standing in 𝒟 is two-gated — every one of them is settled, one gate or the other.
  3. Both settlements occur in 𝒟. Since C is two-gated, a course from it reaches the north gate and another reaches the south. Take either. If it never allowed e, its standings lie in 𝒞 and allowing e at the end gives a member of 𝒟 fated the same way. If it did allow e, then the standing just after that moment is already in 𝒟, and the rest of the course runs on from it. Either way 𝒟 contains a north-settled and a south-settled standing.
  4. So 𝒞 contains neighbours that split. 𝒞 is connected — every standing in it is reached from C by arrivals — so walking from the standing whose image is north-settled to the one whose image is south-settled, there must be two standings C₀ and C₁ = e′(C₀), one arrival apart, whose images D₀ and D₁ are settled opposite ways.
  5. Case one: e′ belongs to a different camp than e. Then by the lemma of separate folds the two commute, so D₁ = e′(D₀). But D₀ is settled north, and every standing reachable from it is settled north — including D₁, which is settled south. Contradiction.
  6. Case two: e′ belongs to the same camp p as e. Consider a course from C₀ in which p never stirs and which does settle the matter; such a course is permitted, since at most one camp may be silent and p may be that camp. Call the standing it reaches A, and the course σ.
  7. Since σ touches no camp in common with e or e′, the lemma of separate folds lets σ be run from D₀ and from D₁ as well. Running it from D₀ gives e(A); running it from D₁ gives e′ then e applied to A.
  8. So from A the siege can still reach a north-settled standing and a south-settled one — which makes A two-gated. But σ was a course that settled the matter, so A is settled. Contradiction.
  9. Both cases are impossible, so 𝒟 contains a two-gated standing: the arrival can be allowed and the hill remain undecided.∎
Chapter IX

What Cannot Be Promised

The three pieces now fit together, and what they make is not a disaster but a limit.

The hill can begin open: that is the scouts' beginning. From any open standing, any arrival can be taken and the hill left open: that is the deferral. So construct the night as follows. Keep the four camps in a queue, in any order you like. Go to the camp at the head of it. If a capsule is waiting for that camp, let it take in the one that has been aloft the longest; if none is waiting, let its keeper walk over and come back empty-handed, which is always allowed. But choose which stretch of night precedes that arrival by the deferral, so that the standing you reach afterwards is two-gated again. Then send that camp to the back of the queue and go to the next.

Run that for ever. Every camp gets its turn again and again, so no camp is starved. Every capsule that is sent is eventually the oldest one waiting for its camp, and so is eventually taken in: nothing is lost. The course is therefore entirely fair — it is a night that could really happen on that hill, with reliable birds and no bound on their flying. And at the end of every stage the siege is two-gated, which is to say that at no moment in it has anything been settled.

What cannot be promised

There is no arrangement the four commanders could have made — none at all, however ingenious, however much they wrote down beforehand — which guarantees that the rams ever go anywhere.

Four things should be said about that sentence, because each of them is a way of misunderstanding it.

Nobody dies in it. The night just constructed contains no assassination at all. All four commanders are alive at dawn and alive the next night. The permission for one camp to fall silent was never taken up; it was needed to make the argument, not to make the trouble. This is the part most easily got backwards. The hill is not defeated by a death. It is limited by the possibility of one — by the fact that, since a death would look exactly like a slow bird, no commander may ever safely treat silence as death, and so no commander may ever safely stop waiting.

Nothing goes wrong. The first demand is never broken: no two camps ever cart their rams to different gates, because no camp ever carts its ram anywhere. The second is never broken either. What fails is only the third, the promise to finish. The camps are never wrong; they are only never done.

It would be enough for one camp to commit. The demand is not that all four finish together — one cart, stood at one gully mouth, would satisfy it, and the others could follow at their leisure. The night above does not even manage that.

And it is not about this hill. Nothing in the three chapters used the number four, or the shape of the cone, or the two gates being north and south rather than east and west. Any number of camps from two upward, any two answers, any hill: the argument is the same and the conclusion is the same.

This account leaves the cart loaded and standing, and that is the most it will do. Whether the beasts were ever yoked to it is not part of what is being explained here, and a book that ended with the yoking would be claiming to know something it has just finished proving nobody could know.

Chapter X

What Would Have to Change

It is worth being exact about which of the hill's conditions is doing the damage, since a limit is only useful if you know what it is a limit on. Three of the four commanders' difficulties are innocent. That the birds are slow is not the problem; that they arrive out of order is not the problem; that nobody can be relied on to be clever is not the problem. The problem is the conjunction of two things: that there is no longest flight, and that a dead commander looks exactly like a slow bird. Take away either and the night above cannot be constructed.

Suppose there were a longest flight — suppose the keepers knew, and knew truly, that no bird of that hill has ever taken more than five hours in the folds. Then silence of six hours means something. It does not mean the commander is dead, necessarily, but it is no longer nothing, and a commander who has waited six hours may act on the waiting itself. The whole construction depends on being able to defer an arrival indefinitely, and a bound on flight is exactly a refusal of indefinitely.

Or suppose there were some report of who has died — not a reliable one; a reliable one would be too much to ask for and more than is needed. Suppose only that someone, somewhere, will eventually stop wrongly naming the living. That is a thin thing to be given, and it is enough, because it breaks the symmetry the construction rests on.

Or suppose the deaths all happened before anything started. This one is worth stating because it sounds like the same difficulty and is not. If some camps were never going to answer at all — dead before the scouts went up — and every camp that can answer is going to keep answering for the whole night, then the commanders can manage, provided more than half of them are in the second class. They spend the first part of the night finding out who can hear whom, and the second part working out from that which camps form a group that heard one another and heard nothing from outside itself — not merely any group whose members all heard each other, but one with nothing upstream of it, which is what makes every camp pick out the same group. Then they all read the same opinions off that group and all reach the same answer. Nothing in that procedure has to distinguish a dead man from a slow bird, because in that world nobody dies during the night, and that is the whole of the difference.

None of these is on offer on this hill. There is no longest flight, nobody reports anything, and a commander may be killed at any moment including the middle of a sentence. So the four camps sit in four hollows in the dark, with three tally boards filling up and one row empty, and a ram apiece under a hide shed pointed at nothing, and the top two feet of the bandits' wall showing over the shoulder of the hill with a glow above it. All of that is exactly as it should be. Nothing is wrong. It is simply not finished, and nothing obliges it to be.

The ideas. No deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system in which even one process may crash. The proof turns on bivalence: a configuration from which both decisions are still reachable. There is always a bivalent initial configuration, and from any bivalent configuration the step that would resolve the indecision can be deferred and then taken without resolving it — so an adversarial scheduler can build an admissible run that never decides.

Three details carry most of the weight and are the ones usually mislaid. First, the run the proof constructs contains no failure at all: the single permitted fault is spent on the argument, not on the system. Second, agreement and validity are never violated; what is lost is only termination. Third, the result bites even though the requirement is merely that some process decide.

The obstruction is not slowness as such but the inability to distinguish a crashed process from a slow one, which is a consequence of asynchrony rather than an extra assumption. Relax it — bound the delays, even only eventually, or supply an imperfect oracle about who has failed — and consensus becomes solvable again. The paper's own §4 relaxes it a third way, by letting processes die only before the protocol begins, and exhibits a protocol that then works whenever a majority are sound.
