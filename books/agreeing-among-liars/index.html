Agreeing Among Liars

Based on “The Byzantine Generals Problem” by Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak and Marshall Pease (1982) and “Reaching Agreement in the Presence of Faults” by Marshall Pease, Robert Shostak and Leslie Lamport (1980)

Can machines agree when some of them may lie, telling one thing to one and something else to another? How many honest machines does each liar cost, and what changes if a message cannot be forged?

Four bandits on a hilltop must hold or scatter together, and cannot tell a lying chief from a lying peer. They can agree, but only if more than two thirds are honest — or, under seal, whatever the number of liars.

Chapter I

The Crown

Bandits hold the crown of Mount Phyle with their loot: the chief, Number 1, inside a small ring wall with no gate, and three of his men at lookout posts out on the slopes, none within sight or earshot of another*. They send word only by rat, and they have one question to settle, with two answers.

The question

The bandits must hold the hill and keep the loot, or scatter and abandon it, and must all do the same thing. Half holding while half scatter is worse than either. Holding ties no man to his post: each holds from wherever he chooses.

Which answer is better is settled nowhere in this book. Nor is any night being narrated: what is described is an arrangement, and nothing comes out.

Nobody on the crown dies, and the runs between the posts are short and bounded. But some men do not report what they were told.

At the foot of the hill are four mercenaries hired by the houses of the ring road to clear the bandits out. The bandits know the camps are there, and nothing of what the mercenaries mean to do. The mercenaries have the opposite trouble. None of them lies, but any of them may die, and a raven between their tents has no longest flight*.

Which is the worse trouble to arrange against: a friend who may be lying, or a friend who may be dead? The book answers only at its end.

Chapter II

What the Bandits Want

Each bandit forms his own account of what should be done: hold, or scatter. The three at the posts have each watched one approach from the slopes; the chief, inside his wall, has seen only sky, and forms his from what he knows of the loot. The four accounts must get into four heads unaltered. Why is that hard? Because a liar can send one thing to one man and something else to another.

So take the accounts one at a time. One man, the chief for this round, sends his order to the other three, the guards, and two things are wanted of that sending.

The two conditions

The first. Every honest guard obeys the same order.

The second. If the chief is honest, every honest guard obeys the order he sent.

If the chief is honest, the second gives the first for nothing. But what if the chief is the liar? Then the second says nothing at all, while the first still stands: honest men must end up together even when the man they listened to told them different things. That asymmetry is the whole problem.

A liar may do anything: send different orders to different men, send nothing, keep a lie up consistently, or agree his lies with another liar. Who the liars are is never learned, by anyone on the hill or by the reader.

And the chief is a job, not a rank. Number 1 sits inside the ring wall, but in these arrangements whoever's account is going round is the chief for that round. Run the thing four times, once for each man's account, and every honest man ends holding the same four accounts, after which any rule they all apply gives them all the same answer.

The chief might lie. Then why doesn't each guard simply ask the others what the chief told them, and compare?

Chapter III

The Rats in the Wall

We asked earlier why each guard doesn't ask the others what the chief told him. On the crown he can, because the rats up here are different. The runs are short and direct, over the rocks and through the chinks of the wall, and no rat has ever taken longer than a sandglass takes to run. At dusk, before the posts were manned, the four turned their glasses together at one word, and each has turned his since the moment it ran out. \1

Word on the crown goes by rat. Each man has trained rats to run to each of the others, and he sends a slip by tying it, on a cord knotted with his own number, to one of them and letting it go. The wall has no gate, but its stones do not fit close, and a rat finds the chinks.

What the rats are assumed to give, on the crown The first. A slip sent arrives, and arrives as written.
The second. The knots on its cord name the man who sent it, so no man can be impersonated.
The third. Silence is detectable: if nothing comes from a post within the round, nothing was sent.

The third assumption is the one that costs something, and it is bought with the short run and the rounds, exactly what the mercenaries at the foot do not have*. Silence is also given a meaning, or a man could block everything by sending nothing: no slip within the round counts as hold. Hold is the default, not the wiser answer, only the definite one.

What none of this gives is any check on content. A plain slip says whatever its writer chose to write, and a guard reporting what the chief sent him may write anything he likes.

So guards can compare notes, and silence means something. Take the smallest party, a chief and two guards, one of the three a liar. Is comparing notes enough to save the two honest men?

Chapter IV

Three Cannot Be Brought to Agree

We asked earlier whether comparing notes saves two honest guards against a liar among three. It does not, and nothing can.

Put an honest guard in two situations. In the first, the chief is honest and sends hold to both guards; the other guard is the liar and reports he sent me scatter. The second condition binds our man: the chief is honest, so he must hold. In the second situation the chief is the liar and sends hold to our man and scatter to the other; the other guard is honest and reports, truthfully, he sent me scatter.

What does our man hold in each? Exactly the same two slips. He cannot tell the situations apart, so he holds in both. But the same argument, run on the other guard, has him scattering in the second, and there both guards are honest and have gone two ways.

That argument is persuasive and not a proof; reasoning of just this shape has settled things that turned out false. The result is correct, and the rigorous proof is in Pease, Shostak and Lamport 1980.

Proof — that three cannot be brought to agree, as far as such an argument goes
  1. Suppose an arrangement works for three men with one liar: all honest men obey the same order, and an honest chief is obeyed.
  2. A. Chief honest, sending hold to both guards; the second guard lies, reporting he sent me scatter.
  3. B. Chief the liar, sending hold to the first guard and scatter to the second; the second guard is honest and reports the same words.
  4. The first guard receives the same two things in A and B and nothing else, so he does the same in both. In A the chief is honest, so he must hold; hence he holds in B.
  5. C, the mirror of A: chief honest, sending scatter to both; the first guard lies. By the same argument the second guard scatters in C, and B looks like C to him, so he scatters in B.
  6. In B both guards are honest and have gone two ways, against the first condition.∎

Three men cannot bear one liar. How many can? What does a liar cost?

Chapter V

Four Men to a Liar

We asked earlier what a liar costs. The answer: more than three men for each. One liar wants four men, two want seven, three want ten. The crown has four, so it bears one liar and no more.

Why? Suppose some method brought three men or fewer to a liar safely through. Then three real men, one a liar, could each privately play a third of that method's men, none playing more parts than the method can bear liars. The method would keep its promise among the parts, and each real man would do what his parts did: three real men would agree against one liar, which, as we saw, cannot happen.

Would it help to ask for less, only that they act at about the same time? No: rough agreement can be turned back into exact agreement, so the one is as hard as the other.

Proof — that fewer than four men to a liar cannot be made to work
  1. Suppose a method brings n men safely through m liars with n ≤ 3m. Call its men parts.
  2. Set three real men, one a liar, to play the parts: the real chief plays the parts' chief and at most m − 1 of its guards, and each real guard plays at most m of its guards. Since n ≤ 3m, they divide so.
  3. Word between two parts played by one man passes in his head; word between parts played by different men goes by rat, and, as the rats above are assumed to give, arrives as sent and correctly attributed.
  4. At most one real man lies, and he plays at most m parts, so at most m parts lie — which the method was assumed to bear. It therefore keeps both conditions among the parts.
  5. By the first condition, all the parts played by one honest real man obey one order, which he obeys; by the second, if the real chief is honest then every honest part obeys his order. So the three real men satisfy both conditions against one liar, which, as we saw, cannot be done.∎

Above the bound there is a method, the Repeating. It needs one auxiliary, the greater count: any rule that takes the accounts a guard holds and returns one, such that if most of them say the same thing, that is what it returns.

The Repeating, bearing m liars (1) The chief sends his order to every guard.
(2) Each guard takes what he received, or hold if nothing came, and — unless m is zero — acts as chief himself in the Repeating bearing m − 1 liars, putting that order about to the other guards.
(3) Each guard now holds his own account and one from each other guard, and obeys the greater count of them.

On this crown, bearing one liar, that is two rounds. If the chief is honest, each honest guard holds hold, hold and whatever the liar said, and the greater count is hold. If the chief lies, all three guards are honest, each repeats faithfully, and all three hold the same three accounts and reach the same answer. Nobody worked out who lied; he was outvoted, which is cheaper.

What the Repeating gives

Bearing m liars, it meets both conditions whenever there are more than 3m men and at most m of them lie.

Proof — that the Repeating meets both conditions above the bound
  1. Lemma. Bearing m liars, the method meets the second condition if there are more than 2k + m men and at most k liars. By induction on m: for m = 0 an honest chief's order arrives as sent. Assume it for m − 1.
  2. An honest chief sends v to all n − 1 guards. Each honest guard puts v about among n − 1 men bearing m − 1 liars, and n − 1 > 2k + (m − 1), so by the hypothesis every honest guard receives v from every honest guard.
  3. Since n − 1 > 2k, most guards are honest, so v is most of what each honest guard holds and the greater count returns it.
  4. The theorem, by induction on m. If the chief is honest, the lemma with k = m gives the second condition, and the first follows from it.
  5. If the chief lies, at most m − 1 guards lie, and there are more than 3m − 1 > 3(m − 1) guards, so the hypothesis applies to their repeatings: for each guard, any two honest guards end holding the same account of what he put about.
  6. So any two honest guards hold the same accounts in the same places, and the same greater count of them. The first condition holds; the second is vacuous, the chief being a liar.∎

Every further liar costs three more men and another round of reports about reports. The trouble was that a plain slip says whatever its writer chose. What if a slip could not be altered?

Chapter VI

The Seal Rings

We asked earlier what happens if a slip cannot be altered. Each man on the crown has a signet cut with his own number. A slip sent under seal is written in wax and stamped, and a man passing it on stamps his own beside, so hold : 1 : 2 is the chief's order as relayed by Number 2.

What a seal is assumed to do (a) An honest man's seal cannot be counterfeited, and tampering shows.
(b) Anyone can tell whose seal is whose.

Nothing is assumed about a liar's seal: liars may lend each other rings. And a seal cannot compel: a man can refuse to pass a slip on; he simply cannot alter it.

Each guard keeps a sheaf of the orders he has properly received, and a fixed standing rule turns a sheaf into one order, with an empty sheaf giving hold.

The Sealed Repeating, bearing m liars (1) The chief seals his order and sends it to every guard.
(2) A guard given a properly sealed order not yet in his sheaf puts it in; and if fewer than m guards' seals stand on it — the chief's not counted — he adds his own and sends it to every guard not already named on it.
(3) When no further slip can come, he obeys the standing rule applied to his sheaf.

Three men are now enough. If the chief lies, sealing hold to one guard and scatter to the other, each guard passes his across, both sheaves hold the same two orders, and the same standing rule sends both the same way. And each guard now holds two orders under the chief's seal, which could not both exist if the chief were honest: the chief has convicted himself, of lying and of nothing else, and only because he was careless enough to seal two orders.

How many liars can be borne? Any number, whatever the size of the party. Only the question gives out: with fewer than two men more than there are liars, too few honest men remain for agreement among them to mean anything. Where plain slips bought each liar with three more men, seals buy him with one more glass.

Proof — that the Sealed Repeating works against any number of liars
  1. Second condition. An honest chief seals one order and sends it to all, and no one can counterfeit his seal, so every honest guard's sheaf holds exactly that order and the standing rule returns it.
  2. First condition. Take the chief to be a liar. It is enough that if an honest guard i has an order in his sheaf, every honest guard j has it too; equal sheaves give equal answers.
  3. If i received it under the chief's seal alone, he sealed it and sent it to every other guard, so j has it.
  4. If it reached i carrying k guards' seals and j is among them, j had it already.
  5. If j is not among them and k < m, then i seals it and sends it to every guard not named on it, j included.
  6. If j is not among them and k = m: the chief is a liar, so at most m − 1 guards lie, so one of the m named on the slip is honest — and he, when he first received the order, sent it to every guard not then named on it, j among them.
  7. Nowhere were the men counted. The bound of four men to a liar belongs to unsealed slips, not to lying, and the seals dissolve it.∎
Chapter VII

The Two Halves

We asked at the start which trouble is worse, a friend who may be dead or a friend who may be lying. Set the two ends of the hill side by side. Above, agreement is had in a few rounds: four men bear one liar by plain slip, and under seal any number of liars is borne. Below, where any man may die and no bird has a longest flight, no arrangement whatever guarantees that anybody ever moves*. The difference is not that the bandits are cleverer. It is that they have rounds to bound the runs between them, and the mercenaries do not.

And each end is short of exactly one distinction, the same shape of trouble twice over.

The two things that cannot be settled

Below. A man with an empty perch cannot tell whether the man in that tent is dead or whether the bird is in a pine. So he can never safely stop waiting, and nothing is ever concluded.

Above. A guard holding two accounts of one order that disagree cannot tell whether the chief lied to one of them or the peer is lying to him. So no single man's word can ever be trusted, and nobody ever learns who lied.

In both cases what is lost is smaller than it looks. Below, nothing goes wrong: the men are never in disagreement, only never finished. Above, nothing is discovered: the men agree, and are wrong together as readily as right together. The arrangements were not built to tell anybody who failed. They were built so that nobody needs to be told.

So which trouble is worse? The one without a clock. Lies can be outvoted when silence means something; a death cannot be told from a delay when nothing ever does.

The ideas. A commander must get an order to n − 1 lieutenants so that IC1 all loyal lieutenants obey the same order and IC2 they obey his if he is loyal. IC2 gives IC1 only when the commander is loyal, and he may not be: that is the problem. With oral messages no solution exists for three generals against one traitor, because a loyal lieutenant cannot distinguish a lying commander from a lying peer; more generally nothing works below 3m + 1 generals, shown by having three generals each simulate a third of a hypothetical smaller solution, and settling for approximate agreement does not help, since it can be turned back into exact agreement. Above the bound, OM(m) works: the commander sends his value, each lieutenant relays what he got by OM(m − 1), and each takes the majority — one extra round per traitor tolerated. Unforgeable signatures change everything: SM(m) tolerates any number of traitors with no requirement at all on the number of generals, and three generals suffice against one. No assumption is made about a traitor's signature, so signatures prove what the loyal did and nothing about the rest, and a traitor can still withhold a message. What no algorithm gives is identification, the sole exception being a commander who signs two contradictory orders. And all of it rests on A3 — detecting absence — which in practice means bounded message time and clocks kept synchronised, itself a problem as hard as this one.
