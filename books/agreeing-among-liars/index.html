Agreeing Among Liars

Can a group of machines agree on a single decision when some of them actively lie — telling one thing to one peer and the opposite to another? And how many honest machines does it take to outvote each liar?

To find out, we follow four bandits holding the rocky crown of a hill. Knowing that one of their company may be a traitor, they must all agree whether to hold the hill or scatter — first using plain written slips, and then using wax seals that cannot be forged.

First, in machine terms

Many computers, some of them lying

Suppose a group of computers must all agree on a single command, even though some of them may be broken or malicious in any way imaginable — and nobody knows which ones.

In distributed computing, arbitrary or malicious failures are called Byzantine faults. A faulty machine does not merely crash. It can tell one neighbor one thing and another neighbor the exact opposite, stay selectively silent, or conspire with other faulty machines. When an honest machine receives two conflicting reports from two peers, it has no way of knowing which peer is lying. In 1980 and 1982, Marshall Pease, Robert Shostak, and Leslie Lamport worked out the exact price of reaching agreement in the presence of such liars, and found two starkly different answers:

  • With plain messages that a relay can alter at will (oral messages), agreement is possible if and only if strictly more than two-thirds of the machines are honest: tolerating f traitors requires at least 3f + 1 machines in total.
  • With signed messages that cannot be forged or altered without detection (cryptographic signatures), agreement is possible for any number of traitors.

The first result prices every liar in honest machines; the second removes that headcount price by making lies impossible to pass along undetected. We tell both parts of the story with four bandits on a hilltop, their trained rats, and their signet rings.

The crown of Mount Phyle

Four bandits, out of sight of one another

Four bandits hold the rocky crown of Mount Phyle along with their stolen strongboxes. Bandit 1, their chief, sits inside a high stone ring wall with no gate, while Bandits 2, 3, and 4 keep lookout posts out on the wooded slopes, none within sight or earshot of another.* They can send word to one another only by trained rat, and tonight they have a single question to settle.

Only ratsEvery message travels by trained rat directly from one post to one other post, because no bandit can see or hear any of the others.
Some men lieNobody on the crown dies, and the rat runs between the posts are short and reliable. Their trouble is dishonesty: some among them may lie about what they saw or what they were told.
Below the cragFour mercenaries camp at the foot of the same hill. Their trouble is the exact opposite: none of them ever lies, but any one of them may die in the night, and their messenger birds can take arbitrarily long.*

Each end of Mount Phyle has a comrade it cannot count on: on the crown above, a comrade who may be lying; at the foot below, a comrade who may be dead. Once we see how the crown settles its business, we will know which of the two is the worse trouble to face.

One question, two answers

Hold, or scatter

The question before the bandits is whether to hold the hill and keep their loot, or scatter into the hills and abandon it. What matters above all is that every honest bandit does the same thing: having half the gang hold while the other half scatters is worse than either choice alone.

Which choice is wiser is not the issue here, and holding does not require anyone to leave their post. Nobody dies on the crown, and we are not telling the story of a single battle. We are looking for a communication rule that guarantees agreement every time.

Each of the four bandits forms an initial opinion of what should be done. The three guards at the lookout posts have each watched one approach up the slopes, while the chief inside the wall has seen only the sky and judges by the weight of the loot. Somehow, all four opinions must reach all four posts without being twisted along the way — which is difficult, because a liar can send "hold" to one comrade and "scatter" to another.

To solve this, Lamport, Shostak, and Pease reduce the problem to a single building block (Byzantine Generals): one person acts as the chief (the commanding general) and sends an order to the other three, who act as the guards (the lieutenants). Whatever rule they use for this broadcast must satisfy two strict conditions, alongside one observation about the role of the chief:

The first condition

Honest guards obey one order

Every honest guard must end up obeying the exact same order (interactive consistency condition IC1).

The second condition

An honest chief is obeyed

If the chief is honest, every honest guard must obey the exact order that the chief sent (condition IC2).

The chief is a job

Not a permanent rank

Bandit 1 sits inside the ring wall, but for the purpose of sharing reports, whoever's opinion is currently being circulated acts as the chief for that round.

Treating the chief as a temporary role allows a single broadcast method to distribute everyone's opinion. If the four bandits run the broadcast four times — once with each bandit acting as chief to send their observation — then every honest bandit ends up with the exact same list of four observations. Once they all hold the same four reports, they can apply any shared rule (such as taking the majority vote) and are guaranteed to reach the same final decision.

Notice that the two conditions do very unequal work. When the chief is honest, the second condition automatically satisfies the first: if every honest guard obeys the chief's order, they all obey the same order. When the chief is a liar, however, the second condition demands nothing at all — yet the first condition still applies in full force! Honest guards must still agree on a single order even when the chief deliberately sends different orders to different posts. That case is the heart of the entire problem.

Worse still, a liar can behave in any way they please: they can send contradictory orders, stay silent, relay false reports about what others said, or coordinate lies with another traitor. Neither the honest bandits on the hill nor we as observers are ever told who the liars are.

Test the two conditions below on any scenario. Pick whose turn it is to act as chief, choose which bandit is secretly lying, and toggle the final decision of each guard. Watch which condition fails, and see how the second condition stops caring the moment the chief is the liar.

Because the chief might be lying, a guard can never simply obey the slip that arrives from the chief. The natural remedy is for the guards to compare notes — asking one another what the chief told them before deciding. Whether that works depends entirely on how messages travel across the crown.

How word travels

The rats in the wall

On the crown, every message travels by trained rat, and a rat's run is short and predictable. That timing guarantee is what allows the guards to ask one another what the chief said and count on getting an answer before they have to act.

The paths over the rocks and through the chinks of the stone wall are direct, and no rat ever takes longer than a single turn of a sandglass. At dusk, before taking up their posts, all four bandits turned their sandglasses at the same signal, and each flips their glass again the instant it runs dry. One turn of the glass defines a synchronous round: within a round, everyone who has a slip to send sends it, and every slip sent is guaranteed to arrive before the sand runs out.

Each bandit keeps rats trained to run to each of the other three posts. To send a message, a bandit writes a slip, ties it to a rat using a cord knotted with their own post number, and lets the rat go. In the paper's terms, these plain slips are called oral messages, and the rats and sandglasses provide three essential guarantees (assumptions A1–A3):

What a rat gives · first

It arrives intact

Every slip that is sent arrives at its destination, and arrives exactly as its sender wrote it (assumption A1).

What a rat gives · second

The knots name the sender

Each rat runs on a dedicated line and carries the sender's knotted cord, so a receiver always knows which post sent a slip — no one can impersonate another post (assumption A2).

What a rat gives · third

Silence can be detected

Because every rat arrives before the sandglass empties, if no rat has arrived from a post when the round ends, nothing was sent from that post (assumption A3).

The first two guarantees come from having reliable point-to-point channels. The third guarantee is the crucial one: it comes from synchronous rounds and bounded message delays — the very guarantee that the mercenaries down at the foot of the hill lack.* Because silence is detectable at the end of a round, the bandits can assign it a fixed default meaning so a liar cannot stall the group by sending nothing: if no slip arrives before the glass runs out, it counts as hold. ("Hold" is chosen purely as a convention; any fixed default order works just as well.)

What plain slips do not provide is any way to verify the truth of a forwarded report. A slip says whatever its writer chooses to write on it. When Bandit 3 sends a rat to Bandit 2 claiming, "The chief told me to scatter," the knots on the cord prove that the slip came from Bandit 3 — but nothing proves that the chief actually told Bandit 3 to scatter.

So the guards can compare notes, and silence is automatically turned into a default answer, but no one can tell whether a peer's forwarded slip is honest or fabricated. To see why this matters, let us test the smallest nontrivial group: one chief and two guards, where one of the three is a liar.

The smallest party

Three cannot be brought to agree

In a party of three — one chief and two guards, with a single liar among them — comparing plain slips cannot bring the two honest bandits to agree. In fact, no method using plain slips ever can.

To see why, stand at the post of an honest guard, Bandit 2, and compare two possible nights. On the first night, the chief is honest and sends "hold" to both guards. Bandit 3, however, is a liar, and tells Bandit 2, "The chief told me to scatter." Because the chief is honest on this night, our second condition requires Bandit 2 to obey the chief's true order and hold.

On the second night, the roles of liar and honest man are swapped: the chief is the liar, sending "hold" to Bandit 2 and "scatter" to Bandit 3, while Bandit 3 is completely honest and truthfully reports to Bandit 2, "The chief told me to scatter." On both nights, Bandit 2 ends up holding the exact same two slips: "hold" directly from the chief, and a report from Bandit 3 saying "the chief sent me scatter." Because the two nights look identical from Bandit 2's post, Bandit 2 must make the same decision in both. And since the first night requires Bandit 2 to obey the honest chief and hold, Bandit 2 must also hold on the second night.

Now apply the exact same reasoning to Bandit 3. Compare the second night with a mirror-image night where the chief honestly sends "scatter" to both guards and Bandit 2 lies. To obey the honest chief in that mirror night, Bandit 3 must scatter whenever they hear "scatter" from the chief and "hold" from Bandit 2. Consequently, on the second night — where both guards are honest and the chief lied — Bandit 2 holds while Bandit 3 scatters, violating the first condition.

The core obstacle is an indistinguishability trap at the guard's post: a lying chief and a lying fellow guard leave an honest guard holding the exact same pair of conflicting slips, yet the two situations demand opposite actions.

As Lamport, Shostak, and Pease note, this informal two-round picture explains the intuition, while their 1980 paper supplies the full mathematical proof showing that no number of additional rounds of plain messages can ever break the tie. Three participants simply cannot tolerate one liar. How many participants does it take?

Proof — that three cannot be brought to agree

The two conditions are the paper's IC1 and IC2, for a commanding general and two lieutenants communicating by oral messages with at most one traitor. Scenarios A, B, and C correspond to the three nights above; the rigorous multi-round proof appears in Pease, Shostak, and Lamport (1980). Claim. No oral-message protocol allows three participants to satisfy both IC1 and IC2 in the presence of one traitor.

  1. Suppose for contradiction that some protocol works for three participants with up to one liar: all honest guards obey the same order (IC1), and an honest chief's order is obeyed by every honest guard (IC2).
  2. Scenario A. The chief is honest and sends hold to both guards; the second guard lies and reports the chief sent me scatter.
  3. Scenario B. The chief is the liar, sending hold to the first guard and scatter to the second guard; both guards are honest and report truthfully what they received.
  4. The first guard receives the exact same messages in Scenario A and Scenario B, and so must make the same decision in both. In Scenario A the chief is honest, so by IC2 the first guard must decide hold; therefore the first guard also decides hold in Scenario B.
  5. Scenario C (the mirror of A). The chief is honest and sends scatter to both guards, while the first guard lies and reports hold. By IC2, the second guard must decide scatter in Scenario C. Because Scenario B looks identical to Scenario C from the second guard's perspective, the second guard also decides scatter in Scenario B.
  6. In Scenario B, both guards are honest, yet the first decides hold and the second decides scatter, violating IC1.∎
What a liar costs

Four men to a liar

With plain slips, tolerating m liars requires strictly more than 3m participants — at least 3m + 1 in all. One liar requires at least four bandits, two liars require seven, and three liars require ten. Because the crown of Mount Phyle has four bandits, it can handle one liar and no more.

This general 3m + 1 bound follows directly from the impossibility of three. Suppose someone claimed to have a protocol that could tolerate m liars among 3m or fewer participants. Then three real bandits (one of whom is a liar) could simulate that entire protocol among themselves! Each of the three real bandits would simply play the role of at most m simulated participants in their head. Because only one real bandit is a liar, at most m simulated participants would lie — which the hypothetical protocol claims to handle. The three real bandits would then reach agreement despite one liar, which we already know is impossible.

Even weakening the goal to approximate agreement does not reduce this headcount cost, because any approximate agreement protocol can be converted back into exact agreement. Use the buttons below to see what each additional liar costs in total bandits and in rounds of exchanged slips.

The impossibility proof tells us what cannot be done with 3m or fewer participants. Once we have at least 3m + 1 participants, however, Lamport, Shostak, and Pease give an algorithm that always works: we call it the Repeating (the paper's recursive oral-message algorithm, OM(m)). It relies on one simple rule called the greater count (a majority function): when a guard collects a set of reports, if a majority of them agree on the same order, the guard chooses that order; otherwise, if there is a tie, the guard falls back to "hold."

For four bandits tolerating one liar (OM(1)), the Repeating takes two rounds: in Round 1, the chief sends an order to all three guards; in Round 2, each guard relays what they heard from the chief to the other two guards; finally, each guard takes the greater count of the three reports at their post (the direct slip from the chief plus the two relayed slips from their fellow guards). 5 · Another night

Now consider the harder case: the chief is the liar, and sends conflicting orders in Round 1 — scatter to Bandit 2, hold to Bandit 3, and scatter to Bandit 4. Since there is only one liar on the hill, all three guards are honest.

6 · Second round

Every guard relays faithfully

In Round 2, because all three guards are honest, each guard truthfully tells the other two exactly what the chief sent them. As a result, every guard ends up holding the exact same three reports: scatter (from Post 2), hold (from Post 3), and scatter (from Post 4).

7 · The greater count

All three guards go the same way

Every guard holds two reports of scatter and one report of hold, so the greater count at every post is scatter. Notice that nobody had to figure out who lied: by ensuring all honest guards hold the same set of reports, the majority vote forces them to move together.

In both cases, the honest guards finish in complete agreement, and whenever the chief is honest they obey the chief's order. Even so, the price of using plain slips is steep: every additional liar requires three more bandits and another round of relayed reports. All of that expense stems from a single weakness — that anyone relaying a plain slip can quietly rewrite it. What if a slip could not be altered?

Proof — that fewer than four men to a liar cannot be made to work

Let n be the total number of participants and m the maximum number of liars the protocol tolerates. The two conditions are IC1 and IC2. Claim. No oral-message protocol can tolerate m liars when n ≤ 3m.

  1. Suppose for contradiction that a protocol tolerates m liars among n ≤ 3m simulated participants (call them parts).
  2. Have three real bandits — one of whom is a liar — simulate those n parts: the real chief simulates the parts' chief plus at most m − 1 simulated guards, and each of the two real guards simulates at most m simulated guards. Since n ≤ 3m, all n parts can be partitioned this way.
  3. Messages between two parts simulated by the same bandit are passed silently inside that bandit's head; messages between parts simulated by different bandits are sent by rat and arrive intact and properly attributed.
  4. Because at most one real bandit is a liar, and that bandit simulates at most m parts, at most m simulated parts behave dishonestly. By assumption, the protocol satisfies IC1 and IC2 among the simulated parts.
  5. By IC1, all parts simulated by an honest bandit agree on the same order, which that real bandit adopts; by IC2, if the real chief is honest, all parts simulated by the real chief are honest, so every honest part (and thus every honest real bandit) obeys the real chief's order. Thus three real bandits solve Byzantine agreement against one liar, which is impossible.∎
The Repeating, stated formally

The Repeating for m liars is the paper's recursive algorithm OM(m), and the greater count is its function majority. Guarantee. OM(m) satisfies IC1 and IC2 for any n > 3m in the presence of at most m traitors.

  1. (1) The chief sends its order to every guard.
  2. (2) For each guard i, let vi be the order received from the chief (or hold if no slip arrived). If m = 0, guard i immediately obeys vi. Otherwise (m > 0), guard i acts as the chief in OM(m − 1) to send vi to the remaining n − 2 guards.
  3. (3) For each i, once all OM(m − 1) sub-broadcasts finish, guard i holds its own vi together with the n − 2 values vj (j ≠ i) obtained from the other guards' OM(m − 1) broadcasts, and obeys majority(v1, …, vn−1).
Proof — that the Repeating meets both conditions above the bound

Let n be the number of participants, m the recursion depth of OM(m), k the actual number of traitors present, and v the chief's order. Claim. OM(m) satisfies IC1 and IC2 whenever n > 3m and k ≤ m.

  1. Lemma 1. For any m and k, OM(m) satisfies IC2 (an honest chief is obeyed) whenever n > 2k + m and at most k traitors are present. Proof by induction on m: For m = 0, an honest chief sends v directly and every honest guard obeys it. Suppose the lemma holds for m − 1.
  2. In OM(m), the honest chief sends v to all n − 1 guards. Each honest guard j then runs OM(m − 1) among the n − 1 guards to relay v. Since n − 1 > 2k + (m − 1), the induction hypothesis guarantees that every honest guard obtains vj = v for every honest guard j.
  3. Because n − 1 > 2k, strictly more than half of the n − 1 guards are honest. Thus a strict majority of the n − 1 values held by each honest guard equal v, and majority returns v.
  4. Main Theorem (by induction on m). For m = 0 (k = 0), no one lies and both IC1 and IC2 hold trivially. For m > 0, if the chief is honest, setting k ≤ m gives n > 3m ≥ 2k + m, so Lemma 1 guarantees IC2, which immediately implies IC1.
  5. If the chief is a traitor, then at most m − 1 of the guards are traitors, and there are n − 1 > 3m − 1 > 3(m − 1) guards. By the induction hypothesis applied to OM(m − 1), for every guard j's sub-broadcast, all honest guards obtain the exact same relayed value vj.
  6. Consequently, every honest guard holds the exact same vector of values (v1, …, vn−1) and computes the exact same majority, satisfying IC1 (while IC2 holds vacuously because the chief is faulty).∎
A slip that cannot be altered

The seal rings

Everything changes once the bandits use slips that cannot be altered without detection. Each bandit on the crown wears a signet ring carved with their post number. When sending an order under seal, its writer stamps it in wax, and anyone relaying that slip stamps their own seal beside the previous ones.

Thus a slip reading hold : 1 : 2 carries Bandit 1's original order to hold, countersigned and forwarded by Bandit 2, and its chain of wax impressions records every hand it has passed through. In the paper's terms, these are signed messages (assumption A4), and their rules are simple:

A seal · first

It cannot be forged

An honest bandit's wax seal cannot be counterfeited by anyone else, and any change to the words on a sealed slip breaks the seal.

A seal · second

Anyone can verify it

Every bandit on the hill can inspect a wax impression and tell whose signet ring made it.

A seal · what is not assumed

Traitors may collude or stay silent

Nothing prevents two liars from lending each other their rings, and a seal cannot force a liar to speak: a liar can still refuse to forward a slip, though they cannot alter what an honest bandit sealed.

Because an honest seal cannot be forged, a guard can no longer lie about what an honest chief sent! That unlocks a much simpler algorithm, the Sealed Repeating (the paper's signed-message algorithm, SM(m)). Each guard keeps a sheaf (a set) of properly sealed orders they have seen, and at the end applies a fixed deterministic standing rule (the paper's function choice) to pick a single order from their sheaf — defaulting to "hold" if the sheaf is empty.

To tolerate up to m liars, the chief stamps an order with their seal and sends it to every guard. Whenever a guard receives a validly sealed order that is not yet in their sheaf, they add it to their sheaf. If that slip carries fewer than m guards' seals (not counting the chief's), the guard stamps their own seal onto the chain and forwards the slip to every guard whose seal is not already on it. Once Round m + 1 ends, each guard applies the standing rule to their sheaf. Because every honest guard relays any new order before the m-seal limit is reached, every honest guard ends up with the exact same orders in their sheaf — and identical sheaves produce identical decisions.

With seal rings, even three bandits can handle one liar! If the chief is honest, no guard can forge the chief's seal on a different order, so both guards' sheaves contain only the chief's true order. And what if a lying chief tries to split the two guards by sealing "hold" for Bandit 2 and "scatter" for Bandit 3? In Round 2, each guard stamps and forwards their slip to the other. Both guards' sheaves now contain the exact same two orders — hold : 1 and scatter : 1 — so the standing rule sends both guards the same way! Better yet, each guard now holds two contradictory orders bearing the chief's own unforgeable seal, proving beyond doubt that the chief lied.

As a result, the 3m + 1 headcount barrier vanishes completely. Under seal, a group of any size can tolerate any number of liars m (with n ≥ m + 2, since with fewer than two honest participants there is no pair of honest peers left to agree with one another).

The Sealed Repeating, stated formally

The Sealed Repeating for m liars is the paper's algorithm SM(m); a wax seal corresponds to an unforgeable cryptographic signature (assumption A4), and guard i's sheaf is the set Vi. The standing rule is the deterministic function choice. A sealed slip hold : 1 : 2 is written v : 0 : j1 in the paper, with the chief (general 0) signing first.

  1. (1) The chief signs an order v and sends v : 0 to every guard, initializing each guard's sheaf Vi to empty.
  2. (2) If guard i receives a validly signed slip v : 0 : j1 : … : jk and v is not yet in Vi, guard i adds v to Vi; if in addition k < m, guard i signs the slip to form v : 0 : j1 : … : jk : i and sends it to every guard not already in {j1, …, jk}.
  3. (3) After Round m + 1, when no further valid slips can arrive, each guard i obeys choice(Vi).
Proof — that the Sealed Repeating works against any number of liars

Let m be the maximum number of traitors tolerated, i and j be any two honest guards, and k be the number of guards' seals on a slip (excluding the chief's). Claim. For any number of participants, if at most m are traitors, SM(m) satisfies both IC1 and IC2.

  1. IC2 (Honest chief). An honest chief signs only a single order v and sends v : 0 to all guards in Round 1. Because no traitor can forge the chief's signature, no other order can ever be validly signed by the chief. Thus every honest guard i has Vi = {v} and obeys choice({v}) = v.
  2. IC1 (Lying chief). When the chief is a traitor, it suffices to show that for any two honest guards i and j, Vi = Vj; applying the same function choice to identical sets then yields the same decision. Suppose an honest guard i adds v to Vi upon receiving v : 0 : j1 : … : jk.
  3. If k = 0 (received directly from the chief in Round 1), then since m ≥ 1 (otherwise the chief could not be a traitor), guard i signs and forwards v : 0 : i to every other guard, so j receives it and puts v in Vj.
  4. If j is already among the signers {j1, …, jk}, then since j is honest and its seal cannot be forged, j has already put v into Vj.
  5. If j is not among {j1, …, jk} and k < m, then guard i signs and forwards the slip to every guard not yet on the list, including j.
  6. If j is not among {j1, …, jk} and k = m, note that because the chief is already one of the at most m traitors, at most m − 1 guards are traitors. Therefore, among the m distinct guard signatures j1, …, jm on the slip, at least one belongs to an honest guard — and when that honest guard first added v to its sheaf (at a step when fewer than m guards had signed), it forwarded the slip to every remaining guard, including j.
  7. Notice that nowhere in this proof did we count the total number of participants n. The 3m + 1 bound is a price of mutable messages, not of lying itself, and unforgeable signatures eliminate it.∎
The two ends of the hill

The two halves

Set side by side, the two ends of Mount Phyle reach completely opposite outcomes. High on the crown, the bandits reach guaranteed agreement in just a couple of rounds: four bandits can outvote one liar with plain slips, and with wax seals they can handle any number of liars.

Down at the foot of the hill, where no mercenary ever lies but a comrade may die in the night and a messenger bird can take arbitrarily long in the pines, no deterministic rule can ever guarantee that the camp reaches a decision at all.* The bandits are no cleverer than the mercenaries; what saves the bandits is their sandglasses and the short, bounded runs of their rats. Each camp is missing a single distinction, and it is the exact same shape of uncertainty in two different guises:

Below · at the foot

A dead man, or a slow bird

A mercenary staring at an empty perch cannot tell whether the comrade in that tent is dead or whether their bird is merely lingering in a pine tree. Because stopping too soon risks disagreeing with a live comrade, they can never safely stop waiting.

Above · on the crown

A lying chief, or a lying peer

A guard holding two conflicting slips cannot tell whether the chief sent two different orders or the fellow guard lied in their relay. No single person's word can ever be trusted alone, and the honest guards almost never learn who the liar was.

Notice that neither protocol ever identifies the culprit. Down below, no one ever learns whether a silent tent holds a dead comrade or a slow bird; up above, the honest bandits reach agreement without ever unmasking the liar (unless a careless chief signs two conflicting orders under seal). Good distributed algorithms do not try to diagnose who failed — they are built so that the honest participants can move together without needing to know.

Between the two troubles — dishonesty and unbounded delay — the worse trouble is the lack of a clock. When messages arrive within a known time bound, silence carries information: by the time the sandglass runs out, every slip that was going to arrive has arrived, and lies can be outvoted or exposed by seals. When delays have no bound, silence means nothing at all.

The crown was a group of machines

Now read the story of the crown again with computers at the posts. Every bandit is a machine that may suffer arbitrary (Byzantine) faults, every rat is a network message, and a wax seal is a cryptographic signature that cannot be forged. On the crown
In the machine room

With plain messages, machines can agree despite liars only if more than two-thirds are honest. With unforgeable signatures, they can agree regardless of how many lie.

Neither method needs to identify which machine lied, and both rely on a crucial timing assumption: being able to detect silence at the end of a round, which requires bounded message delays and synchronized clocks.

On the crown, the bandits go by number rather than by name: Bandit 1 is the chief inside the ring wall, and Bandits 2, 3, and 4 hold the lookout posts on the slopes. The mercenaries at the foot of the hill are numbered separately.

The ideas. A commanding general must send an order to n − 1 lieutenants so that (IC1) all loyal lieutenants obey the same order and (IC2) if the commander is loyal, every loyal lieutenant obeys the commander's order. IC2 implies IC1 when the commander is loyal, so the entire difficulty lies in satisfying IC1 when the commander is a traitor. With oral messages (plain slips whose contents a relay can falsify), no solution exists for three generals in the presence of one traitor, because a loyal lieutenant cannot tell a lying commander from a lying peer; by a reduction argument in which three generals simulate a larger system, no oral-message solution exists for n ≤ 3m generals with m traitors. Above that bound (n ≥ 3m + 1), the recursive algorithm OM(m) works in m + 1 rounds: the commander sends an order, each lieutenant relays what it received via OM(m − 1), and each takes the majority of the resulting values. Adding unforgeable cryptographic signatures (SM(m)) removes the 3m + 1 headcount requirement entirely: traitors can no longer falsify what a loyal general signed, and relaying signed chains of length up to m ensures that every loyal lieutenant collects the exact same set of orders. Both algorithms rely on assumption A3 — that the absence of a message can be detected by the end of a round — which requires synchronous rounds and bounded message delays.
