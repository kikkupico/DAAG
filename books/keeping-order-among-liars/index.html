Keeping Order Among Liars

Can a replicated service keep all of its commands in the exact same order when some of its machines lie, including the leader, and when messages across the network can be delayed for arbitrarily long?

To find out, we return to the Tholos of Schedia. All five legislators have sailed across the sea, leaving four unvetted stewards in charge of the council's law scroll—ensuring that every honest copy records the same lines in the same order even when one of them, the writer included, is a liar.

We find out by following the protocol directly: four replicas of a service, one of which may be faulty, the primary included, that must keep every copy of the log in the same order while messages can be delayed for any length of time.

After Miguel Castro and Barbara Liskov, Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance, 1999

The law grows one line at a time. A line is a single decree added to the law, such as harbour dues are two obols a hull or the cove is closed to nets in spring, and any citizen at the door may petition for one. Crucially, every honest copy of the scroll must record the lines in the exact same order, because the meaning of a new line depends on what the scroll already says. Stated formally · a line, from numbering to standing

In the paper's terminology, the writer is the primary, the numbering is a PRE-PREPARE, the first echo is a PREPARE, readied is the predicate prepared(m, v, n, i) (holding the pre-prepare and 2f matching prepares from backups), the second echo is a COMMIT, and standing is committed-local(m, v, n, i) (prepared plus 2f + 1 matching commits). Writing in number order is deterministic state-machine execution in sequence-number order. (Castro and Liskov, §4.2.)

  1. Numbering (Pre-prepare). The writer of term v assigns sequence number 7 to line X and sends a sealed message ⟨PRE-PREPARE, v, 7, X⟩ to all benches.
  2. First echo (Prepare). A steward accepts the numbering if the seal belongs to term v's writer, the steward is currently in term v, has not already accepted a different line for number 7 in term v, and 7 lies within the allowed sequence-number window (see settling checkpoints below). Upon accepting, the steward sends ⟨PREPARE, v, 7, X⟩ to all benches. Once a steward holds the numbering and two matching first echoes from benches other than the writer (three distinct seals on (v, 7, X)), the line is readied at that bench.
  3. Second echo (Commit). Once (v, 7, X) is readied at a bench, that steward sends ⟨COMMIT, v, 7, X⟩ to all benches. Once a steward holds the readied line and three matching second echoes (possibly including their own), the line stands at that bench.
  4. Execution. Each steward writes a standing line into their copy of the scroll as soon as every lower sequence number has been written, and sends a sealed confirmation to the citizen.

Proof · that the three seals of readiness cannot be split

In the paper's terms, a line is a request m, a term is a view v, a sequence number is n, and there are 3f + 1 = 4 replicas (f = 1). Invariant (§4.2). If prepared(m, v, n, i) is true, then prepared(m′, v, n, j) is false for every non-faulty replica j and every m′ ≠ m with different digest.

  1. For a line to be readied at any bench in term v, that bench must hold seals for that line, sequence number, and term from three distinct stewards (the writer's numbering plus two first echoes from other benches).
  2. Suppose for contradiction that two different lines X and Y were both readied for sequence number 7 in term v. The set of three stewards who sealed X and the set of three stewards who sealed Y are subsets of four stewards, so by the pigeonhole principle they share at least 3 + 3 − 4 = 2 stewards. Since at most one steward is a liar, at least one steward in their intersection is honest.
  3. That honest steward must have sealed both X and Y for sequence number 7 in term v. However, an honest steward accepts and echoes at most one line per sequence number in any given term, and an honest steward's wax seal cannot be forged. Contradiction.∎

Why four stewards are the minimum for one liar, even with seals: in a calm council where every runner arrived within a known round, wax seals allowed just three members to agree despite one liar. Four are needed now because messengers can be delayed across the stone floor for an unknown time. Since one steward might be a liar who stays deliberately silent, no bench can ever afford to wait for all of them; it must be able to act after hearing from all but the number of liars ($n - f = 3$). Proof · that a new writer must keep what stood before

In the paper's terms (§4.5.1): non-faulty replicas agree on the sequence numbers of requests that commit locally, even if they commit in different views at different replicas. Recall that committed-local(m, v, n, i) holds when prepared(m, v, n, i) holds and replica i has collected 2f + 1 matching COMMIT messages.

  1. Suppose line X stands (commits locally) as sequence number 7 at some honest bench in term v. Then three distinct stewards sent second echoes (COMMIT) for (v, 7, X). Since at most one steward lies, at least two honest stewards had (v, 7, X) readied (prepared) in term v.
  2. Consider the earliest term v′ > v in which any line is readied for sequence number 7. To open term v′, its writer must present three signed VIEW-CHANGE requests. By pigeonhole intersection among four benches, those three requests share at least one honest steward with the two honest stewards who readied X in term v.
  3. Because no line was readied for number 7 in any term strictly between v and v′, the highest-term readied proof for number 7 in the three VIEW-CHANGE requests is (v, 7, X) (since by the single-term invariant no conflicting line could have been readied in term v). Therefore, the rule of NEW-VIEW forces the writer of term v′ to re-propose X for number 7, and honest stewards verify this proof before echoing. By induction over all subsequent terms, only X can ever be readied or stand for number 7.∎

Every hundred lines, each steward writes a compact summary of their scroll state, seals it, and sends it to all benches. The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. Miguel Castro and Barbara Liskov, Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance. Proceedings of the Third Symposium on Operating Systems Design and Implementation (OSDI), New Orleans, February 1999. Introduces the three-phase normal-case protocol, view changes, stable checkpoints, the partial-synchrony liveness mechanism, and the MAC-based cryptographic optimization.

The ideas. Castro and Liskov present the first practical state-machine replication protocol that tolerates f arbitrary (Byzantine) faults out of 3f + 1 replicas in an asynchronous network, guaranteeing linearizable safety with no timing assumptions at all and liveness once message delays stop growing faster than time indefinitely.

Computation proceeds through numbered views, in each of which a single replica acts as the primary (chosen round-robin as p = v mod n) and assigns sequence numbers to client requests. Normal-case operation uses three phases: PRE-PREPARE (the primary proposes a sequence number for a request), PREPARE (replicas broadcast their agreement on that numbering), and COMMIT (replicas broadcast that they hold a prepared certificate of 2f + 1 matching messages). Because any two quorums of size 2f + 1 out of 3f + 1 overlap in at least f + 1 replicas — at least one of which is non-faulty — the prepare phase guarantees that no two conflicting requests can ever prepare at the same sequence number within a view. The commit phase ensures that if any non-faulty replica commits a request locally, at least f + 1 non-faulty replicas have prepared it.

When a backup's timer expires waiting for a request to execute, it votes for a view change, sending its prepared certificates to the next primary. The new primary gathers 2f + 1 signed VIEW-CHANGE messages into a NEW-VIEW message and re-proposes the highest-view prepared request for each sequence number. Because 2f + 1 view-change messages must intersect the f + 1 non-faulty replicas that prepared any locally committed request, committed requests are preserved across views. Exponentially doubling view-change timeouts and joining a higher view upon seeing f + 1 view-change messages ensure liveness once delays stabilize, while periodic 2f + 1 stable checkpoints garbage-collect the log and bound acceptable sequence numbers between water marks h and H.
