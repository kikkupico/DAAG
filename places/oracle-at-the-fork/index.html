Students Asking Which Life to Lead

Groups of students who ask an oracle whether the work they mean to do together lets them live as scholars, apart and never waiting, or makes them live as legislators, who wait for everyone.

Read this first, and the book set at the oracle can be read on its own. It says who comes to the oracle and what they ask, what carries word between them once they are settled, and what is sent. The sanctuary itself, at the fork of the island, comes last.

The Students and the Priestess

Groups of students come to the fork of Paxos when their schooling is done, before they choose how to live. A group means to do some work together, and the choice is between two lives. Legislators live as the assemblies of the Chamber and the Tholos do: they keep a roster of their members and settle nothing until every name on it has been heard from. Scholars live as the libraries of the coast do: each takes a post at a library, keeps no roster, and answers from whatever has reached it.

The priestess of the oracle tells each group which life its work allows. She never asks who the students are or what they know, and she sees no fact they have not seen. She reads only the work they mean to do, and its shape decides her answer.

A group's work is always a question its members mean to answer, once they are settled at their posts in the libraries, from the facts scattered across the libraries' shelves: which copies have been made, which works cite which, which readers are waiting. No post holds all the facts.

Couriers

Once the students are at their posts, facts travel from the library that holds them to the libraries that need them by courier, and they arrive late, in batches or out of order. A courier may be stopped on the road, and while he is stopped nobody can say whether more is coming.

Nothing else carries word. No library can look into another's shelves, and no bell or beacon tells the coast that every courier has arrived. The oracle hears nothing from the coast either: what it knows of a group's work is what the group wrote down.

Two Kinds of Work

Some work only grows. Has at least one copy of this work been made? can be answered yes as soon as a single letter says so, and once it is yes it is yes for ever, whatever news comes later. A post can answer from the letters it holds, with no roster and no waiting. The priestess sends such a group south, to live as scholars.

Other work rests on an absence. Is this every copy that has been made? or Has no one claimed this? can be answered only by hearing from everyone, since a single late courier could carry the letter that overturns the answer. That needs a roster and a wait for every name on it, and the priestess sends such a group north, to live as legislators.

Most work is mixed, and the counsel is still one road or the other. A group whose work grows in every step but one may still go south, if it closes that one step with a manifest, so that only that step waits, and only for the notices the manifest names.

The Oracle at the Fork

Paxos is shaped like a Y. Two arms reach north and face each other across a bay: the western arm holds the Chamber of the Paxon Parliament, the eastern arm the city of Schedia and its Tholos. Below the fork the stem widens into the scholars' coast. The island road runs up the stem, forks, and runs out along both arms.

The oracle stands at the fork, on common ground that belongs to neither city, as the Messon on Lesbos was the common sanctuary of that island's cities. It is a sanctuary in the manner of Dodona: a low wall encloses an old oak, with a small temple and an altar beside the tree. The enclosure has two gates. The north gate opens on the road that forks up the two arms, to the Chamber and the Tholos; the south gate on the road down the stem, to the scholars' coast.

The oracle does not rule on anything, and the students it counsels do not join the Parliament or the council of Schedia. A group sent north goes to live as those bodies live, and a group sent south as the libraries live. Putting both cities and the scholars on one island makes the difference a walk.

Where the allegory leaks

Real oracles were famous for answers that could be read two ways: Croesus was told that if he crossed the Halys he would destroy a great empire, and the empire was his own (Herodotus 1.53)†. This oracle is never vague and never wrong, because the theorem behind it is exact: the shape of the work decides, and the priestess only says so. She foresees nothing.
