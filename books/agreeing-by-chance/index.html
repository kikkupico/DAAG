Agreeing by Chance

When messages can take arbitrarily long and even a single computer might crash without warning, no deterministic plan can guarantee that the group will ever reach agreement. But what if a machine that finds itself deadlocked simply flips a coin? Can pure chance break a tie that no fixed rule can escape?

To see how, we return to the four Greek mercenaries camped at the foot of Mount Phyle and watch how a silver coin, tossed privately by each man inside his tent, breaks their deadlock and brings the camp to a single decision.

To see how, we follow the protocol round by round. Four processes, stuck in a deadlock that no fixed rule can escape, each flip a private random bit—and that is enough to bring the system to a single decision.

After Michael Ben-Or, Another Advantage of Free Choice: Completely Asynchronous Agreement Protocols, 1983

What it bought

Probability one vs. every path

Is it quick?

Private coins vs. shared coins

When N is large and the adversary schedules messages worst-first, waiting for N independent coins to land alike can take an exponential number of rounds on average (though when faults are at most O(√N) and messages arrive in lockstep, it takes only a constant number of rounds!). Later protocols replaced private coins with a cryptographic shared coin to finish in a small constant number of rounds on average.

At the foot of the hill
In computing

When an asynchronous machine cannot decide from messages alone, flipping a random coin guarantees that no two machines ever decide differently and that every working machine decides with probability one.

The FLP impossibility theorem still holds for deterministic rules. Randomization escapes it by changing one assumption—requiring a working majority, and trading worst-case termination for termination with probability one.

Later randomized consensus protocols replaced independent local coins with a cryptographic common coin, bringing the expected number of rounds down to a small constant.

The paper behind this book

Michael Ben-Or, Another Advantage of Free Choice: Completely Asynchronous Agreement Protocols (extended abstract). Proceedings of the 2nd ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), Montreal, August 1983, 27–30. Presents the randomized consensus protocol for crash failures with N > 2t (Theorem 1), the Byzantine protocol with N > 5t (Theorem 2), and constant expected rounds when t = O(√N) (Theorem 3).

The ideas. Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson proved that no deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system if even a single process may crash: an adversarial scheduler can always delay and order messages to keep the system bivalent forever. Crucially, their proof relies on determinism, which lets the adversary predict how each process will react to any message delivery.

Ben-Or showed that local randomness breaks the adversary's control without requiring any timing assumptions at all. Processes execute numbered rounds of two message phases each. In Phase 1 of round r, each process broadcasts its preference and waits for N − t reports; if strictly more than N/2 reports favor the same value v, it broadcasts a Phase 2 decision proposal D(v), and otherwise broadcasts “?”. In Phase 2, it waits for N − t Phase 2 messages: if it sees more than t proposals D(v), it decides v; if it sees at least one D(v), it adopts v for round r + 1; and if it sees no D-proposals at all, it flips a private fair coin to choose 0 or 1 for round r + 1.

Safety holds unconditionally on every execution: because two strict majorities of N must overlap, at most one value v can be proposed with D in any round; and if any process decides v (seeing at least t + 1 messages D(v)), every other process (missing at most t messages) sees at least one D(v), adopts v without flipping a coin, and decides v in the next round. Termination holds with probability 1 whenever N > 2t (rigorously proved against an adaptive adversary by Aguilera and Toueg in 2012). Non-terminating executions still exist, but their total probability measure is zero.
