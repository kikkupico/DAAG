What You Can Promise Alone

Based on “Highly Available Transactions: Virtues and Limitations” by Peter Bailis, Aaron Davidson, Alan Fekete, Ali Ghodsi, Joseph M. Hellerstein and Ion Stoica (2014)

When a librarian cannot stop to check with the other libraries on every request, what can he still promise a reader on his own, and what can no one promise alone?

In the reading room of every library on the scholars' coast, a reader may ask for several works or leave several corrections in one visit, while the couriers may be stopped for as long as they like. The librarian answers from his own shelves and waits for no one. Some promises he can keep that way, some only if the reader keeps to his library, and some no librarian can keep alone: that two readers never borrow the last copy, and that a copy is the latest.

Setting The Scholars' Coast

In a Nutshell

Readers come to the libraries of the scholars' coast with business that takes several steps, and the couriers between libraries may be stopped for as long as they like. A librarian who must answer from his own shelves without waiting for anyone can still promise that no reader is shown a half-made correction, that corrections bound together are seen all or none, that a reader gets the same answer twice and never an older one, and, if the reader keeps to one library, that he finds his own corrections and sees letters before their replies. He cannot promise that two readers will not borrow the last copy, nor that a copy is the latest. In real terms, this covers highly available transactions: which isolation levels and consistency guarantees survive a network partition, and which cannot.

Chapter I

The Couriers Stop

Every library on the scholars' coast has a reading room, where a reader comes with business. The library holds copies of works, and what the other libraries know reaches it by courier; a courier can be delayed, and couriers can be stopped altogether, cutting one part of the coast off from the rest for as long as they stay stopped*. A librarian answers a reader from the copy he holds. Since a stopped courier can be stopped for any length of time, what can the librarian still promise?

What counts as answering alone? Every reader who can reach a library that is open is answered, whatever the couriers are doing and however long they have been stopped. The librarian does not wait for another library to reply, nor for a courier to return, nor for a majority of libraries to agree; and he does not answer "probably". A librarian who must hear from most of the coast before he can speak does not answer alone, because the day the couriers stop he cannot speak. The promise is only worth having if it holds on that day.

Is it enough for the librarian merely to reply? No: a librarian who answered every reader "come back later" would always reply. A visit must be able to finish. A reader may withdraw from his own visit, as is his right, and the library may not turn him away on its own account for ever; given an open library, the reader's visit is eventually finished or withdrawn by him. That is the whole of the setup: a visit is answered from the shelves at hand, and it ends.

If a librarian cannot ask anyone and still must answer, which promises about a reader's visit can he keep?

Chapter II

A Reader's Visit

A reader's business at one library is a visit: several works asked for, or several corrections left, one after another, before the reader goes. The promises a reader might want are about what he sees during a visit and across visits, and they come in a good many kinds. Is he ever shown a correction its writer has not finished or has dropped? Does he see half of a set of corrections that were made together? Does asking twice for the same work give two answers? Does a later visit show something older than an earlier one? Does he find the corrections he left himself? Does he see a reply before the letter it answers? Can two readers each borrow the last copy? Is the copy he is handed the latest?

Which of these can a librarian keep alone? They fall into three groups, and the rest of the book takes each in turn. Some he can keep for any reader who comes to any library, whatever the couriers do. Some he can keep only if the reader keeps to one library, coming back always to the same door, which is itself something a reader may choose to do. And some no librarian can keep alone, because to keep them he would have to know what the other libraries are doing at that moment, which is exactly what a stopped courier prevents.

Is the order of the groups a ranking of how good the promises are? Not quite. The promises of the first group are the more modest, but they are the ones that cost the reader nothing; each of the second asks a little of him; and the third are the ones most people would want first. The question is what the second and the third groups need that the first does not.

Which of the promises that can be kept alone is the most basic: what must a reader never be shown?

Chapter III

No Half-Made Corrections

A reader leaves three corrections to three works, writing them one after another. Meanwhile another reader comes to the same library and asks for one of those works. What may the librarian show him? Not a correction its writer has not finished, since the writer may yet abandon it, and a correction that is shown and then dropped has been read by someone as though it stood. The first promise is therefore that no reader is shown a correction that was abandoned or is not yet complete.

How can a librarian keep that alone? By keeping a writer's corrections apart until the visit is over. The reader writes them in the librarian's hands, not onto the shelves, and when he finishes, all of them go onto the shelves together; if he withdraws, none do. Nothing in this waits for another library: the librarian does it with what he has at hand. What it does not give is any promise that the reader asking will be shown the newest corrections, only that whatever he is shown was finished.

Is there a second, smaller promise? Yes. When two readers at two libraries correct the same works in ignorance of each other, the libraries will later meet, and their corrections must be ordered the same way for every work. Suppose one visit's corrections to two works are ordered before another visit's on the first work and after it on the second: the shelves would then hold the first visit's correction of one work and the second visit's of the other, a mixture of the two visits which neither reader wrote. To prevent this, every correction in a visit carries the same tally, and where two corrections to one work meet, the larger tally wins, as it always does*. A visit then wins on every work it touched or on none.

Proof — that Read Uncommitted and Read Committed are achievable with high availability
  1. This is §5.1.1. Read Uncommitted (Adya's PL-1) requires that writes to each object are totally ordered and that the order is consistent across objects: the graph of transactions with an edge from Ti to Tj when Tj overwrites Ti's write must have no cycle (no G0). Mark every write of a transaction with one timestamp unique across transactions (a client's identifier combined with a sequence number) and let each replica apply last-writer-wins by timestamp.
  2. Then for any two transactions T1 and T2 that write to the same two items x and y, all of T1's writes carry the same timestamp, and so does all of T2's; whichever has the larger timestamp wins on both. It cannot happen that wx(1) precedes wx(2) while wy(2) precedes wy(1). No replica contacts any other to decide, so every replica can respond.
  3. Read Committed (PL-2) adds that no transaction reads uncommitted or intermediate versions (Adya's G1a–c). A client buffers its writes until it commits, or sends them to a server that does not reveal them to other readers until it is notified of the commit. Then a reader is never shown a write of a transaction that later aborts, nor an intermediate write.
  4. This provides no recency or monotonicity, and the paper notes it satisfies the implementation-agnostic definition rather than the lock-based behaviour of centralised implementations.∎

The corrections are now shown only when finished. But a visit's corrections are shown one after another, onto different shelves at different libraries, as the news reaches them. What of a reader who sees some of a set of corrections that were made together?

If a visit's corrections reach the libraries at different times, can a reader be shown half of them?

Chapter IV

Corrections Bound Together

Say a scholar corrects two works at once: the work X says the strait is crossed at one place, and the work Y, which cites it, must say so too. The corrections reach the libraries separately. A reader asks Library 1 for X and is shown the corrected X; later in his visit he asks Library 2 for Y, which has not yet received its correction, and is shown the old Y. He has seen half of what was made together, and the two works now contradict each other in his hands. How can a library prevent it?

By binding the corrections under one cord. Every correction in the set carries the same tally and a list of all the works in the set. A library does not show a correction to readers the moment it receives it. It holds it back, and shows it only when it knows that every library which is to receive the others in the set has received them too. Until then it answers with the older copy, which is a perfectly good answer, if an old one. Once a reader has been shown a correction from the set, the cord tells his library which tally it must show for each of the other works, or something newer: it shows the matching correction, which by then is certainly there.

Does holding back mean waiting? Not in the sense that matters. The library does not wait to answer: it answers at once from what it has, which may be the older copy. What it waits for is only to change what it shows, and it learns that from the notes the couriers carry, whenever they carry them. If the couriers are stopped, the library goes on showing the older copy for as long as they are stopped; nobody is made to wait for it.

What is this good for? Anything that must change together: a work and the list that cites it, a catalogue and its index, two sets of figures that must add up. A reader sees either the state before the bundle or the state after, never between. What it does not give is anything about which of those two states he sees, nor any promise about two readers.

Sketch — that Monotonic Atomic View is achievable with high availability
  1. This is the algorithm sketched in §5.1.2. MAV requires that once some effect of a transaction Ti is observed by Tj, all later reads of Tj observe all of Ti's effects (or later versions): if Tj reads a version written by Ti, a later read cannot return an earlier version of an item Ti wrote.
  2. Begin with the Read Committed algorithm. Replicas withhold new writes from readers until all replicas for the final writes of the transaction have received them (the writes are pending stable). Each write carries the transaction's single timestamp and the list of items written.
  3. When a client reads an item, the returned timestamp and item list give a lower bound on the versions the client must read for the other items in the list. The client attaches the current bound to each later request.
  4. A replica responds with a write matching the bound or a pending-stable write with a higher timestamp. Each replica keeps, per item, the highest pending-stable write and the set of writes not yet pending stable. Since the bound refers only to writes already pending stable, which every replica holds, a replica can always respond, so the algorithm is master-less and operations never block on replica coordination.
  5. The paper gives the full algorithm in its technical report; this is its outline.∎

A reader might also be shown different answers to the same question inside one visit, even without any bundle: ask a library the same thing twice and the answer may have changed between the asks.

Can a reader at least be sure of the same answer when he asks twice in one visit?

Chapter V

The Same Answer Twice

A reader can be sure of the same answer twice in one visit, and he can do most of it himself. A reader who has been shown a work in a visit keeps a note of the copy he was shown, on his own tablet. If he asks for the same work again, the librarian's answer is replaced by the note, so he sees the copy he saw before, unless he has changed it himself in the meantime. The tablet is wiped when he leaves. This is the promise that within one visit a reader reads the same work the same way each time. With a little more care it extends from single works to lists: asked for every work on the stars, he is given the same list however often he asks within the visit, because his note covers the whole list and not only each work in it.

Is there a promise across visits? A reader coming back should never be shown an older copy than he saw before, and his corrections should become visible in the order he made them. Both are kept alone, by a different device. Every library shows readers only what it knows every library holds: a floor of editions, which couriers' notes raise as each new edition is known to be everywhere. A floor can only rise, since an edition known to be everywhere stays everywhere, so no reader can be handed an older copy than the floor was when he last asked, wherever he asks. And since a reader's corrections enter the floor in the order they were made, they appear in that order.

What does it cost? Recency. The floor is only as new as the slowest courier, so a reader may be shown a copy far behind what some library holds. And it gives no promise that a reader sees his own corrections, since they may not yet be on the floor: it is a common floor, not a promise to this reader. The promises of this kind are kept by any library for any reader, and they are all about not going backwards; they say nothing about going forward.

If the floor can lag, can a reader at least be sure of finding the corrections he left himself?

Chapter VI

Keeping to One Library

A reader leaves a correction at Library 1 and, after the couriers have stopped, comes back the next day and asks for the work. If he goes to Library 1, the correction is on its shelves and he finds it. If he goes to Library 2, which the couriers cannot reach, the librarian must answer from what he has, and what he has is the old copy. The reader's own correction is missing. Could Library 2 do anything? It could wait until it hears of the correction, and it would wait for as long as the couriers stay stopped, which is to say it would not answer. Answering alone, it cannot know that the correction exists.

What does the reader need to do? Keep to one library. A reader who returns always to the library he left his correction at finds it, since that library has it. This is the second shelf of promises. The librarian does not have to do anything new; what is new is the reader's side of the bargain, that he comes back to the same door, or that he keeps his own copy of his corrections and shows them to any library that does not have them, which comes to the same thing, since he is then acting as a library himself.

And what is promised? That a reader finds his own corrections. Put together with the earlier promises of not going backwards and of seeing his corrections in the order he made them, it is the guarantee the paper calls PRAM: a visit's reads and writes appear in the order the reader did them, as though his session were one line of acts. The price is the same as before and one more: if the library he keeps to is lost, his promises are lost with it.

Proof — that Read Your Writes is not achievable with high availability, and is with sticky availability
  1. This is §5.1.3. Read your writes requires that whenever a client reads an item after updating it, the read returns the updated value or one that overwrote it. Consider a client that executes T1 : wx(1) and then T2 : rx(a).
  2. Suppose the client executes T1 against a server partitioned from all others. For transactional availability the server must allow T1 to commit.
  3. If the network topology then changes so that the client can execute T2 only on a different replica, partitioned from the one that executed T1, that replica has no way to learn of wx(1). By transactional availability it must respond, so it either stalls indefinitely, violating availability, or returns a value other than 1 or one that overwrote it, violating read your writes.
  4. If the client remains sticky with the server that executed T1, that server holds the write and the scenario is excluded. So read your writes, and with it PRAM (monotonic reads, monotonic writes and read your writes) and causal consistency, require stickiness and are provided by default in a sticky system.∎

One more thing remains on this shelf, and it concerns not the reader's own corrections but those of others.

Can a reader be shown a reply, whose letter has not yet reached his library?

Chapter VII

Letters and Replies

A scholar at Library 1 writes a letter on some question and it is entered as a work. A scholar at Library 2 reads the letter and writes a reply, which is also entered. The reply travels quickly to the reader's library and the letter slowly. If the reader's librarian shows the reply as soon as it arrives, the reader reads an answer to a question he has not seen asked. The promise wanted is that no reply is shown before the letter it answers.

How is it kept? By holding the reply back, as the corrections were held in the cord, until every work it depends on is visible: the letter and whatever the letter itself answered. A reply carries a note of what its writer had seen when he wrote it, and a library shows the reply only when each of those is already on the floor. As before the librarian answers all the while, with what he may show; he only does not show the reply yet. This promise needs no stickiness: it is one of those kept for any reader at any library, since it is only about what is on the floor.

What of the reader's own letters and replies, put with all the rest? Together, the promises of the second shelf and this one make a single guarantee, the strongest the librarian can give alone to a reader who keeps to one library: he never goes backwards, he finds his own corrections, he sees them in order, and he never sees a reply before the letter. It is called causal consistency. It is stronger than anything on the first shelf, which is why it needs the reader to keep to one library, and it is the last thing a librarian can promise alone.

Beyond that line, what can no librarian promise alone, and why?

Chapter VIII

The Last Copy

The libraries lend. A reader may borrow a copy for a term, and while it is out the library does not have it*. Say a work has one copy left on the coast, and it is on the shelf at Library 1 as far as Library 2 can tell; the couriers are stopped. A reader at Library 1 borrows it. A reader at Library 2, reading the same note, borrows it too. Each librarian did what the promises allow: answered from the shelves, and each was right as far as he could see. Two readers now hold the one copy.

Could Library 2 have refused? Only if it knew that Library 1 had lent it, and it could not know that without a courier. The two lendings are alike: each reads the count of copies, finds one, and writes a count of none. Neither reading sees the other's writing, so neither lending is prevented. Nothing a librarian can do from his own shelves prevents it, and any rule he tries, such as waiting to hear from the other library, is a rule that needs a courier and so stops working the day the couriers do. The promise two readers never borrow the last copy cannot be kept alone.

Is it only about one copy of one work? No: it spreads to rules that cover several works. Say two works are kept as reference copies, and at least one of them must always stay on the shelf. A reader at Library 1 looks at the shelf, sees the second work there, and borrows the first. A reader at Library 2, at the same moment, sees the first work there and borrows the second. Each kept the rule by what he could see, and the rule is broken. This is the same failure with two works instead of one, and it cannot be prevented alone for the same reason.

What can be done? Some of it can be mended in the world outside the library: the two readers meet on the road, and one gives way. The paper calls this moving the clash out into the real world, where it is settled by someone able to settle it. And some of it can be dodged by changing the rule. A new work needs a shelf-mark; if the mark must be unique it can be made at any library from the library's number and a count of its own, and no two will ever clash; if it must be the next in sequence over the whole coast, two libraries will each give out the same next number, and that cannot be prevented alone. A count of copies that must not fall below zero can be kept from falling below zero by having the library make another copy whenever a lending would take it below zero, so that the count is never found below none; but the coast may end with more copies than it would have had, if every library had waited.

Proof — that Lost Update and Write Skew cannot be prevented with high availability
  1. This is §5.2.2. Lost Update: T1 reads an item, T2 updates the same item, and T1 writes based on its original read, losing T2's update. Take T1 : rx(100) wx(100 + 20 = 120) and T2 : rx(100) wx(100 + 30 = 130), submitted on opposite sides of a network partition.
  2. By transactional availability both must be allowed to commit. Whichever of 120 and 130 a replica then chooses, the state could not have arisen from a serial execution of T1 and T2, since a serial execution gives 150.
  3. To prevent it, one of the transactions must not commit. Each side's server could try to detect that another write occurred, but this requires knowing the version of the latest write to x, which reduces to a requirement for linearizability, provably at odds with high availability by Gilbert and Lynch's proof of the CAP theorem.
  4. Write Skew generalises Lost Update to several items: T1 : ry(0) wx(1) and T2 : rx(0) wy(1), with a constraint that only one of x and y may be 1. Run on opposite sides of a partition, both commit and the constraint is violated, though each would have preserved it in isolation; any prevention needs the same knowledge of the other side and is likewise unavailable.
  5. Hence Snapshot Isolation, Cursor Stability and consistent read (which require preventing Lost Update), and Repeatable Read and one-copy serializability (which require preventing both) are not achievable with high availability.∎

So the second thing no librarian can do alone is to stop two things happening that depend on each other's absence. One more promise remains, and it is the one readers ask for first.

Can a librarian at least promise that the copy he hands over is the latest?

Chapter IX

The Latest Edition

A librarian cannot promise that the copy he hands over is the latest, nor any looser form of that. The promise this is the latest edition, or no more than a day behind, or no more than some bound behind, would need the librarian to know that nothing newer has been written anywhere since. If the couriers are stopped for longer than the bound, a newer edition may have been written at another library and be on the road; the librarian must still answer, from what he has, which is behind by more than the bound. However long a bound is named, a longer stoppage breaks it. The promise cannot be kept alone.

Proof — that no recency guarantee, including linearizability, is achievable with high availability
  1. This is §5.2.1. A recency guarantee bounds how out of date a read may be: linearizability (reads return the last completed write), safe and regular register semantics, and any bound such as "no more than five seconds out of date".
  2. Let a write complete at a replica on one side of a partition and let a client then read the item at a replica on the other side. The partition may last indefinitely, with no message crossing.
  3. By availability the reading replica must respond, and it can respond only from information it received before the partition began, which does not include the write. So the response is out of date by at least the time the partition has lasted.
  4. Since the partition can last longer than any fixed bound, no recency bound is enforceable. This is the argument of Gilbert and Lynch for the CAP theorem, applied to every recency guarantee.∎

What does the librarian gain for giving all this up? Time, and not having to stop. The paper measured it: in its trials a store that routes every operation through a single master across distant sites took ten to a hundred times as long per operation as one that answers from any replica. The promises on the first two shelves cost nothing in waiting. The promises on the third shelf cost as much waiting as the slowest road. The paper's survey of eighteen databases found that only three keep the strong promises by default, so readers already live with modest ones, which suggests that they can do a good deal with them.

What does the paper not claim about these promises?

Chapter X

What It Does Not Say

Is "answering alone" a loose standard? It is a strict one, and the paper says so. A library that needs a majority of the coast to be open does not answer alone; neither does one that answers correctly only with high probability. The standard is that of the CAP theorem, a response from every open library whatever the stoppage, because that is the one under which the impossibilities can be proved and under which delay is lowest. A weaker standard would allow more.

Does it say how to build the libraries efficiently? No. The constructions are proofs that the promises can be kept alone, not designs for doing it well: the common floor, for one, keeps every old edition and may be slow to rise. The paper says its algorithms are proofs of feasibility, not optimal or even efficient ones. It does show a more efficient way of keeping bundles, and it measured a working version.

Does keeping several promises at once cost more? The paper says a combination is as available as the least available of its parts: add one promise that needs the reader to keep to one library and the combination needs it too. Many promises can be kept together; the paper counts a hundred and forty-four combinations of the available ones and does not claim to have listed every promise there could be.

Does it say the modest promises are enough? No. It says they exist, and that the third shelf, the last copy and the latest edition, is not among them; whether a given coast can live without those depends on the coast. Some of what falls on the third shelf can be changed to fit the first, with unique shelf-marks in place of sequential ones, or mended in the world outside. And some cannot, which is why the paper's advice to a designer is to expect a few operations that must be kept apart from the rest and given the slower, stronger treatment, and to use it sparingly.

The ideas, plainly

Bailis and his co-authors ask which of the guarantees that databases and distributed systems offer can be kept by a replica that must always answer, even when it is cut off from every other replica for as long as the cut lasts. They call such systems highly available, and with transactions, highly available transactions. They go through the known guarantees and sort them into those that can be kept, those that can be kept if each client keeps to one replica, and those that cannot.

The first group includes read committed, which stops a client reading writes that are not finished, monotonic atomic view, which makes the writes of one transaction visible all together or not at all, repeatable reads within a transaction, and monotonic reads and writes and writes-follow-reads. The second adds read-your-writes, PRAM and causal consistency. Each is built by holding writes back from readers until it is safe to show them, never by making a reader wait.

The third group is made of the guarantees that need a replica to know about concurrent operations elsewhere: preventing lost update and write skew, which rules out snapshot isolation, repeatable read in its other senses and serializability, and any bound on how out of date a read may be. These are fundamental, not weaknesses of particular systems. The paper concludes that many applications can live with the first two groups, that some operations will need the third and should use it sparingly, and that high availability buys the lowest delay.
