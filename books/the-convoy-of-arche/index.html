The Convoy of Arche

Being an account of how the trading houses agreed whether their merchantmen should sail, without trusting the weather

Three trading houses with merchant ships anchored at their slipways, needing to sail together as a convoy across the open sea. While winter gales batter the cliff road, runners face unbounded delays and no consensus can be forced. But when the storm breaks and transit times fall within a bound, a rotating coordinator and the rule of majority locks decide whether the fleet sails, without risking a divided one.

Chapter I

The Sea Road and the Convoy

At the end of autumn, the trading houses of Arche prepare their merchant ships for the crossing to Alexandria, Syracuse, and Rhodes. At the Dolphin on the western shelf, the Vine on the northern terraces, and the Anchor in the eastern bay, broad-beamed merchantmen lie in the slipways, loaded with the season's dried fruit, wine-amphorae, and silver ingots.

To cross the open Mediterranean alone is folly. A single merchantman running into sudden squalls or sighted by Cilician pirate galleys is taken or sunk. The ships must sail as a single, united convoy, or remain moored securely in their coves.

Three coves on three sides of a mountain cannot put to sea at one instant, and the convoy does not ask them to. It forms at the Needle, a stack of rock half a day's sail off the southern cape, in whose lee ships can lie for a few days. Each house, once it knows the fleet is to sail, sends its ships out on the first dawn land-breeze. They gather under the Needle and wait for one another for three days, which is as long as the lee can hold ships and their water can last. Then the convoy leaves with whoever has come. It cannot wait for "the last ship", because a house may have been crushed by rockfall and have no ships to send, and nobody at the Needle can tell that house from a slow one. The decision the houses must share is therefore not when but whether, and it does not matter that one house learns it a day after another, so long as each learns it inside the three days.

What would matter is two houses deciding different things. Ships sent to the Needle for a fleet whose house has decided to hold wait out their three days in open water the pirate galleys watch. Then they must cross short-handed, too few to beat off an attack, or beat home against a turning season with the cargo spoiling in the holds. If half the ships slip their cables while the other half stay fast, the convoy is broken, and the isolated vessels are lost.

Where the allegory leaks

The three days at the Needle quietly assume that every house still standing hears the decision within three days of the first house to decide. Consensus promises only that each will hear it eventually. After the storm has broken for good, the commit slips reach every house within one runner's transit, so the assumption holds. A decision reached in a lull that then closes up again could leave one house's ships sailing a week late to an empty rock. The paper's consensus settles a value. Acting on it together by a deadline is a stronger problem, and this book does not solve it.

Each house watches its own local cove, examines its hull caulking, and reads its oil-glass. At dawn of the decision season, each house forms an initial judgment:

The two choices

0 · Hold at anchor. The roadstead is rough, the glass is falling, or the rigging is unready. The ships remain tied to the stone bollards.

1 · Sail. The roadstead is calm, the glass is steady, and the crews are aboard. Each house's ships leave on the first land-breeze after it learns the decision, and the fleet forms under the Needle.

Between these choices, the houses must reach an agreement that satisfies three exact demands, established by the archons of the island:

The three demands of consensus (1) Agreement. No two honest houses ever decide differently. It cannot happen that the Dolphin puts to sea while the Vine remains at anchor.
(2) Validity. If every house initially judges that the fleet should sail (all 1), the convoy must sail. If every house initially judges that it must hold (all 0), the convoy must hold.
(3) Termination. Every house that does not suffer catastrophe must eventually reach a final, irrevocable decision.

The houses cannot shout to each other across Mount Phyle. They communicate solely through runners along the narrow ring road cut into the cliffs above the surf. And in the decision season, the weather on the cliff road makes mockery of all simple plans.

Chapter II

The Storm and the Shadow of the Mercenaries

When the winter gales strike Arche, the sea smashes against the base of the cliffs. Spray washes over the ring road, and loose shale tumbles down the slopes of Mount Phyle. A runner dispatched between houses cannot maintain a steady pace. He may be forced to shelter under a rocky ledge for six hours while breakers clear the road; a sudden rockslide may block a bend, forcing him to scramble over rubble on his hands and knees.

No runner is lost — the island's messengers are tough and stubborn, and every message eventually arrives. But how long a runner will take is completely unpredictable. A journey that takes a runner half a day in clear weather may take two days, or five, in a howling gale.

Furthermore, a house itself may suffer a localized cliff collapse that crushes its gates, silencing it permanently. Up to t houses may fail in this manner. On Arche, with three houses in total (N = 3), the network must endure the silencing of at most one house (t = 1).

The houses are familiar with the famous theorem proved by the mercenaries on the lower slopes of Mount Phyle — the impossibility demonstrated by Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson. Where message delays are unbounded and a participant may die without warning, no protocol can guarantee both safety and termination. If the houses set a fixed sandglass deadline and decide upon its expiry, they cannot distinguish a dead neighbor from a runner delayed by spray. A premature decision risks splitting the fleet; perpetual waiting risks rotting the cargo at the quays.

If the storm were perpetual, consensus would be unattainable. But storms in the Aegean, however violent, do not rage forever.

Chapter III

The Breaking of the Storm

The insight that rescues the fleet was formulated by three scribes: Dworkia of the Dolphin, Lynchaia of the Vine, and Stockmeros of the Anchor. They recognized that Arche does not inhabit a world of permanent chaos, nor a world of permanent clockwork. It inhabits partial synchrony.

The model rests on a physical fact of the island's climate:

Partial synchrony (Model 2 of DLS 1988) There exists an unknown moment in time, GST (the Global Stabilization Time, which the scribes call the breaking of the storm), such that:
(1) Before GST: Message delivery along the ring road is arbitrarily and unpredictably delayed. Communication is purely asynchronous.
(2) After GST: The storm clears, the cliff path dries, and every runner dispatched between any two houses arrives within a known upper bound of time, Δ (measured by three turns of a standard customs sandglass).
(3) Unannounced arrival: The houses do not know when GST has arrived. A brief lull in the gale looks identical to the final clearing of the sky.

This formulation captures reality with dry precision. The algorithm cannot wait for an angel to blow a trumpet announcing GST. It must run continuously through the storm, never violating safety (never sending part of the fleet to sea while others stay behind), and then, when the storm finally breaks, swiftly reach a common decision.

Nor does the clearing of the sky open another road. A beacon on Mount Phyle would be seen from every cove once the cloud lifts, but the summit belongs to the siege and no one from the houses goes up. The swell goes on breaking over the reefs between the coves for days after the wind has dropped, so no skiff coasts from one house to the next. The ring road is still the only way word travels. What the calm changes is how long it takes.

Chapter IV

The Rotating Coordinator and the Quorum

To organize the decision without a common clock, the scribes divided time into successive rounds: r = 1, 2, 3, …

Each round is governed by a designated coordinator chosen in unbroken rotation:
— Round 1 is directed by Dworkia at the Dolphin.
— Round 2 is directed by Lynchaia at the Vine.
— Round 3 is directed by Stockmeros at the Anchor.
— Round 4 returns to Dworkia at the Dolphin, and the cycle repeats (r mod 3).

Each house maintains a local sandglass timer of fixed duration T = 5Δ. When a house's sand runs out for round r, it advances its ledger to round r + 1, regardless of whether round r reached a conclusion.

Three sandglasses on three sides of a mountain do not stay together. A house cut off for a week of storm turns its glass as often as the others, but it started at a different moment. It may come out of the storm in round 7 while its neighbours are in round 9, and since all three glasses run at the same rate, it would stay two rounds behind for ever. So the scribes added the rule of catching up. At the start of every round a house sends its status slip to all the houses, not only to the coordinator. A house that receives any slip bearing a round higher than its own drops its round at once, advances its ledger to the higher number, and turns its glass afresh. Slips bearing a lower round are set aside, except a commit slip, which is honoured whenever it comes.

Before the storm breaks, the rule of catching up changes nothing that matters. After it, the house furthest ahead drags the others level within one runner's transit, so every round begins at the three houses within Δ of one another. That is why T is 5Δ and not 4Δ: four transits for the round's business, and one more for the houses that started late.

The second pillar of the arrangement is the majority quorum. On Arche, there are three houses (N = 3), and at most one may be crushed by rockfall (t = 1). Therefore, no coordinator can ever wait to hear from all three houses, for the third house may be buried under stone. Every action must proceed upon hearing from a majority of N − t = 2 houses.

The Quorum Intersection

Any two majorities of size 2 out of 3 houses must share at least one house:

|Q1| = 2, |Q2| = 2, N = 3 ⟹ |Q1 ∩ Q2| ≥ 1

That single overlapping house is the witness that prevents the island from ever contradicting itself across rounds.

Chapter V

The Four Phases of a Round

Each house i keeps three entries inked on its decision tablet:
— vi: its current proposed value (0 or 1), initially its own local weather judgment.
— lock_roundi: the round number in which it last locked its value (initially 0).
— decidedi: whether it has committed to a final decision.

Every round r proceeds through four strictly ordered phases:

The Four Phases of Round r
  1. Phase 1 · Status. On entering round r, each house i dispatches runners to every house carrying a slip
    STATUS(vi, lock_roundi, r).
    The coordinator of round r uses it. The other houses use only its round number, to catch up.
  2. Phase 2 · Proposal. The coordinator waits until she receives status slips from a majority of houses (2 houses). She inspects the reported lock_round numbers. She chooses the value v associated with the highest reported lock_round. (If all report 0, she proposes her own initial preference). She dispatches to all houses:
    PROPOSE(v, r).
  3. Phase 3 · Lock & Acknowledgment. When house i receives PROPOSE(v, r) before its round timer expires: if r ≥ lock_roundi, the house adopts v, sets its lock_roundi ← r, and sends back to the coordinator:
    ACK(r).
  4. Phase 4 · Commit. If the coordinator receives ACK(r) from a majority of houses (2 houses) before her timer expires, she decides v, inscribes it in stone as the final sailing order, and dispatches to all houses:
    COMMIT(v, r). Any house receiving a COMMIT slip immediately decides v.

If a house's local sandglass runs out before a phase completes, the house abandons that round and advances to round r + 1. It keeps its lock_round and locked value intact. A house that has decided goes on taking part in the rounds, so that a house whose commit slip went astray is not left alone to finish.

Chapter VI

Safety in the Wildest Gale

The first necessity of the fleet is that it must never divide. Even if the gale rages for three weeks, and runners shelter for days under the rocks, and timers expire out of order, it must be impossible for the Dolphin to decide to sail while the Vine later decides to hold.

This safety guarantee does not depend on the weather. It holds during the height of the storm, under unbounded delay, by virtue of the majority lock.

Theorem 1 · Unconditional Agreement (DLS 1988)

No two houses ever decide different values, regardless of runner delays or the time of GST.

Proof — that no two rounds can commit different values
  1. Suppose some round r is the earliest round in which a coordinator decides a value, and let that value be v.
  2. To decide v at round r, the coordinator received ACK(r) from a majority of houses Q1. By Phase 3, every house in Q1 set its lock_round to r and locked value v.
  3. Now consider any subsequent round r' > r. We show by induction on r' that the coordinator of round r' can propose no value other than v.
  4. To form a proposal in Phase 2, the coordinator of r' must receive status reports from a majority Q2.
  5. Because N = 3 and each quorum contains 2 houses, |Q1 ∩ Q2| ≥ 1. Let w be a house in the intersection.
  6. Because w ∈ Q1, w locked v at round r. Thus, when w reports its status to round r', its reported lock_roundw is at least r.
  7. By the induction hypothesis, no coordinator in any intermediate round between r and r' could have proposed or locked any value other than v. Therefore, any lock held by any house at or above round r must be on value v.
  8. The rule of Phase 2 compels the coordinator of r' to choose the value with the highest reported lock_round. Since w reports a lock at round r or higher, the highest reported lock cannot be 0, and must belong to some round ≥ r.
  9. Hence the coordinator of r' is forced to choose value v. No conflicting value can ever be proposed, acknowledged, or committed in any round.∎
Chapter VII

Liveness When the Calm Arrives

Safety prevents a split fleet, but safety alone is not enough: the ships cannot remain in their slips until the timbers rot. The protocol must guarantee that once the weather relents, a decision is reached.

Theorem 2 · Guaranteed Termination after GST (DLS 1988)

Once the storm breaks (after GST), every house that has not suffered catastrophe reaches a final decision within a bounded number of rounds.

The proof relies on the synchrony that sets in once the sky clears:

Proof — that consensus is reached after GST
  1. After GST, every runner between any two functioning houses completes his transit within time Δ.
  2. Because coordinators rotate cyclically among the houses, there must eventually begin a round r, first entered by some house at a time t₀ after GST, whose designated coordinator c(r) is a non-faulty house.
  3. The first house to enter r sends its status slip to every house. By the rule of catching up, every non-faulty house is in round r by t₀ + Δ. No house can leave r before t₀ + 5Δ, when the earliest glass runs out, and slips from the storm carry no round above r, so none can pull a house past it.
  4. Each non-faulty house sends its status slip on entering r, so the coordinator holds status from every functioning house (at least 2, a majority) by t₀ + 2Δ. She selects the value of the highest reported lock and dispatches PROPOSE(v, r) to all houses.
  5. These proposal slips arrive at all houses by t₀ + 3Δ, while every house is still in round r. Because r is at least as high as any lock yet taken, each functioning house locks v and dispatches ACK(r) to the coordinator.
  6. The acknowledgments reach the coordinator by t₀ + 4Δ, still within the round. She decides v and dispatches COMMIT(v, r) to all houses.
  7. The commit slips arrive by t₀ + 5Δ. A commit is honoured whatever round the receiver is in, so every non-faulty house decides v.∎

Notice how neatly the boundary is navigated: during the storm, rounds may abort harmlessly because sandglasses expire before runners arrive; but the moment the calm arrives, the very next honest round drives the decision home.

Chapter VIII

The Bounds of Faults and Liars

It remains to explain why three houses are sufficient for Arche, and what would happen if the houses did not merely crash, but lied.

In Arche's convoy problem, houses may be crushed by rockfalls (crash failures), but runners do not forge signatures or alter numbers. Under crash failures in partial synchrony, DLS proved that consensus requires strictly more than double the number of failures:

N ≥ 2t + 1

With N = 3 houses, Arche can endure t = 1 failure. If two houses could fail simultaneously (t = 2), three houses would be helpless: a solitary surviving house cannot distinguish two silent neighbors from two runners delayed by the gale.

What if the houses could act maliciously — forging status reports or proposing different values to different neighbors, as the bandits did atop Mount Phyle?

DLS established the exact boundaries for Byzantine failures in partial synchrony:

Failure TypeAuthenticationResilience BoundTolerated on Arche (N = 3)
Crash (fail-stop)Plain inkN ≥ 2t + 1t = 1
Byzantine (malicious)Wax seals (signed)N ≥ 3t + 1t = 0 (needs N ≥ 4)
Byzantine (malicious)Plain ink (unsigned)N ≥ 3t + 1t = 0 (needs N ≥ 4)

Against rockslides, three houses suffice. Against treachery under partial synchrony, the houses would need a fourth house on the southern cape — and seals would not spare them it.

That is a sharp contrast with the summit, where the bandits' seal rings dissolved the bound altogether. The difference is the storm. On the summit a jar measures every flight, so a man who hears nothing knows nothing was sent. On the ring road before GST, a house can never wait for every other house, since one may be buried. It can wait only for all but t, and t of the ones it does hear from may be liars. A seal stops a liar from forging another house's word. It does not stop him answering promptly while an honest house's runner is still under a ledge, so the honest must outnumber the liars in every set of replies a house can be made to act on. That needs N ≥ 3t + 1, sealed or not.

The ideas, plainly

The FLP impossibility theorem demonstrated that consensus is impossible in an asynchronous network if even one crash failure can occur. Yet real-world networks are not adversarial in perpetuity: periods of severe packet delay or network partition are eventually followed by relative calm.

Dwork, Lynch, and Stockmeyer introduced partial synchrony: systems where message delays are unbounded before an unknown Global Stabilization Time (GST), but strictly bounded by Δ thereafter. They showed that consensus is entirely achievable in such systems, requiring only a majority of functioning nodes (N ≥ 2t + 1 for crash failures).

The core mechanism is a round-based rotating coordinator protocol with quorum locks. In each round, the coordinator proposes a value chosen from the highest-round lock reported by a majority quorum. If a majority acknowledges and locks the proposal, the coordinator commits. Because any two majority quorums must intersect, any future coordinator is guaranteed to see the locked value, preserving safety unconditionally even if the network is chaotic. Once GST arrives, messages arrive within Δ, allowing the protocol to terminate cleanly.

This foundational architecture directly inspired Lamport's Paxos and the consensus engines of modern distributed databases.
