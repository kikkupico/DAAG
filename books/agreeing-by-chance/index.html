Agreeing by Chance

If no plan can guarantee agreement when a machine may stop without warning, can chance succeed where planning cannot? What if a machine that is stuck simply tosses a coin?

Four mercenaries at the foot of the hill must go in at one gate, and any one of them may fall silent while no bird has a longest flight. No arrangement guarantees that any of them ever goes. Change one thing: let a man who cannot settle toss a coin, alone in his own tent. Then the four never go to different gates, and they go in the end with probability one, though on no night anyone can name.

Chapter I

The Same Four Tents

Bandits hold the crown of Mount Phyle, where the loot is. At the foot, at the four quarters, four mercenaries keep four small tents, one man to a tent, hired separately and paid separately, under nobody's command. Their shields name the tents*: Benorios at the bull, Menon at the boar, Eudoros at the trident and Hagnon at the stag. Between any two tents stand two wooded spurs, so no man can see another. They light no fires and sound no horns, and no man leaves his quarter to carry word, since leaving it is the very thing that must be agreed.

They must go in at one gate, the narrow slot on the north side of the crown or the timber-framed gap on the south, and all four at the same one. Each formed his own opinion at dusk from what he could see of his quarter: his sighting, north or south. Going is a definite act. A man goes by striking his tent and taking the path round the slope to his gate, and once he has done it his quarter is open behind him and he will not be back before dawn. What he thinks before that does not count; then he does one thing, and it counts for good.

Word passes by raven, on the same terms as before. A man writes on a strip, rolls it into a bronze tube and ties it to a bird belonging to the tent he is writing to. The birds always arrive, and what they carry is what was written. But there is no longest flight: a bird may spend an hour or the night among the hill's crows before it goes on, and birds sent an hour apart arrive in either order. And any one of the four may be killed. It has not happened, and no one is killed in this book; what matters is that it may. A dead man's tent falls silent and stays silent, and a silent tent cannot be told from a live one whose bird is in a pine two spurs away.

What follows from that is known. Nobody lies, nothing goes wrong, and still there is no arrangement the four could make which guarantees that any of them ever goes; the birds can always be ordered so that the night stays open*. The three demands are these, put in the form this book's paper uses:

The three demands First. No two of them go to different gates.
Second. If all four sighted the same gate, that is the gate they go to.
Third. Every man who is not killed eventually goes.

Two of them ask more than the impossibility needs. For it, it is enough that both gates stay possible and that somebody go*. Here the second says exactly how the gates stay possible: when the sightings all agree, the four follow them, which is what rules out agreeing on the north gate in advance. And the third asks going of every man left alive. Even the weaker demands cannot be guaranteed, so the stronger ones cannot either.

That earlier proof rested on one assumption, stated in its second chapter and easy to pass over: that the four may not draw lots. Each acts only on what has reached him, so that given the same sighting and the same birds in the same order, he does the same thing every time. This book is about what happens when that one assumption is dropped, and nothing else is changed.

Chapter II

A Coin in Each Tent

Every man at the foot was paid in Attic silver, and every Attic coin has the goddess's head on one face and her owl on the other. Before the tents were pitched it was settled, as part of the arrangement all four knew, that the owl stands for the north slot and the goddess for the south gap. A man who, by the rules below, cannot settle on a gate spins a coin on the lid of his chest and takes the gate it shows.

Each man tosses his own coin, alone in his tent. They cannot toss one coin together, or draw one lot from one bag: that would need them in one place, and nothing on the hill brings them there. Nor can one man toss for all and send the result round, since a bird from him might be the one still in the pines, or he might be the one who is killed. So there are four coins and four separate tosses, and nobody learns another's toss except by bird, if at all.

Three things are asked of a coin, and a coin of silver spun on a wooden lid gives them.

What a toss is taken to be (1) Fair. Owl and goddess come up equally often.
(2) Fresh. Each toss owes nothing to any toss before it, in this tent or any other.
(3) Unforeseen. Before it is made, no one knows how it will fall. After it is made, anyone may know it.

The third clause matters because of how the arguments of this book treat the birds. Without coins, the birds are taken in the worst order the argument can find: whatever the four arrange, the order of arrivals can be chosen against them*. The same is done here, and done harder. Think of the order in which birds come down as arranged by someone who can see into every tent, read every strip and see every toss already made, and who arranges the arrivals to keep the four apart for as long as possible. The one thing this arranger cannot do is see a toss before it is made. It is only the worst case, and no such person exists; but anything that holds against him holds on every night.

Chapter III

Rounds of Birds

Benorios's arrangement goes in numbered rounds, and every bird carries the number of its round, so that a man can tell a late bird of the second round from an early one of the third. In each round each man sends two birds to each of the others, and counts his own two as though they had come to him.

The first is his report: the gate he holds to at the start of the round, beginning with his sighting. The second says one of two things: sure of a gate, or unsure. A man writes sure of north only when the reports that reached him in that round show more than half of all four men holding north.

Every waiting in the arrangement is a waiting for three birds, not four. With four men and at most one silent, three always come; a man who waited for four would wait for ever whenever one of them had been killed, and could not know it.

One round, r, at one tent (1) Send the report of round r, the gate held now, to all.
(2) Wait for three reports of round r. If more than two of them, that is all three, name the same gate, send sure of that gate to all; otherwise send unsure.
(3) Wait for three second birds of round r. Then:
  (a) if at least one says sure of a gate, hold that gate from now on;
  (b) if two or more say sure of it, be settled on it;
  (c) if none says sure, toss the coin and hold the gate it shows.
(4) Go on to round r + 1.
The same, for N men of whom t may fall silent Wait each time for N − t birds. Send sure of v when more than N/2 of the reports received name v. Hold v on one sure of v; settle on v on more than t of them; toss otherwise. The arrangement needs N > 2t. On the hill, N = 4 and t = 1.

A settled man still has to go, and there is a catch in it. A man who strikes his tent falls as silent as a dead one, and the others are waiting for three birds each round; if two men went and a third were killed, the last would wait for ever. So a settled man does one thing more before he goes.

The rule of going A man who becomes settled on a gate in round r at once sends both his birds of round r + 1 to all: the report, that gate, and the second bird, sure of that gate. Then he strikes his tent and goes to it.

This rule is not in Ben-Or's paper. There a process that has decided simply goes on taking part, and nothing is said of stopping. On the hill deciding is going, and going is silence, so the rule is needed; Chapter IV shows that the two birds it sends early are exactly the two the man would have sent had he stayed.

Chapter IV

Never Two Gates

The first two demands hold on every night, whatever the birds and whatever the coins. The whole of it rests on one small fact about majorities.

One sure gate a round

In any round, every sure bird names the same gate.

Each man sends one report a round, so the round has at most four reports. A man writes sure of north only after seeing three reports of north, and sure of south only after seeing three of south. Three and three out of four must share a man, and he cannot have reported both.

Never two gates

No two men go to different gates. If one man is settled on a gate in round r, every man who finishes round r holds that gate, and every man who is not killed is settled on it in round r + 1.

Proof — that once one man is settled, all end at his gate
  1. Say Benorios is settled on north in round r. Then two or more of the three second birds that reached him said sure of north, so at least two such birds were sent in round r.
  2. Any other man who finishes round r read three of the four second birds of that round, so he missed at most one. He therefore read at least one sure of north. By one sure gate a round he read no sure of south, so he holds north: by rule (a) if he read one, and he may also be settled on north by rule (b). He does not toss.
  3. So every report of round r + 1 names north. Every man who reaches step (2) of that round sees three reports of north and sends sure of north; every man who reaches step (3) reads three sure of north and is settled on north.
  4. The birds a settled man sends early under the rule of going are a report of north and sure of north. By step 3 these are exactly the birds he would have sent in round r + 1 had he stayed, whichever three reports he had read. So the rule changes nothing the others see, and each of them still gets three birds at every step, since at most one man is dead and the settled ones have sent theirs.
  5. No man is settled on south in round r or any later round, since no sure of south is ever sent from round r on. So no two men go to different gates.∎
The sightings respected

If all four sighted the same gate, every man who is not killed is settled on it in the first round.

Proof — that four matching sightings settle the first round
  1. Every report of round 1 names that gate, so every man reads three reports of it and sends sure of it.
  2. Every man then reads three sure birds for it, more than one, and is settled on it. No coin is tossed.∎

Neither result has anything to do with chance. A coin is tossed only by a man who read no sure bird at all, and one sure gate a round means that while anyone at all is settled, no one is tossing against him. The coins can delay the four. They can never divide them.

Chapter V

The Toss

The coin does its work in the rounds where nobody is sure. Here is one way a night can go, with the stag silent from the start, so that every waiting is for the three who remain. The sightings were north at the bull, south at the boar and north at the trident.

Two things are worth seeing in it. First, the tosses did not have to agree with anybody's sighting. The trident sighted north and tossed north, the bull sighted north and tossed south; that is allowed, because the second demand speaks only of nights when all four sightings agree. Second, round 2 settled nothing even though two of the three held south, because sure needs more than half of all four men, not more than half of those heard from. That is what keeps one sure gate a round true when a slow bird may be carrying the fourth report.

Nor is there anything to be read into which gate the coins chose. The coins pick a gate the way the birds' order would otherwise have picked it: by nothing anyone can know in advance. The night could as well have ended south.

Chapter VI

With Probability One

The example ended in three rounds. It could have taken thirty, and there is a course of birds and coins, one course among very many, in which it never ends at all: the tosses keep coming up split, round after round, for ever. What can be promised is that such courses, taken together, have no chance whatever of happening.

Going with probability one

With at most one of the four silent, and however the birds are ordered, the chance that every man who is not killed eventually goes is one.

The idea is that the coins keep giving the four a way out, and the birds cannot shut it for good. In any round at most one gate can be the subject of sure birds. If every man who tosses in that round comes up with that gate, or, when there is none, all with the same gate, then every man starts the next round holding one gate, and the next round settles everyone, as the proof of Chapter IV showed. The arranger can make that harder, round by round, but a way out that keeps coming back with some chance is taken in the end with chance one.

Proof — a sketch, that the four go with probability one
  1. Fix a round r in which no one is yet settled. Let g be the gate named by sure birds in round r, if any are sent, and let g be north otherwise.
  2. Every man who finishes round r either reads a sure of g and holds g, or tosses. Each toss is fair and fresh, and the arranger cannot see it before it is made, so each comes up g with chance one half, whatever has gone before.
  3. At most four men toss in round r, so with chance at least 1/16 (the sketch's figure, not a proven bound) every one of them comes up g. Then every report of round r + 1 names g, and as in the proof of never two gates, every man who is not killed is settled on g in round r + 1.
  4. The rounds' tosses are fresh, so the chance that this fails in each of k rounds in a row is at most (15/16)k, which shrinks towards nothing as k grows. The chance that it fails for ever is therefore nothing.∎

That is a sketch, and it should be said plainly where it fails. Step 1 treats the gate g as fixed before the round's tosses. But a slow man's report for round r comes from how his round r − 1 ended, and the arranger chooses which second birds he reads there, and may choose after seeing some tosses of round r. So the gate named sure in round r can be picked against the early tosses, and the one-in-sixteen of step 3 is not a bound anyone has proved. Ben-Or's paper states its theorems without any proof. A proof that closes every such gap was published only in 2012, by Aguilera and Toueg; it comes to the same conclusion by a longer road.

Now say out loud what is not claimed. No night can be named by which the four will have gone. There is no round after which going is certain; there is only a chance, growing towards one, that it has happened by then. Ben-Or's paper gives no figure for how fast that chance grows, and this book gives none. A man who asks will we have gone by the fortieth round? can only be told almost certainly. And the courses that never end still exist. They have been given chance nothing, not abolished.

Chapter VII

What the Coin Changed

Without coins, the endless night is built in three steps*: there is a way for the sightings to fall that leaves the night two-gated; from any two-gated standing, any arrival can be deferred and taken without closing the night; and so an arranger who defers well can keep it open for ever. Every step leans on the four being unable to draw lots. Given the same birds in the same order, each man does the same thing, so the arranger knows, before choosing an arrival, what it will lead to.

With coins the arranger no longer knows. At a toss the night forks, one way for the owl and one for the goddess, and the fork is taken by the coin, not by him. He can still be as clever as before about the birds. What he cannot do is steer both forks at once. The two-gated standings are still there, and so are the courses that stay two-gated for ever: fix every toss in advance and the argument without coins runs unchanged*. What has changed is that an endless course now needs the coins to cooperate, round after round, and the coins do not.

So the result without coins stands, and nothing here contradicts it. No arrangement guarantees that anybody ever goes. Benorios's arrangement does not guarantee it either. It guarantees that the four never go to different gates, and makes going certain in the sense that failing to go has no chance at all. The difference between on every course and with probability one is the whole of what the coin bought.

Three things would change matters at the foot of the hill: a longest flight, or some report of who has died, or all the deaths happening before the evening begins*. The coin is a fourth way, and a different kind. The other three give the four men more knowledge of the world. The coin gives them none. It gives the world less power over them.

Chapter VIII

What Chance Does Not Buy

More than half must live. The arrangement needs the dead to be fewer than half, and that is not a weakness of the arrangement. With two of the four allowed to fall silent, no arrangement at all, with coins or without, can keep the first demand while keeping the other two.

Half is too many

If half the men or more may fall silent, no arrangement, with or without coins, can meet all three demands: that no two go to different gates, that matching sightings are followed, and that every man not killed eventually goes.

Proof — that with two of four silent, the gates can divide
  1. Suppose the bull and the boar sighted north and the trident and the stag sighted south, and take any arrangement that follows matching sightings and makes every man not killed go eventually.
  2. Two men may be silent, so the bull and the boar must be able to go without ever hearing from the trident or the stag: for all they can tell, those two are dead. Hold every bird from the trident and the stag in the pines; the bull and the boar, hearing only each other, go. Whatever their coins show, what they see is exactly what they would see on a night when all four sighted north and the other two were killed at dusk, and on that night the second demand sends them north. So they go north.
  3. In the same way hold every bird from the bull and the boar, and the trident and the stag go south.
  4. Do both at once, releasing the held birds only after all four have gone: all four are alive, every bird is merely late, and neither pair can tell. The pairs go to different gates.∎

Ben-Or notes this in a single line: with half or more silent, the order of birds can imitate a hill split in two. Four tents bear one silent man, and no more.

Liars are another matter. Nobody lies at the foot of the hill, and this book has not needed to ask what a lying man could do. Ben-Or's paper gives a second arrangement, of the same shape, for men who may lie as well as die: each man must be able to tell whose bird he holds, and the liars must be fewer than one in five. Against one liar that is six men. The four bandits on the crown could not use it, and do not need to; they have their own methods*. Ben-Or did not know whether one in five was the best that could be done.

It is not quick. With many men and many possible deaths, the tosses that must all come up alike are many too, and Ben-Or warns that the expected number of rounds may grow very fast. Ben-Or showed one happier case: if the silent are few compared with the whole, about the square root of their number, and the birds happen to come in step, the rounds needed on average do not grow at all. No deterministic arrangement can do that when birds come in step, since those need at least one more round than there are men who may fail.

It does not choose a good gate. The coins say nothing about which gate is wiser. When the sightings agree, the four go where they sighted; when they do not, the gate is whichever the coins and the birds happen to reach. As always on the hill, nothing follows from it: no assault is told, and no one learns whether it was the right gate.

The ideas, plainly

Fischer, Lynch and Paterson proved that no deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system if even one process may crash: an adversary ordering the messages can always keep the outcome undecided. Their proof depends on determinism, since the adversary must know in advance what each step will lead to.

Ben-Or showed that randomness removes the obstacle. Processes run in rounds. In each round every process broadcasts its current value, waits for N − t values, and broadcasts a proposal marked D if a strict majority of all N processes hold the same value. It then waits for N − t proposals: it decides on more than t D-proposals for a value, adopts the value on at least one, and otherwise flips a private fair coin.

Safety holds on every run. Two strict majorities intersect, so only one value can be proposed with D in a round; a decision means more than t D-proposals exist, so every process that finishes the round sees one and adopts the value, and all decide it in the next round. Termination holds with probability 1 when N > 2t: once every correct process holds the same value, the next round decides, and the coin flips keep giving that a chance the adversary cannot remove. Ben-Or states this without proof; a complete proof, which must handle an adversary that sees earlier flips, is Aguilera and Toueg's (2012). Runs that never terminate still exist, but together they have probability zero.

The protocol does not contradict FLP; it changes a hypothesis. It needs a correct majority, which is necessary, and it may take many rounds. Later work replaced the private coins with a shared coin to make the expected number of rounds small.
