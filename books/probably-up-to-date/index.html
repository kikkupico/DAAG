Probably Up to Date

Based on “Probabilistically Bounded Staleness for Practical Partial Quorums” by Peter Bailis, Shivaram Venkataraman, Michael J. Franklin, Joseph M. Hellerstein and Ion Stoica (2012)

When a reader asks only a few of the libraries that keep a work, how likely is the copy he is handed to be out of date, and by how many editions? Can a library promise that it is probably not?

A work on the scholars' coast is kept at three libraries. A new edition is sent to all three by courier and announced as done once enough have answered; a reader asks all three and takes the first replies. If the numbers are small the answers come quickly, and some reader will sometimes be handed an older copy. The book works out how often, by how many editions, for how long, and why that is often good enough.

Setting The Scholars' Coast

In a Nutshell

A work is kept at three libraries. A writer sends a new edition to all three and announces it once some of them have answered; a reader asks all three and takes the newest of the first few replies. When the two numbers are small the groups may miss each other and the reader is handed an old copy. The book finds how likely that is, how fast the chance falls with the number of editions a reader will accept and with the time since the announcement, and why only a probability, never a promise, can be given. In real terms, this covers Dynamo-style partial quorums and Probabilistically Bounded Staleness: k-staleness, t-visibility, the WARS model and monotonic reads.

Chapter I

A Work and Its Keepers

On the scholars' coast of Paxos a library answers a reader from whatever copy it holds, and lives with copies that differ for a while*. Some works are kept at more than one library. Say a work is kept at three: its keepers are those three libraries and no others, and every copy of the work on the coast is held by one of them. Each copy is an edition with a tally, the day and hour by the sun at which it was written, with the library's number to break a tie, and of two editions the larger tally is the newer.

What happens when someone writes a new edition? The writer, at his own library, sends a courier to each of the three keepers with the new edition. A keeper that receives it enters it on its copy and sends a courier back to say so. When enough keepers have answered, the writer announces the edition: from then on he regards it as done. The couriers still on the road keep walking, so a keeper that has not yet answered goes on receiving the edition after the announcement.

And what does a reader do? He writes to all three keepers, asking for the work, and takes the newest copy among the first few replies; he does not wait for the rest. How few? That is the second number, and the whole book is about what the two numbers cost and what they buy: how many keepers a writer waits for, and how many replies a reader takes.

Is there any rule that makes the reader's copy the newest? Only if the numbers are chosen so. Otherwise the keepers he hears from may all be ones the edition has not yet reached, and nothing in the replies says so. How often that happens, and how bad it is, is a matter of chance and of the couriers' roads. Can that be said exactly?

Before asking how likely a stale copy is, which numbers rule it out altogether, and what do they cost?

Chapter II

How Many to Wait For

Why not wait for all three keepers, and ask all three? Because the slowest courier then decides how soon anything is done. The writer announces only when the last acknowledgment is back, and the reader answers only when the last reply is in, and a single slow road or a stopped courier holds up everyone. Waiting for fewer is quicker: the writer announces after the first acknowledgments, and the reader answers on the first replies.

What does waiting for fewer risk? That the group the writer waited for and the group the reader asked do not share a library. Suppose the writer waits for one acknowledgment and the reader takes one reply, with three keepers. The edition has certainly reached the keeper that answered the writer; the reader's first reply may come from a different keeper, one the edition has not reached, and he is handed the old copy.

Is there a pair of numbers for which this cannot happen? Yes, and it is the rule the Chamber has always used. A decree there passes when a majority hold it, and any two majorities of the same legislators must share one*. The same holds for keepers: when the number the writer waits for and the number the reader takes add up to more than the number of keepers, the two groups cannot help sharing a library. With three keepers, waiting for two and taking two is enough. The reader then always hears from at least one keeper that has the announced edition, and since he takes the newest copy he hears, he is handed it or something newer.

Proof — that R + W > N forces every read quorum to meet every write quorum
  1. This is the standard property of strict quorums, which the paper recalls in §2. Let there be N replicas, a write quorum QW of size W and a read quorum QR of size R, both subsets of the same N replicas.
  2. |QW ∪ QR| ≤ N, since both are subsets of a set of size N.
  3. |QW ∩ QR| = |QW| + |QR| − |QW ∪ QR| ≥ W + R − N.
  4. If R + W > N, the right side is at least 1, so the two quorums share a replica. That replica holds the committed write, and a read that returns the value with the greatest version among its responses returns that write or a later one.∎

Why would anyone choose numbers that add up to no more than the keepers? For speed. The paper observes that real systems with three copies often wait for one and ask one, and that this is a deliberate choice, made for the quicker answers, which matter more than occasional old copies. On the coast it is the same: waiting for one and asking one is the quick way, and a reader may then be handed an old copy.

If the quick way sometimes hands over an old copy, how often is that, and does it get better with more keepers?

Chapter III

When the Groups Miss Each Other

The chance of an old copy can be worked out in the plainest case, where the keepers that answer the writer, and the keepers the reader hears from, are chosen as if by lot. With three keepers, the writer waiting for one and the reader taking one: the edition is at one keeper, and the reader's keeper is one of the three. He misses it with chance two in three. A reader handed a copy that is out of date is the usual case, not the rare one.

Does it get better with more keepers? Yes, and quickly, if the numbers grow with them. With ten keepers, the writer waiting for three and the reader taking three, the chance of a miss is under three in ten. With thirty keepers and nine and nine, about one in fifty. With a hundred keepers and thirty and thirty, about two in a million. The reason is the birthday paradox: two groups drawn from a large crowd are very unlikely to share no one. The paper calls such systems probabilistic quorums, and their odds are excellent.

But for whom? For a coast with a hundred keepers of each work. A work on this coast has three, and the paper notes that real systems keep one to three copies. The odds that are so good for a hundred are poor for three, and nothing about the large case helps the small one. The good news has to come from somewhere else.

Proof — that the probability of a read missing the last write is C(N − W, R) / C(N, R)
  1. This is Equation 1 of §2.1, with the paper's assumption that the W replicas written and the R replicas read are each chosen uniformly at random.
  2. The read quorum is one of the C(N, R) subsets of size R, each equally likely.
  3. The read misses the write quorum exactly when every one of its R members lies among the N − W replicas not written to. There are C(N − W, R) such subsets.
  4. So ps = C(N − W, R) / C(N, R). For N = 3, R = W = 1 this is 2/3; for N = 100, R = W = 30 it is about 1.88 × 10−6, as the paper states.∎

So a reader on this coast, with three keepers and the quick way, is likely to be handed an old copy. Is that all there is to say? No, because the reader asked something narrower than is this the newest? Usually he would be content with is this one of the newest? Is an edition or two behind as bad as an old copy of a different age?

If a reader will accept one of the newest few editions, how much does the chance of an old copy fall?

Chapter IV

How Many Editions Behind

A reader who will take any of the newest few editions is missed only if his keepers lack every one of them. Each announced edition was sent to its own group of keepers, and the reader's group has the same chance of missing each. So the chance of missing the newest two is the chance of missing one, multiplied by itself; of missing the newest three, multiplied again. The chance of being handed an old copy falls exponentially with the number of editions the reader will accept.

What does that come to? With three keepers, the quick way, the chance of being handed the newest edition is one in three, but the chance of being handed one of the newest two is five in nine, of the newest three about seven in ten, of the newest five about 87 in a hundred, and of the newest ten about 98 in a hundred. If the writer waits for two keepers and the reader still takes one, the chances are better: two in three for the newest, eight in nine for one of the newest two, and over 99 in a hundred for one of the newest five.

Proof — that PBS k-staleness is psk = psk
  1. This is §3.1 for quorums that do not expand. A read returns a value within k versions of the latest committed version exactly when its read quorum intersects at least one of the write quorums of the last k committed versions.
  2. Treat the k write quorums as independent, each missed by the read quorum with probability ps = C(N − W, R) / C(N, R).
  3. The read quorum misses all k with probability psk, so psk = psk and the read is within k versions with probability 1 − psk.
  4. With N = 3, R = W = 1, ps = 2/3, so 1 − (2/3)k is 5/9 for k = 2, 0.704 for k = 3, 0.868 for k = 5 and 0.983 for k = 10. With W = 2, ps = 1/3 and the values are 2/3, 8/9 and 0.996 for k = 1, 2 and 5, as the paper's examples give.
  5. For expanding quorums the paper notes this is an upper bound on the probability of staleness, since writes only reach more replicas with time.∎

Is there a second gain? Yes. A library that need not return the newest edition need not ask as many of its keepers, so a busy keeper is asked less often. The paper shows that the load on the busiest keeper falls as the number of editions of leeway grows, which means that a coast with such readers can serve more of them with the same libraries.

The numbers above ignore time altogether. They would be the same whether the edition had been announced a moment ago or a week ago, and yet the edition does not stand still: the couriers still on the road are still arriving. Surely a reader who comes a week later has better odds than one who comes at once?

If editions keep arriving after the announcement, how do the odds change with the time since it?

Chapter V

How Long Until It Shows

The edition was sent to all three keepers at the start, so after the announcement it goes on reaching those that had not answered. A reader who comes a while after the announcement is therefore asking keepers of which more have it than when it was announced. How much better are his odds? The answer is the visibility of the edition after that while: the chance that a reader coming that long after the announcement is handed the edition or a newer one.

What decides it? The number of keepers that hold the edition at that moment. If all three do, no reader can miss it. If only the one that answered first does, the odds are as bad as in the quick case. In between, the chance of a miss is the chance that the reader's keepers are all among those that lack it. So to know the odds after a given while, the book needs to know how likely each number of keepers is to hold the edition by then, which depends on how long the couriers take.

Does the edition's visibility ever lapse? Yes: if a second edition is announced before the first one has finished arriving, the first no longer matters, because a reader handed the second is handed something newer, and the first edition's visibility after that while is no longer asked. And an edition that is written but not yet announced is counted as fresh, not as stale: a reader who is handed it is handed something newer than the announced edition, not older.

Proof — that the probability of a stale read t after commit is a sum over how many replicas hold the write
  1. This is §3.4. Let qc(t) be the probability that exactly c replicas hold version v at time t after v commits, for c in [W, N]; at commit exactly W do with certainty, so qW(0) = 1.
  2. Conditional on exactly c replicas holding v, a read quorum chosen uniformly misses all of them with probability C(N − c, R) / C(N, R), by the argument of Equation 1.
  3. Summing over the possible c: pst = Σc=WN qc(t) · C(N − c, R) / C(N, R). The paper writes this with its distribution Pw(c, t) of the number of replicas reached; its printed Equations 4 and 5 differ from one another in the range of the sum and the sign of the difference, and the sum above is the quantity both mean.
  4. The paper notes that this assumes instant reads and instant commitment after the Wth copy arrives, so it is a conservative upper bound on the true probability of staleness. When the last k writes all happened at the same time the same argument gives the chance of missing all k as the kth power, the paper's ⟨k, t⟩-staleness.∎

The combined question, one of the newest few editions, a while after the announcement, has, as a conservative rule of thumb, the same answer raised to the power of the number of editions, so a reader may trade one kind of leeway for the other. That is why the book studies the odds in editions and in time one at a time. Whatever fixes how many keepers hold the edition at a given moment is the thing left to understand.

What decides how many keepers hold an edition a while after it is announced, and when does a reader beat it to a keeper?

Chapter VI

The Four Legs

Four journeys are in every write and read, and each takes a time that varies from courier to courier. There is the edition's way out from the writer to a keeper; the acknowledgment's way back; the request's way out from a reader to a keeper; and the copy's way back to the reader. Take one keeper. The writer sends the edition, which arrives after its way out. The writer announces once enough keepers have answered, and some time later a reader sends his request, which arrives at the keeper after its own way out.

When is the keeper's reply stale? When the reader's request reaches the keeper before the edition does. The keeper then has only the old edition to give, and it sends that back. The edition arrives afterwards and is useless to this reader. The reader is handed an old copy only if this is so for every one of the first replies he takes: if even one of them comes from a keeper that already holds the edition, he is handed it.

What makes this likelier? A slow and uneven way out for the edition, and quick requests. When the edition sometimes takes very long, a keeper can lag far behind the announcement; when requests are quick, they reach the keeper before the edition does. The numbers waited for and asked make the other difference: when they add to more than the keepers, the groups share a library, and no read can be stale.

Can the chance be worked out in closed form, as the chance for editions was? No, and the paper says so. The time of announcement is the time that the writer's chosen number of acknowledgments have come back, which depends on the first few of the combined journeys out and back, and the reader's chosen replies depend on the first few of the combined requests and copies. Each of these depends on the order of many random times, and the two overlap. So the odds are found by playing the situation over many times, drawing each journey's time from what the couriers' logbook records, and counting how often the reader is behind.

Proof — that under WARS no read is stale when R + W > N
  1. This is the remark of §4.1. Let the write be sent at time 0, reach replica i at wi (a draw from W) and be acknowledged at wi + ai (a draw from A). The write commits at wt, the Wth smallest of these acknowledgment times.
  2. The W replicas whose acknowledgments came first each received the write at wi ≤ wt.
  3. A read begins t ≥ 0 after commit, so its request to replica i arrives at wt + t + ri (a draw from R), which is at least wi for each of those W replicas. Such a replica returns the write.
  4. The read takes the first R responses, which are R distinct replicas. If R + W > N they cannot all lie among the N − W other replicas, so at least one is among the W that hold the write, and the read is not stale.∎

The method of §5.1 for the general case: draw N samples from each of W, A, R and S; compute wt, the Wth smallest of the sums W[i] + A[i]; order the replicas by R[i] + S[i]; and count the read stale when each of the first R satisfies wt + R[i] + t ≤ W[i]. Repeating this many times gives the probability for each t.

Is the replay to be trusted? The authors checked it against a working system they set up, with chosen journey times for each leg: across fifty thousand writes, the predicted chance of seeing the edition after each moment differed from the measured by well under one part in a hundred on average. So the replay stands in for the real thing.

What do the couriers' real logbooks give when the replay is run on them?

Chapter VII

What the Logbooks Say

The first finding is about which of the four journeys matters. With three keepers, the quick way, and journey times that are only a little uneven, the paper made the edition's way out slower or quicker than the other three. The slower and more uneven it was, the longer the reader needed to wait for near certainty: when it was very quick and even, a reader was handed the edition nine times in ten the moment it was announced and 999 times in a thousand a little later; when it was ten times slower than the others, only four in ten at once and the same 999 in a thousand only after a long wait. The unevenness of the way out mattered more than its average.

Then it turned to the real logbooks of two large systems, fitted by the authors from the times the operators gave them. Take four coasts. On the first, roads are quick and copying an edition into a keeper is quick, and a reader hears the new edition about 97 times in a hundred the moment it is announced and almost always a moment later. On the second, the way out has a long tail, now and then taking very long, and the odds at once are about 89 in a hundred; but the tail hampers the climb to near certainty, which takes the longest of the four. On the third, copying an edition onto papyrus is slow, and the odds at once are about 44 in a hundred. On the fourth the keepers are on both sides of a sea, and only about a third of readers are handed the new edition at once, until the long crossing has been made.

Does adding keepers help? Less than one expects, and in an odd way. With more keepers the chance of a reader hearing the new edition at once falls: on the slow-copying coast, from about 58 in a hundred with two keepers to about 21 in a hundred with ten. But the time needed to reach near certainty barely changes with the number of keepers: from two keepers to ten it rose by about a fifth. Many keepers hurt the first moment and not the end.

What is bought by taking the quick way, then? On the long-tailed coast, waiting for one and asking one gives the quickest answers and a long wait for near certainty; asking for a second reply cut the wait to about a seventh, and the answers, writer and reader together, were still about four-fifths faster, even at the slowest tenth of one in a hundred, than the fastest way with no chance of a miss. The paper's point is that the price of a small chance of an old copy is small and the saving is large, which is why, in practice, the quick way is so often chosen.

A reader may accept an old copy once. Should he ever be handed an older one than he was handed before?

Chapter VIII

Never Going Backwards

A reader who comes back to a work may not mind that his copy is not the newest, but he does mind finding that it is older than the one he read last time. He wants to see the work move only forward. This is the guarantee the paper calls monotonic reads, and it is a special case of leeway in editions: the reader needs a copy at least as new as the one he was handed before, and the editions that will do are the one he saw and every edition announced since.

How many is that? The number announced between his visits, plus the one he saw. If the work is written to a hundred times as often as the reader visits, a hundred editions will do; if he visits as often as it is written, only two. So the chance of going backwards is the chance of an old copy raised to the power of one plus the ratio of the work's writing rate to the reader's visiting rate. The reader who visits seldom has the widest range and the best odds; the reader who visits all the time has the narrowest.

Can a reader be sure of never going backwards? Yes, in one way: if he keeps to the same keeper every time, whose copy only ever moves forward, he can never be handed anything older than before, unless that keeper is lost. This is sticky use of one library. It does not make him see strictly newer editions where there are any; for that the reader would need the keeper to hand over a newer one if it exists, and the paper treats strict monotonic reads separately, with the exponent one less. The odds for the readers who do not stick are the ones the book has worked out; the paper adds that a library may control the rates, by limiting how often a reader may come, to bring the odds where it wants.

Proof — that PBS monotonic reads is k-staleness with k = 1 + γgw / γcr
  1. This is §3.2. Let a client read a data item at rate γcr and let the item be written, by all clients, at rate γgw. Between two successive reads by this client the expected number of versions committed is γgw / γcr.
  2. A read satisfies monotonic reads if it returns the version the client last read, or any newer one. If the last read returned version vi−k and the latest committed is vi, with γgw / γcr versions in between, the acceptable versions are the newest 1 + γgw / γcr.
  3. That is PBS k-staleness with k = 1 + γgw / γcr, so the probability of violating monotonic reads is psMR = (C(N − W, R) / C(N, R))1 + γgw / γcr, Equation 3.
  4. For strict monotonic reads, where the client must read strictly newer data if any exists, the exponent is γgw / γcr. The paper uses expected values of the rates, which in practice are measured as distributions.∎

So a library can promise a reader a good chance of never going backwards. A chance, not a promise: is there anything the paper does not claim?

What can a library say to a reader, and what must it leave unsaid?

Chapter IX

What It Does Not Say

Does a library promise a reader that his copy is current? No. What it can say is nine times in ten you are handed the newest, and nearly always one of the newest few, which is a probability and an expectation. In the worst case, with the quick way, a reader can be handed an old copy of any age: nothing in the numbers puts a bound on how old, and the paper says so. The advance is to measure the chance and tell the reader, not to turn it into a guarantee.

What do the odds assume? The paper says plainly. The odds for editions assume the keepers that answer are chosen as if by lot and the groups for different editions are independent. The odds for time assume the couriers' journeys are independent and alike, drawn afresh each time from the logbook, with the logbook a fair picture of how the couriers will go next, and the logbooks themselves were fitted from summaries and are not complete. They assume the usual state of the coast: no library closed, no courier stopped; where those happen, the paper says they only lengthen the tails of the logbook. And they assume the least help: no festival and no word of mouth, and no reader who finds a keeper behind and puts it right; the paper counts none of these, since they depend on how often they happen, so the real odds are better than the book's.

Can a reader tell, when he is handed a copy, whether it is stale? Not then. To know he would need to know the newest edition, which is what he lacks. He can find out afterwards: the keepers he did not wait for still answer, and a late reply with a newer tally shows that the copy he was given was behind. That finding can be wrong in a harmless way, since the later reply may carry an edition written after his request, or one not yet announced, which is not staleness; to be rid of those the coast would have to agree on one order of announcement for every work, which it does not do, and the paper notes that it would need changes. A reader has therefore no means in hand of knowing in the moment.

Does it say anything for readers who need more than recency, such as seeing a letter's reply only after the letter? No. Those are other guarantees, which the paper leaves aside, conjecturing that their odds in the worst case would be low. And it leaves open the choice of keepers' numbers for a stated odds, that a coast could set the two numbers by what it measures, and offer readers the quickest way that still meets a promise of odds. That is the use the authors suggest, not one they build.

The ideas, plainly

Bailis and his co-authors study data kept on several machines, where a write is acknowledged after W of N machines have it and a read returns after R machines have answered. If R + W exceeds N, a read always meets the latest write. Operators often choose smaller numbers, for speed, so that a read may miss it. The paper asks how often, and shows that a read within k versions of the latest has probability 1 minus p to the k, where p is the chance of a plain miss: the chance of being stale falls exponentially with the leeway allowed. Monotonic reads is the same idea with k set by how often a reader returns compared with how often the data is written.

Staleness in time is harder, because writes keep reaching more machines after they are acknowledged. The paper models the four message delays of a write and a read, W, A, R and S, shows that a read is stale when its request reaches a replica before the write does, and works out the odds by simulation using measured delays. The results are that the delay of the write message, and above all its variance, matters most, and that typically the chance of reading the latest write is high within a short time of its commit, at a latency far below that of a strict quorum.

What the paper offers is therefore a probability and a way to tune it, not a guarantee: the worst case stays unbounded, and the odds rest on assumptions about independence and about the delays staying as they were measured.
