# Hellenistic Greek (323-31 BC) dimensions, second pass: gaps in arms, armour, siege engines, clothing, animals

Companion to `dimensions_arms_dress.md` (first pass; not repeated here). Labels: certainty P = museum record or primary text, S = secondary/scholarly summary, W = Wikipedia, L = low (retailer, reenactor, popular site), E = estimate or computed here. Period flag: [H] Hellenistic, [C] Classical (479-323), [A] Archaic, [R] Roman. Retrieval date for all web sources: 2026-10-02. Met object links are `https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/{id}`; data pulled from `https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/public/collection/v1/objects/{id}`. Nothing is invented; unanswered items sit in Gaps.

## Sarissa head conflict (51 cm Vergina vs about 13.5 cm)

### Takeaway
Both numbers are real but answer different questions. 51 cm / 1.235 kg is the one large leaf-shaped iron head from a Vergina tomb, identified by Andronikos as a sarissa head; modern scholars doubt it was a combat head (Connolly: probably a butt-spike, i.e. sauroter; Sekunda: a ceremonial rank-badge spear). 13.5 cm diamond-shaped iron is Sekunda's reconstruction for the working battlefield head. For modelling a phalangite, use about 13.5 cm (E) and mark it as reconstruction; the 51 cm piece is a one-off.

### Cited Findings
- Vergina find: leaf-shaped, 51 cm long, 1.235 kg, found by Manolis Andronikos; "21st-century scholarship has been more skeptical" that it was a sarissa head. Certainty W (summarising Andronikos), Late Classical/early Hellenistic [C/H] — [Wikipedia: Sarissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa)
- Connolly: the 51 cm piece is "more likely a spear butt rather than a spearhead"; Sekunda: a spearhead used only on ceremonial spears as a badge of rank — [Wikipedia: Sarissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa)
- Sekunda reconstruction: tip about 13.5 cm (5.3 in), iron, diamond-shaped, because ancient writers say the sarissa pierced shield and armour (small focused head). Certainty E/W, [H] — [Wikipedia: Sarissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa)
- Shaft probably slightly tapered; the article gives no diameter or sauroter length — [Wikipedia: Sarissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarissa)

### Inferences
- Resolution: model the infantry sarissa with a short (about 13.5 cm) diamond iron head and a long sauroter; if a long leaf head is wanted for a ceremonial or officer's spear, the Vergina 51 cm / 1.2 kg specimen is the only evidence. The weight of the 51 cm piece alone (1.2 kg) is a strong reason it cannot have been the head on a 5.8 m infantry shaft (4 kg total in Connolly's reconstruction, first pass).

### Gaps
- Sauroter length, shaft diameter (Wikipedia gives none). The primary Andronikos and Sekunda publications were not opened.

## Spears and javelins (doru, thureophoros spear, akontion, xyston)

### Takeaway
Dory is about 2-3 m, 0.9-1.8 kg with a 5 cm grip; xyston 3.5-4.25 m. No measured Hellenistic akontion or thureophoros spear found. Those are Classical-derived or reenactor values.

### Cited Findings
- Dory: about 2-3 m long, grip diameter about 5 cm, weight 0.91-1.81 kg, counterbalanced by an iron sauroter. Mostly [A/C] data; used in [H] by non-phalanx hoplites and thureophoroi. Certainty W — [Wikipedia: Dory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dory_(spear))
- Xyston (cavalry lance): about 3.5-4.25 m long; wood density 825 kg/m3 (cornel). Certainty W, [C/H] — [Wikipedia: Xyston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xyston)
- Thureophoros spear: Galatoi refers to "the standard Greek three meters spear" and a longer dory for Iphikrates' light hoplites, with no figure. [C/H], certainty L — [Galatoi: Thureophoroi](https://www.galatoi.com/warriors/thureophoroi.php)
- Met spearheads (all pre-Hellenistic, shown only for scale): Cypriot iron spearheads 14.1-28.1 cm [A], e.g. [244192](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/244192) (28.1 cm), [244196](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/244196) (14.1 cm). Certainty P, but not Hellenistic.

### Inferences
- Use dory 2.4 m (middle of 2-3 m) as thureophoros spear (E); akontion not measurable from sources found.

### Gaps
- Akontion length/weight, thureophoros spear and sauroter lengths, xyston head size: no source found. Wikipedia Javelin page and Machaira/Thureophoros pages contain no figures.

## Siege engines: formulas worked out (euthytonon, palintonon)

### Takeaway
Two formulas, d = L/9 (bolt-throwers) and d = 1.1 x cube root(100 m) dactyls (stone-throwers, m in minae), reduce to the same modern expression d = 0.130 x cube root(m) metres (m in kg). Standard sizes below are computed here (E), not read from tables.

### Cited Findings
- Euthytonon spring washer diameter f = arrow length / 9; palintonon f = 0.130 x cube root(m) metres, m in kg (SI form of Philo's formula, which was written in Attic minae). Standardisation around 220 BC per this paper (Marsden: c. 270 BC Alexandria). Certainty S — [IMEKO 2023, Paasch](https://www.imeko.org/publications/tc4-Archaeo-2023/IMEKO-MetroArchaeo-2023-039.pdf)
- Ampurias (Spain) is the only surviving Greek catapult frame; four circular bronze washers survive (Museo Arqueologico, Barcelona) — same paper.
- Vitruvius X.10 (c. 25 BC, source tradition Ctesibius/Diades) proportions in hole diameters: arrow length 9 holes; capital plates 1 thick, 1.75 wide; side posts 4 high x 5 thick; slider channel 19 holes; windlass 8.125 holes; arm length 7 holes. Certainty S (relayed summary of the translation; check Gaps), [H tradition, R text] — [Vitruvius X, LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/10*.html)
- Vitruvius X.11 ballista stone-to-hole table (Roman lb, digits): 2 lb: 5 digits; 4: 6; 6: 7; 10: 8; 20: 10; 40: 12 9/16; 100 lb: 1.5 ft + 1.5 digits (relayed). [R text] — same.

### Inferences (computed, E)
Constants: dactyl 1.93 cm; mina 437 g; Philo's talent = 60 mina = 26.2 kg.
- Euthytonon, bolt 3 spans (3 x 12 dactyl = 36 dactyl = 69.5 cm): d = 36/9 = 4 dactyl = 7.7 cm. This agrees with the 60-74 cm full-arrow range in the IMEKO table.
- Euthytonon, bolt 2 cubits (48 dactyl, 92.6 cm): d = 5.3 dactyl = 10.3 cm.
- Euthytonon, bolt 1 cubit (24 dactyl, 46.3 cm): d = 2.7 dactyl = 5.1 cm.
- Using Vitruvius multiples with d = 7.7 cm: arm 7d = 54 cm; slider channel 19d = 1.46 m; windlass 8.1d = 62 cm; post height 4d = 31 cm, post thickness 5d = 38 cm. Overall width of frame about 4 spring-holes plus posts, roughly 1 m (E, rough).
- Palintonon, 10 mina stone (4.37 kg): d = 1.1 x cbrt(1000) = 11 dactyl = 21.2 cm (IMEKO form: 0.130 x cbrt(4.37) = 21.2 cm, matches).
- Palintonon, 30 mina (13.1 kg): d = 1.1 x cbrt(3000) = 15.9 dactyl = 30.6 cm.
- Palintonon, 1 talent (26.2 kg): d = 20.0 dactyl = 38.6 cm (matches first pass).
- Palintonon, 3 talent (78.6 kg; Archimedes class): d = 1.1 x cbrt(18000) = 28.8 dactyl = 55.6 cm.
- Caution: Vitruvius' ballista table is not the Philo formula; for 2 Roman lb (0.65 kg) it gives 5 digits (about 9 cm) while Philo's formula at 1.5 mina (0.655 kg) gives about 11.3 cm. Digit and pound conversions are assumed here, so treat the mismatch as unresolved.

### Gaps
- Marsden (1969/1971) and Kotsanas full reconstructed frame lengths, widths and weights were not retrievable (the Philo translation PDF at Harvard returned HTML; Wikipedia gave none). Heron/Philo euthytonon multiples beyond arm 7d were not independently checked, and the Vitruvius figures are a relayed summary of the English translation.

## Rams, tortoises, siege towers

### Takeaway
Only literary figures (Vitruvius, c. 25 BC, citing Hellenistic engineers). Use as relative proportions.

### Cited Findings
- Tortoise (testudo) for filling ditches: base 25 ft square, posts 9 ft high, 1 ft 1 palm wide; wheels on iron axles; roof palm-wood planking with wicker and raw hides stuffed with seaweed/straw. Certainty S (relayed), [H tradition] — [Vitruvius X, LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/10*.html)
- Ram-tortoise of Agetor of Byzantium (X.15): base 60 ft x 18 ft; eight wheels 6.75 ft high, 3 ft thick; posts 36 ft high; ram 106 ft long, 1 ft 1 palm at butt tapering to 1 ft x 5/8 in head; four 8 in ropes; worked by 100 men; weight 4,000 talents (as stated). Certainty S (relayed) — same.
- Helepolis by Epimachus (Vitruvius): 125 ft high, 60 ft wide, 360,000 lb, proof against a 360 lb stone. This differs from the 135 ft cited in the first pass: the 135 vs 125 spread is a translation/manuscript issue, flagged — same.

### Inferences
- Roman foot about 29.6 cm (assumed): ram of 106 ft is about 31 m; Agetor tortoise about 17.8 m x 5.3 m; helepolis about 37 m x 18 m. Converted here (E). Vitruvius feet for Hellenistic Greek machines are taken as Roman feet; the Greek foot (about 30.8 cm) differs by 4 percent.

### Gaps
- Archaeological or Kotsanas reconstructions of ram heads (iron-capped) and tortoise: not found.

## Human proportions: Lysippan canon, statures, children

### Takeaway
The Lysippan canon is a head-to-height ratio of 1:8 (Polykleitos 1:7). No measured child heights found.

### Cited Findings
- Pliny, Natural History 34.65: Lysippos made heads smaller than the older sculptors and bodies leaner and drier, so figures looked taller; modern restatement: head one-eighth of height (Polykleitos one-seventh). [C/H, literary] — [Wikipedia: Lysippos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysippos); [Wikipedia: Apoxyomenos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apoxyomenos)
- Vatican Apoxyomenos (Roman marble copy of a Lysippan bronze): 2.05 m, "slightly larger than lifesize"; Croatian bronze Apoxyomenos 1.92 m. Certainty W — [Wikipedia: Apoxyomenos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apoxyomenos)
- Met Hellenistic bronze torso in muscle cuirass (63.5 x 67.3 x 38.1 cm, Greek or Roman): useful for cuirass-torso depth versus width (about 0.57 depth/width) — [Met 257639](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/257639)

### Inferences
- A 171 cm Hellenistic man (first pass, Angel) at 8 heads gives a head of about 21 cm in the canon; real skulls are about 22-24 cm tall, so canon figures are idealised (E).

### Gaps
- Child heights by age: no Greek sourced figures found. Terracotta boys at the Met (15.8 cm, [247482](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/247482)) are figurine scale, not child stature.

## Animals

### Takeaway
Ancient Greek horses were small: about 130-150 cm at the withers. War elephants: Indian about 2.4-2.75 m (Asian species figures, modern), Ptolemaic North African about 2.4-2.5 m. Cattle, mule, donkey and dog only have loose sources.

### Cited Findings
- Greek cavalry horse average 13.1 hh (about 133 cm); Thessalian 140-150 cm modern breed, "around 13.2 hh" for the ancient type; Macedonian estimated 130-140 cm. Certainty L (search summary, includes modern-breed data and an estimate), [C/H] — [Xenophon and the ancient Greek cavalry horse (Academia; page not retrievable, 403)](https://www.academia.edu/112169684/Xenophon_and_the_ancient_Greek_cavalry_horse_an_equestrian_perspective); [Amalthia: Thessaly horse](https://amalthia.org/en/breeds/horses/163-thessaly)
- Met bronze horse statuette, late 2nd-1st c. BC: 40.2 cm high (figurine) — [Met 251268](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/251268)
- Asian (Indian) elephant, adult: bull about 2.75 m at shoulder, 4.0 t; cow about 2.40 m, 2.7 t. Modern species data; the Seleucid elephants. Certainty W — [Wikipedia: Asian elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_elephant)
- North African elephant (Ptolemaic): "very small elephants, perhaps 2.5 m at the shoulder", from Carthaginian frescoes and coins; Polybius said they lost to the larger Indian elephants (Raphia, 217 BC). A secondary source gives 2.35 m (Indian just under 3 m). Certainty W/L, [H] — [Wikipedia: North African elephant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_African_elephant); [Battle of Raphia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Raphia). African forest elephant males average 2.2 m, largest 2.75 m — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_forest_elephant)
- Greek donkey: "Mediterranean ass", about 1 m at withers, 120 kg (breed-site claim, modern); modern Greek donkeys rarely exceed 125 cm. Certainty L, modern — [Amalthia: Greek donkey](https://www.amalthia.org/en/component/content/article?id=163%3Agreek-donkey)
- Mule: Roman-period archaeological mules 132-148 cm withers; military-site mules about 140-155 cm; an Iron Age Barcelona mule 105-126 cm. Not Greek; [R]/Iron Age proxy. Certainty S — [ScienceDirect: Roman Switzerland equids](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305440320301746); [La Brujula Verde](https://www.labrujulaverde.com/en/2025/11/the-oldest-mule-in-europe-found-in-barcelona-sacrificed-and-buried-with-a-woman-2700-years-ago-in-the-early-iron-age/)
- Cattle: Classical and Hellenistic Greek and Roman cattle about 20 cm taller at the withers than Iron Age and medieval cattle (the search summary gave no absolute figure). Certainty L — search summary of [Archaeopress, Phoenice (Butrint) zooarchaeology](https://archaeopresspublishing.com/ojs/index.php/groma/article/download/fiori-zooarchaeological-analysis-phoenice-butrint/1573) (not opened)
- Molossian hound: "can reach 75 cm" and 60 kg+ (Wikipedia-level, mixes ancient and modern); a marble Molossian statue 66.5 cm high, 114 cm long [date not checked]. Laconian hound about 18 in (46 cm) at the shoulder (Wikipedia). Certainty W/L — [Wikipedia: Molossian hound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molossian_hound); [Wikipedia: Laconian dog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laconian_(dog))

### Inferences
- For modelling: horse 135 cm (E, 13.1-13.2 hh); Seleucid Indian elephant 2.5-2.75 m, Ptolemaic 2.4-2.5 m (E); donkey 1.0-1.25 m; mule 1.3-1.4 m if Greek-sized (E, Roman data is larger).

### Gaps
- Withers heights for Hellenistic Greek horses, cattle, mules, donkeys and dogs from osteometric reports (the zooarchaeology PDFs were not opened); ox draught size; ancient dog skeleton sizes.

## Clothing: textile sizes, himation conflict, footwear, hats

### Takeaway
No Hellenistic himation survives, so both ranges are modern reconstruction. The 4-5 m figure comes from popular and costume-history compilations ("five yards by one yard"); reenactor and experimental work uses smaller cloth. Resolution: model the himation at roughly 1.2-1.5 m wide by 2.5-3 m long for ordinary wear (E, within the brief's 2-3 m), and treat 4-5 m as an upper bound for voluminous Hellenistic drapery.

### Cited Findings
- Himation 4-5 m x 1.2-1.5 m (World History Encyclopedia type compilation); another account 5 yards x 1 yard (4.6 x 0.9 m); another 3-5 m x 1.5-2 m. Certainty L, none gives a primary or museum source — [World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/20/ancient-greek-clothing/); [Fashion-era](https://fashion-era.com/ancient-costume/chiton-clothing) (as in first pass); [Tastes of History](https://www.tastesofhistory.co.uk/post/how-to-dress-as-an-ancient-greek)
- Encyclopedia.com Himation (cites Houston 1947 and Symons 1987) and Wikipedia Himation/Draped garment give no measurements; the Encyclopedia.com page gives only "large rectangular pieces of fabric". The reenactor site below states "virtually no Greek clothing has survived" — [Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/sports-and-everyday-life/fashion-and-clothing/clothing-jewelry-and-personal-adornment/himation); [Wikipedia: Himation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himation)
- Chiton (reenactor reconstruction from vase and stele images): one rectangle about 3-5 ft tall x 6-10 ft wide (0.9-1.5 m x 1.8-3 m), doubled and pinned. Certainty L, based on art, not textiles — [The Greek Phalanx: Chitoniskos](https://www.thegreekphalanx.org/chitoniskos)
- Chlamys: experimental weaving at 76 x 178 cm, knee length, an approximation from 5th c. BC pottery images. Certainty L, [C] — [EXARC: Ancient Greek weaving](https://exarc.net/issue-2021-4/at/ancient-greek-weaving-experimental-archeology). First-pass chlamys 1.8-2.1 m x 1.1 m is consistent.
- Doric chiton cloth height = wearer's height plus 12 in, width = full arm span (popular source) — [Tastes of History](https://www.tastesofhistory.co.uk/post/how-to-dress-as-an-ancient-greek)
- Peplos / loom-cloth "4-5 ft wide x 5-7 ft long" appeared in a search summary; I could not trace it to a named source (the Wikipedia warp-weighted loom page does not say it). Not used.
- Sandals: terracotta left foot with sandal, South Italian Greek, 2nd half 4th c. BC, H 7.8 cm, W 18 cm (the Met's "w." probably the foot length; not confirmed) — [Met 247466](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/247466). Roman bronze foot with sandal straps, 1st-2nd c. ### Inferences
- Because no Hellenistic cloth survives, textile sizes are scaled to the body (arm span for the chiton width, height for the length), consistent with the brief's lower figures (E).
