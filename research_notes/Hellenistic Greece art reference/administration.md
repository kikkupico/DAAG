# Hellenistic Greek administration, law, finance, economy and civic life

Scope note: web research was shallow (about 15 calls). Cited findings are only what a fetched or searched page supported. Items from general scholarly background that I could not source this session are marked "[unsourced, from background knowledge; verify]" and sit under Inferences or Gaps. Most Wikipedia pages summarise Classical Athens, so treat them as a floor for Hellenistic practice.

## 1. City governance: assembly, council, magistrates, decrees, voting, euergetism

### Takeaway
Hellenistic poleis kept the Classical machinery (assembly plus council, enactment formula, stone publication) but ran on benefactors. Wealthy citizens moved from compulsory liturgies to voluntary euergetism, and the city paid them back in inscribed honours.

### Cited Findings
- Athenian ekklesia: met monthly at first, later three or four times a month. A quorum of 6,000 is attested "sometimes". Votes were by show of hands, counting stones, or ostraka. The boule set the agenda. Around 300 BC regular meetings moved from the Pnyx to the Theatre of Dionysus. — [Wikipedia, Ecclesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekklesia_(ancient_Athens))
- Pay for attending the assembly came in the 390s BC, so the poor could attend. — [Wikipedia, Ecclesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ekklesia_(ancient_Athens))
- The kleroterion (allotment machine): bronze identification tickets (pinakia) were slotted in by tribe, and whole rows were accepted or rejected by a random process. One stood outside each court. Photo at Agora Museum. — [Commons, File:AGMA Kleroterion.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:AGMA_Kleroterion.jpg); category of 8 files: [Commons category](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Kleroterion_in_the_Ancient_Agora_Museum_(Athens))
- Euergetism: the trierarchy and choregia were compulsory liturgies and became voluntary prestige spending in the Hellenistic period. In 284/83 BC the poet Philippides, elected agonothetes, was not reimbursed by the city for his personal outlay. Benefactors paid for theatres, odeia, gymnasia, fountains and markets. By the mid-2nd century BC it was formal institutionalised civic patronage. — [Wikipedia, Euergetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euergetism)
- Proxeny/honorific decree structure: enacting body (council and people), proposer, honorand with patronymic, reason, the award, and sometimes publication instructions. Standard formula: "it was resolved by the council and the people" (ἔδοξεν τῇ βουλῇ καὶ τῷ δήμῳ). Texts were highly formulaic. Typical benefits included display of the stele in a temple. — [Search results incl. Bonanno 2017, Malta](https://classicsmalta.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Bonanno-2017-A-Friend-in-High-Places.pdf); [Wikipedia, Proxeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxeny)
- Cost of a stele at Delos (3rd c. BC): 25 dr stone, 1 dr 3 ob transport, 100 dr engraving (1 dr per 300 letters), 5 dr lead, 1 dr wood, 2 dr 3 ob erecting. Total about 135 dr. — [Search result summary of a Delos text, via Bonanno 2017 / related search](https://classicsmalta.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Bonanno-2017-A-Friend-in-High-Places.pdf); I did not verify the primary text.
- Magistrates: the agoranomos oversaw agora order, weights and measures, goods quality and sales. Astynomoi were mostly an Ionian office; at Athens there were ten, five in the Piraeus and five in the city. Sitonai supplied and regulated grain, meal and bread. — [Encyclopedia.com, Agoranomos](https://www.encyclopedia.com/religion/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/agoranomos); [Oxford Reference, Astynomoi](https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803095430833); [Harpokration](https://topostext.org/work/537)
- Ephebeia: Classical ages 18-20, two years of training. In the 3rd c. BC service ceased to be compulsory and shrank to one year. It became "a sort of university for well-to-do young men" with literary and philosophic study. The ephebic college was a "miniature city" with archon, strategos and herald. Names were engraved on a pillar before the council-chamber. Foreigners were admitted later. — [Wikipedia, Ephebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephebi)
- Delos sanctuary-linked civic life: independent of Athens to 167/166 BC, then an Athenian-administered free port. — [Wikipedia, Delos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delos)

### Inferences
- A small island polis likely had a council of perhaps tens of members and an assembly in the theatre or agora. [Inference; sizes not sourced.]
- A polis with a benefactor-dependent budget would hold honours (crowns, statues, front seats, stele) as its main currency, since decrees repeat the same formulae.
- Background, not sourced this session [verify]: prytaneis are the council's rotating presiding committee. Typical Hellenistic city offices are a board of strategoi, a secretary (grammateus) and treasurers (tamiai). Hellenistic gymnasiarchs were often benefactors who paid for oil. Ballots were bronze or stone psephoi, hollow or solid, dropped into urns. Many cities used show of hands for most decisions and secret ballot for honours, citizenship grants and judicial matters. Decrees often fix the crown's value and announce it at the Dionysia.

- No primary decree text retrieved for a small island (Paros, Tenos, Amorgos, Kos). Next step: PHI Greek Inscriptions (epigraphy.packhum.org) and Attalus [royal correspondence index](https://www.attalus.org/docs/rc/index.html).
- Citizenship rules, prytany mechanics, gymnasiarch duties, voting-token types were not sourced. Try Commons categories "Psephoi" and "Voting ballots".

## 2. Hellenistic kingdoms and leagues

### Takeaway
Kingdoms ruled cities through reciprocal "letter and decree" diplomacy, not direct administration, except in Egypt, where the king ran a bureaucratic estate. Leagues gave small states collective power while keeping local autonomy.

### Cited Findings
- Ptolemaic: bureaucracy exploited Egypt for a Greek ruling class. Greeks lived under Greek law and courts. Egyptians kept local and religious institutions and rose only gradually if Hellenised. Under Rome the Ptolemaic system persisted and Greek stayed the language of government. — [Wikipedia, Ptolemaic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom)
- Ptolemaic nomes: more than 30 nomes, each with a Greek strategos (originally garrison commander, then administrator), above whom stood the epistrategos. The oikonomos (nome steward) auctioned tax-farming rights using censuses and cadasters. The tax-farmer paid monthly into the royal bank and reviewed accounts monthly with the oikonomos, who appointed an antigrapheus controller. A temple-epistates monitored temple finances. — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ptolemaic-dynasty/Administration-and-economy); [Bevan, House of Ptolemy](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Africa/Egypt/_Texts/BEVHOP/5C*.html); [Epistrategos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistrategos)
- Seleucid: Greek customs and language privileged, many Greek settlements founded, local traditions tolerated. Capital moved Babylon, Seleucia on the Tigris, then Antioch. — [Wikipedia, Seleucid Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seleucid_Empire)
- King-city relations (Ma): documents are royal letters and civic decrees. The relationship is reciprocity, expressed in conventional language ("contract clauses"). The king was celebrated as liberator and protector and honoured with altars where magistrates sacrificed. Examples: decrees of Iasos, Sardis, Amyzon; letters of Antiochos III and his official Zeuxis. Iasos decree (196-194 BC) set up cult honours for Antiochos III and Laodike. — [BMCR review of Ma](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2001/2001.02.15); [PHRC043 Iasos](http://s.phrc.it/phrc043)
- Nesiotic League (Islanders): founded c. 314/3 BC under Antigonos Monophthalmos. Antigonid c. 314-287, Ptolemaic c. 287-mid-3rd c., a second league c. 200-167 under Rhodes. Members include Mykonos, Kythnos, Keos, Ios, Naxos, Andros, Amorgos, Paros. Delos was the ceremonial centre. The synedrion of representatives met annually at Delos (at Tenos under Rhodes). The nesiarch was appointed by the suzerain. A treasurer (tamias) managed syntaxeis (contributions) and the league often borrowed from Apollo's Delian sanctuary. Named nesiarchs: Apollodoros, Bakchon (c. 279-276), Hermias (267). — [Wikipedia, Nesiotic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nesiotic_League)
- Achaean League: strategos elected annually (one only from 251 BC), no consecutive terms. Synodos four times a year at Aegium for all male citizens of member communities. Boule open to men over 30. Board of ten damiourgoi. Refounded 280 BC by Dyme, Patrae, Pharae, Tritaea. Dissolved by Rome 146 BC. — [Wikipedia, Achaean League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaean_League)
- Rhodes: maritime commercial centre with coins circulating nearly everywhere, balance-of-power policy, mainland Peraia, appealed to Rome 201 BC, formal alliance 164 BC with reduced freedom. — [Wikipedia, Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes)
- Roman timeline: 197 Cynoscephalae, 191 Thermopylae, 168 Pydna, 146 Corinth and Macedon abolished. Aetolian and Achaean Leagues were governed by councils of city representatives and assemblies. — [Wikipedia, Hellenistic Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Greece)

### Inferences
- An island polis in the Antigonid/Ptolemaic period had a protector king, whose officials (nesiarch, garrison commander, envoys) were the visible administrators. The city's own officials handled internal affairs.
- Background [unsourced, verify]: Attalid Pergamon ran through royal letters to cities and a royal bureaucracy. Antigonid Macedon had a looser army-and-assembly monarchy with garrisons ("Fetters of Greece": Chalkis, Corinth, Demetrias). Aetolian League federal officials were a strategos, hipparch and synedrion (the Panaitolika assembly). Rhodian magistrates included prytaneis rotating every six months and a nauarch, and the navy was state-run.

### Gaps
- Attalid and Antigonid administration details not sourced here. Aetolian League details not retrieved. Rhodian internal constitution not sourced (the Wikipedia page lacked it).
- Ptolemaic "basilikos grammateus", royal banks and monopolies (oil, beer) not confirmed beyond tax-farming above.

## 3. Law, courts, interstate relations, contracts

### Takeaway
Cities settled disputes by arbitration among third-party cities, secured sanctuaries with asylia recognitions, and merged via sympoliteia. Records were inscribed or kept in archives (not retrieved).

### Cited Findings
- Hellenistic temples and cities were declared "sacred and inviolable" (asylia). Documented in Royal Correspondence for Kos (RC 25-28) and Teos (RC 35), and for Magnesia. Sympoliteia: Teos with Kyrbissos (3rd c.) and Magnesia with Smyrna (probably 245 BC). Isopoliteia, asylia, sympoliteia and arbitration were linked interstate tools. — [Attalus topics](https://www.attalus.org/docs/topics.html); [Attalus concordance](https://www.attalus.org/docs/inscriptions4.html); [JSTOR, Asylia (Rigsby)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.5973089)
- Ptolemaic Greeks were tried in Greek courts. — [Wikipedia, Ptolemaic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom)
- Delphic manumission records: roughly 1,300 inscriptions, mostly from 200 BC to 100 AD. A slave was fictitiously sold to Apollo, witnesses and priests are named, and many included a paramone (stay with the master for a fixed time or until his death). Over 60% concern women, and women's prices were about 20% lower. The practice began with Aetolian owners and spread to Locris and Phocis. — [Wikipedia, Delphic manumissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manumission_inscriptions_at_Delphi)

### Inferences
- Background [unsourced, verify]: Foreign judges (dikastai) were often invited from another city. Their visits were honoured by decree, which preserves them in inscriptions. Contracts were typically written by a notary or archive official and deposited in the archeion. Loans were secured on property with inscribed horoi (mortgage stones).

### Gaps
- No sourced details of dikasteria in small cities, archeia, notaries, or specific arbitration cases (e.g., Miletos-Magnesia, Kos-Halasarna). Try Austin and Attalus.

## 4. Finance and economy

### Takeaway
Price and wage evidence is patchy and mostly Classical or Egyptian. Use ranges with explicit uncertainty.

### Cited Findings
- Grain: a medimnos of wheat typically 5-6 dr and barley 3-5 dr in the cited summary. There was a great inter-annual range of wheat prices on Delos. A 3rd-century text gave 4 dr for a medimnos and 4 dr for a wine measure. — [Search result summary (Cambridge, Soldiers, Wages and the Hellenistic Economies ch. 4)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/soldiers-wages-and-the-hellenistic-economies/forms-of-remuneration-and-standards-of-living/F8CE687BE760DAAE4717F6BC84FEE3FA); [Coinweek](https://coinweek.com/prices-and-coins-in-the-ancient-world/)
- Wages: 329 BC Eleusis unskilled 1.5 dr/day, skilled 2.25 dr/day. An entry dated 257 BC gives a worker 1 obol/day, buying 2 choinikes of wheat. This is probably Ptolemaic Egypt, not an Aegean polis (uncertain). — same sources.
- The search summary quoted military pay of 50, 20 and 10 dr per day for cavalry, hoplites and light troops. This is implausibly high for a daily wage (probably total or ceremonial payments, or garbled). Do not use it.
- Delos: free port from 167/166 BC under Athens, the Mediterranean's largest slave market (tens of thousands of slaves), with Italian merchant guilds and Berytian Poseidoniasts (shipmasters, warehousemen) with a clubhouse. Mithridates' attacks in 88 and 69 BC ended its prosperity. — [Wikipedia, Delos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delos)
- Ptolemaic taxes were farmed out and paid into royal banks. — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Ptolemaic-dynasty/Administration-and-economy)
- Rhodian coins circulated across the Mediterranean. Antiochos III tetradrachm shown on the Hellenistic Greece page. — [Wikipedia, Rhodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes); [Wikipedia, Hellenistic Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Greece)
- Nesiotic League financed itself with contributions and loans from the Delian Apollo sanctuary. — [Wikipedia, Nesiotic League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nesiotic_League)

### Inferences
- Background [unsourced, verify]: Standard relationship 1 drachma = 6 obols, 100 drachmas = 1 mina, 60 minas = 1 talent. Hellenistic cities often struck bronze for local use and silver for regional trade. Trapezitai (bankers) took deposits and changed coin at a fee. Harbour dues commonly ran at about 2% (e.g., Rhodes, 2% pentekoste/eikoste variants). Pirates (Aetolian, Cretan, later Cilician) made ransom and kidnapping a routine risk, and cities negotiated redemptions and honoured ransomers. Treat all these as to be verified.
- A budget for a stele of about 135 dr, set against a wage of 1-2 dr/day, means a public stele costs roughly 70-130 labour days.

### Gaps
- Piracy page fetch failed (404). No sourced piracy, ransom, harbour dues, debt law, weights and measures, or coin-type material. The Delos accounts (ID inscriptions) and Rhodes sources should supply these.
- Price list for slaves outside Delphi, and Aegean wage lists, not found.

## 5. Religion, associations, education, language, communication

### Takeaway
Little sourced this session beyond the Delos associations and ephebeia. Remaining items need further research.

### Cited Findings
- Delos associations: Italian merchant guilds set up monuments in the marketplace, and the Berytian Poseidoniasts had a clubhouse honouring Phoenician gods and Roma. Samaritan and Jewish communities are attested by inscriptions. — [Wikipedia, Delos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delos)
- Ephebeia as civic school, see section 1. — [Wikipedia, Ephebi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephebi)
- Greek remained the language of Ptolemaic government. — [Wikipedia, Ptolemaic Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_Kingdom)

### Inferences
- Background [unsourced, verify]: Koine is the shared Greek. Writing materials were papyrus in Egypt, wax tablets and lead sheets across the Aegean, and stone and bronze for public records. Public decrees were announced by heralds and carried by envoys (theoroi/presbeis) to other cities. Festivals tied to Dionysia, Delia, Thesmophoria were the main calendar, and sanctuary treasures were administered by boards (hieropoioi, naopoioi) and inventoried on stone.

### Gaps
- No sourced material on sanctuary finance, oracle consultation procedure, thiasoi/koina rules, literacy rates, or messenger practice.
