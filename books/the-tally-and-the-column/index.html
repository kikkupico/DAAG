The Tally and the Column

Being an account of how the trading houses of Arche came to agree on the order of their business without any of them possessing a clock

In which a number that only ever rises is found sufficient to settle every dispute about what came first, and insufficient to answer a different question that nobody had thought to ask.

Chapter I

The Harbour Under Cloud

The harbour town of Arche sits on the island's south-facing shore, and its business is carried on by some dozens of trading houses, each keeping its own books in its own counting room. Three of them will do for everything that follows: the house of the Dolphin, which stands nearest the mole; the house of the Vine, halfway along the quay; and the house of the Anchor, at the eastern end where the warehouses begin. Each has its sign cut over the door, and each keeps a running book of what it has bought and sold.

The houses do not sit in one room. A clerk of the Dolphin who wants the Anchor to know something writes it on a slip, and a boy carries the slip along the quay. This takes as long as it takes. The boy may be quick or he may stop to watch a ship come in, and there is no arrangement by which the Dolphin can discover which. What the Dolphin knows is what is written in its own book and what has been carried through its own door. It knows nothing else about the world, and in particular it does not know what the Anchor is doing at this moment, because “at this moment” is not a thing the quay provides.

There were dials once, and there still are: a row of them on the open headland above the town, where nothing shades them. They are good dials. The difficulty is the weather. Arche's summer is clear, but its trading season is not, and under a grey sky a dial does not run slow — it stops saying anything at all. A clerk who has built his practice on reading the headland finds, four mornings in ten, that there is nothing to read. So the houses may not settle their affairs by the hour, because on the mornings that matter most there is no hour.

The trouble this causes has a shape, and the shape is always the same. A cargo of Pontic grain is lying in the roads, and two houses want it. The Vine writes to the Dolphin offering for it. The Anchor, knowing nothing of this, writes to the Dolphin offering for it as well. Two boys are on the quay at once. Whichever reaches the Dolphin's door first is the offer the Dolphin sees first — and if the Dolphin also wrote to the Vine that morning, then the Vine and the Anchor may each end the day holding a perfectly correct account of the morning's business, and the two accounts may disagree about which offer came first.

Nobody has lied and nobody has blundered. The two houses simply saw the morning in two orders, and there is no third party with a clock who can say which order was the real one. This book is about what the houses did instead.

Chapter II

What Happened Before

Lamportios, who kept the books of the Dolphin and was by temperament a man who took problems apart before trying to solve them, began by noticing that the houses were asking for something they did not need. They had been asking which of two things happened first. What their business actually required was narrower: they needed to know when one thing could have been the cause of another, because those are the only pairs where the order is worth arguing about.

He set down three rules, and they are the whole of it. One thing came before another when:

The three rules of coming-before
  1. They happened in the same house, and the book of that house has the first one written above the second.
  2. The first is a house writing a slip, and the second is another house taking that same slip through its door.
  3. Or there is some third thing that the first came before, and which came before the second.

That is all. If none of the three can be made to apply — if you cannot walk from one event to the other by going down a house's book and along the slips — then neither came before the other, and Lamportios called such a pair unrelated. He was insistent on the point that unrelated does not mean simultaneous. It means that nothing either house did could have reached the other in time to matter, and therefore that the question of which was first has no answer the quay can supply, and needs none.

Read the picture the way a clerk reads it. You may travel downward along any house's line, and you may travel along any arrow in the direction it points, and you may change from one to the other as often as you like. Anything you can reach from an event came after it. Anything from which you can reach it came before. Everything else is unrelated, and there is a great deal of everything else.

Stated formally Coming-before is the relation a → b on events, defined as the smallest relation such that (i) if a and b are in the same process and a precedes b, then a → b; (ii) if a is the sending of a message and b its receipt, then a → b; (iii) it is transitive. Events with neither a → b nor b → a are concurrent.

One property is worth pinning down before anything is built on it, because everything is built on it: no event comes before itself.

Proof — that nothing comes before itself
  1. Suppose some event a had a → a. Then by the third rule there is a chain of steps from a back to a, each step being either a move down one house's book or a move along one slip.
  2. Give every event a physical instant at which it really occurred — not one any clerk can read, but one that exists. Moving down a house's book strictly increases that instant, since a house's own events are written in the order they happened.
  3. Moving along a slip also strictly increases it: a slip cannot be taken through a door before it was written, because the boy carries it, and the boy takes a positive time to walk.
  4. So every step of the chain strictly increases the instant, and the chain returns to its start. That would require the instant of a to be strictly greater than itself.
  5. Hence no such chain exists. Coming-before is irreflexive, and being transitive by construction, it is a strict partial order.∎

The word partial is the one that earns its keep. A partial order is an order with holes in it, and the holes are not a defect to be repaired. They are the true report of a harbour in which two houses can act without either knowing of the other.

Chapter III

The Rule of the Rising Number

Having decided what he wanted, Lamportios built something small enough to keep in a counting room. Every house is to keep one number. It is not an hour and it is not a date; it is a tally, and its only property is that it rises.

The rule of the tally house
  1. Before writing anything in your own book — a purchase, a sale, a slip sent — advance your tally by one, and write that number beside the entry.
  2. Every slip carries the tally it was written under. When a slip comes through your door, look at the number on it. Set your own tally to whichever is greater, yours or the slip's, and then advance it by one. Write that number beside the taking-in.

Two rules, no hours, no headland. A boy delivering a slip carries with it everything the receiving house needs to place it. The Dolphin's clerk does not need to know when the Anchor wrote; he needs only to be certain that his own book will not afterwards claim the Anchor wrote later than he read it. The second rule buys exactly that, and nothing more.

What it buys is worth stating plainly, because it is the property the whole harbour depends on: if one thing came before another, the second carries the higher number.

What the tally guarantees

Take any two entries anywhere in the harbour's books. If you can walk from the first to the second — down the book-lines, along the slips — then the number written beside the second is strictly greater than the number beside the first.

And take note of what is not claimed. Nothing whatever is said about two entries you cannot walk between. Their numbers may run either way, or be equal, and the fact tells you nothing at all.

Proof — that the numbers respect coming-before
  1. It suffices to prove it for a single step, since coming-before is the transitive closure of single steps, and a chain of strict increases is a strict increase.
  2. Step within one house. If a and b are consecutive entries in one book, the first rule advanced the tally by one between them. So C(b) = C(a) + 1 > C(a).
  3. Step along a slip. Let a be the writing of a slip, carrying the number T = C(a), and let b be its taking-in. By the second rule the receiving house set its tally to at least T and then advanced by one, so C(b) ≥ T + 1 > T = C(a).
  4. Both kinds of step strictly increase the number, so every walk does. ∎

Now the same morning, with the numbers written in.

This is the crack in the arrangement, and it is worth dwelling on because it is not a fault in the rule. The rule promised that walking uphill means rising numbers. It did not promise the reverse, and the reverse is false: a rising number does not mean a walk. The Dolphin's second entry carries 2, the Anchor's third carries 3, and there is no path between them in either direction. The numbers have manufactured an order where the harbour had none.

For most of the houses' business this manufacturing is harmless and even welcome, as the next chapter shows. It becomes a real loss only when someone wants to know whether two entries were made in ignorance of each other — and that question waits until Chapter VII.

Chapter IV

The Order of the Signs

A partial order will not run a market. When two houses have offered for the same grain, somebody must be served first, and “their offers were unrelated” is not an answer a shipmaster will accept. The houses needed a rule that always produces an answer, the same answer everywhere, without anybody conferring.

The tally nearly does it. Where two entries carry different numbers, take the smaller to be first. The only gap is entries carrying equal numbers, and for those Lamportios took the crudest device available: the signs over the doors, in a fixed order agreed once and never revisited. The Anchor, the Dolphin, the Vine — alphabetical by sign, as good as anything and better than argument.

The rule of precedence
  1. The entry with the smaller tally is earlier.
  2. If the tallies are equal, the entry from the house whose sign comes first is earlier.

Now every pair of entries in the harbour stands in a definite order, and — this is the part that matters — every house computes the same order, from the numbers and the signs alone, with no conferring and no clock. The order is arbitrary where the harbour was silent. It is not arbitrary where the harbour spoke: it never contradicts a walk.

Proof — that precedence is a total order extending coming-before
  1. Any two entries are comparable. Either their tallies differ, and the first clause decides; or they are equal, and since two entries of the same house never carry the same tally (rule one advances it between them), the two entries belong to different houses, whose signs differ, and the second clause decides.
  2. No entry precedes itself, since it neither has a smaller tally than itself nor a sign earlier than its own.
  3. It is transitive. Compare by the pair (tally, sign). This is the lexicographic order on a pair drawn from two total orders, which is itself a total order.
  4. It never contradicts a walk. If a → b, then by the previous chapter C(a) < C(b) strictly, so the first clause already places a earlier and the signs are never consulted.∎

It is worth being honest about what has been done here. The harbour has not discovered the true order of its morning; there was no true order to discover. It has agreed on an order, chosen so that it never denies a real dependence, and it has arranged for every house to arrive at that same agreed order alone in its own counting room. That is a smaller thing than the truth and a far more useful one.

Chapter V

The Single Berth

The harbour has one deep berth on the mole where a heavy grain ship can lie alongside and discharge into carts. There is one of it. Two ships cannot use it at once, a house that has it keeps it until it is done, and the houses must settle among themselves who has it next — with no harbourmaster, since the harbourmaster is exactly the single point of failure they are trying to do without.

Lamportios's arrangement uses nothing but the precedence of the last chapter. Each house keeps a private waiting-list of requests, its own and others', kept in precedence order. Every house's list begins empty.

The rule of the berth
  1. To ask for the berth: write a request under your current tally, put it on your own list, and send a slip carrying it to every other house.
  2. On taking in a request: put it on your list, and send back an acknowledging slip.
  3. To give up the berth: take your own request off your list, and send a slip saying so to every other house.
  4. On taking in a release: take that house's request off your list.
  5. You may take the berth when both of these hold at once: your own request stands first on your list by precedence, and you have taken in from every other house some slip carrying a tally greater than your request's.

The fifth rule is the whole art, and its second half is the half that is always missed. It is not enough that your request stands first on the list you happen to hold. Your list is only what has reached you, and a request older than yours may be on the quay at this moment in a boy's hand. The second half is what closes that door: a slip from a house carrying a higher tally is that house's own testimony that it has moved past the moment your request was made, and therefore that it has nothing older still to send.

Two properties of the quay are being leaned on here, and both should be said out loud. The boys on any one route do not overtake one another, so slips between a given pair of houses arrive in the order they were written. And no slip is lost. Neither is a law of nature; both are arrangements, and the arrangement fails if they fail.

Proof — that two houses never hold the berth at once
  1. Suppose the Dolphin and the Anchor both take the berth, the Dolphin on a request RD and the Anchor on RA. Precedence is total, so one is earlier; say RD is.
  2. When the Anchor took the berth, clause five required that RA stood first on the Anchor's list, and that the Anchor had taken in from the Dolphin some slip carrying a tally greater than RA's.
  3. The Dolphin sent RD to every house, including the Anchor, and sent it before any slip carrying a higher tally — because the tally only rises, and RD was written under the tally it carries.
  4. Slips between the Dolphin and the Anchor do not overtake one another. So the Anchor had already taken in RD before it took in the higher-tallied slip of step 2, and RD was on the Anchor's list at that moment.
  5. But RD is earlier than RA by precedence, so RA did not stand first on the Anchor's list, and clause five was not satisfied. The Anchor did not take the berth — a contradiction.∎

Every house runs the same rules over the same agreed order and arrives at the same answer, and no house is in charge. The arrangement generalises past the berth to anything the houses must do in one agreed sequence — which is the observation that the harbour has stumbled on something larger than a mooring, though this book does not pursue it.

What it costs is worth naming. Every request speaks to every house and is answered by every house. The berth is not cheap. It is merely nobody's.

Chapter VI

The Order That Came Outside the Letters

The arrangement held for a season, and then it produced a result that everyone agreed was wrong and nobody could fault.

A factor of the Vine, standing on the quay, called across to a friend that he was about to lodge an offer for the Pontic grain, and went inside to write it. The friend, who was of the Anchor, walked twenty paces to his own counting room and lodged the Anchor's offer first. Both offers went out under their tallies. The Anchor's carried the smaller number, and by precedence the Anchor's offer was earlier, and the grain went to the Anchor. The Vine's factor had spoken first, in front of witnesses, and was ranked second.

No rule was broken. The shout was not a slip. It went from the Vine's factor to the Anchor's friend by a route the harbour's rules know nothing about, and so no walk in the books joins the two offers, and the tallies were free to fall as they fell. The order the houses agreed on is faithful to everything that passed through their doors, and the quay is not one of their doors.

The shape of the fault

The tally orders everything the system did. It cannot order anything that travelled by a route outside the system, because it never saw it. Where two people can pass word by some path the books do not record, the agreed order may contradict what those people know to have happened.

There are two remedies and the houses have used both. The first is to put the burden on the man who shouted: if the Vine's factor tells his friend the tally his offer will carry, the friend can lodge the Anchor's under a higher one. This works and it is not a system's answer; it is a courtesy.

The second is to go back to the headland. Not to abandon the tally — the tally does the ordering — but to derive it from dials good enough that a number is never lower than one belonging to an entry a man could physically have carried word from. That is a demand about real elapsed time, and it is the only place in this book where real time appears.

Stated formally The Strong Clock Condition: for events a, b of the wider world 𝒮, if a ⇒ b then C(a) < C(b), where ⇒ is the ordering of that wider world and is stronger than →. Logical clocks do not satisfy it. Physical clocks can.

Dials drift; that is the one thing every dial-keeper knows. The demand is not that they stop drifting but that their drift be bounded, and that the houses re-set them against one another often enough that they never wander further apart than the shortest time a boy can cross the quay. If the dials can never disagree by more than it takes to carry word, then a number derived from a dial can never rank a later act earlier.

|dCi(t)/dt − 1| < κ     |Ci(t) − Cj(t)| < ε     ε / (1 − κ) ≤ μ

The first says a dial runs at very nearly the true rate, off by at most a fraction κ. The second says no two dials in the harbour differ by more than ε. The third is the condition that removes the anomaly: μ being the shortest time any word can take to cross between houses, the dials' disagreement must be smaller than that crossing. Then the grain cannot be lost to a shout.

Proof — that re-setting keeps the dials close, and how close
  1. Each house sends a slip along every route it keeps at least every τ of elapsed time, and each slip carries the dial-reading at which it was written. Delays are unpredictable but bounded: no slip takes longer than its route's minimum by more than ξ.
  2. On taking in a slip, a house never sets its dial back — setting back would destroy the rising property everything else rests on — but sets it forward if the slip shows the sender was ahead, allowing for the crossing.
  3. Consider two houses joined by a route. Between re-settings, at most τ elapses, and in that time their readings can separate by at most 2κτ, since each may be off by κ in either direction. The unpredictable part of the crossing adds at most ξ.
  4. So neighbours stay within roughly 2κτ + ξ. Houses not directly joined are reached by relaying, and the error accumulates along the path.
  5. Taking d to be the greatest number of routes needed to get from any house to any other, the whole harbour stays within ε = d(2κτ + ξ), and does so from time t0 + τd onward — the time it takes the re-settings to propagate once through the harbour.
  6. The paper notes that the full argument is considerably harder than this sketch, and gives it in an appendix.∎

So the dials come back to the headland, and they come back diminished. They are no longer the authority on when anything happened. They are a device for keeping the tally honest against the one kind of message the harbour cannot see.

Chapter VII

The Column Room

The room next to the tally house is narrow and badly lit and was for years a place to keep rope. Phidias and Matternos, who came to Arche separately and some years apart and who arrived at the same arrangement without either knowing of the other — a fact the harbour has always found funnier than they did — had it cleared out for a different kind of book.

Their question was the one Chapter III left lying. The tally can tell a house that one entry came before another. It cannot tell a house that two entries were made in ignorance of each other, because every pair of entries gets numbers, and numbers always compare. A house asking “did the Anchor already know this when it acted?” gets an answer from the tally, and the answer may be a fabrication.

The remedy is not subtle. Instead of one number, keep one column for every house in the harbour.

The rule of the column room
  1. Each house keeps a row of figures, one column per house, all starting at nothing.
  2. Before writing in your own book, advance the figure in your own column by one. Write the whole row beside the entry.
  3. Every slip carries the whole row it was written under. On taking one in, set each of your columns to whichever is greater — yours or the slip's — then advance your own column by one, and write the row beside the taking-in.

A house's row is a claim about the whole harbour: this is how much of each house's business I have heard of. The Dolphin's own column counts what the Dolphin has done. The other columns count what has reached it, directly or by relay.

The test is mechanical and a clerk can do it in a moment. One row is earlier than another when it is nowhere greater and somewhere smaller. If each row is greater than the other in some column, the two entries are unrelated, and now the books say so instead of inventing a precedence.

And this time the promise runs both ways.

What the column guarantees

One entry came before another if and only if its row is nowhere greater and somewhere smaller. Two entries are unrelated if and only if each row exceeds the other in some column.

This is the thing the tally could not do. The tally never lied about a real dependence; it invented dependences that were not there. The column invents nothing.

Proof — that the rows detect unrelatedness exactly
  1. Write V(a) for the row beside entry a, and V(a)[k] for the figure in house k's column. Write V(a) ≤ V(b) to mean it is nowhere greater, and < to mean also somewhere smaller.
  2. First, an observation. For an entry a made by house k, the figure V(a)[k] is exactly the count of k's own entries up to and including a — because only k ever advances that column, and it advances it by one at each of its own entries.
  3. If a → b then V(a) < V(b). Each single step — down a book or along a slip — takes the maximum of the two rows and then advances one column, so no column ever decreases along a step, and the acting house's own column strictly increases. Chaining steps preserves both facts.
  4. Conversely, if V(a) < V(b) then a → b. Let a be made by house k. By the observation, V(a)[k] is the number of k's entries through a. Since V(b)[k] ≥ V(a)[k], the house that made b has heard of at least that many of k's entries — and a house's figure for k only ever rises by taking in a slip that carries word of k's entries, directly or relayed. Following those slips back gives a walk from a to b.
  5. Hence unrelatedness is detected exactly. a and b are unrelated exactly when neither V(a) < V(b) nor V(b) < V(a) — and since the rows are equal only for the same entry, that is exactly the case where each exceeds the other somewhere.∎

Nothing is free. The tally is one figure and a slip carries one figure, whatever the size of the harbour. A row is as long as there are houses, every slip carries the whole row, and a harbour that admits a new house must widen every book in the town. The tally house and the column room stand next door to each other because the houses use both: the tally to decide who is served first, the column to decide whether anybody knew.

Chapter VIII

What No Tally Can See

Everything in this book rests on an assumption that has not once been stated, and it is time to state it: every clerk inks honestly.

The rules of the tally house are rules about what a clerk does when a slip comes through the door. They say advance your number, take the greater, write it beside the entry. They do not — cannot — compel it. A clerk who writes a smaller number than the rule requires has not broken any mechanism, because there is no mechanism; there is a practice. And the harbour has no way to detect it. The whole apparatus of Chapters III to VII is a set of conclusions drawn from the books, and the books are what the clerks wrote.

This is not a hypothetical in Arche. The bandits who hold the crown of the mountain inland have had people in this town for as long as anyone can remember, and some unknown number of those inking tallies on the quay are among them. Nobody knows which. Nobody has ever known which. They are not newly arrived, there is no account of how they came, and nothing about the day's business on the quay would look any different if there were none of them or a dozen.

It should be said that they are not doing anything. The road inland is held by the soldiers besieging the mountain and has been for a long season, so whatever understanding once ran between the town and the crown does not run now. What remains in the market is not a plot but a condition: a certain number of the hands writing in the harbour's books are not bound by the harbour's practice, and the books cannot tell you whose.

What the assumption is worth

Everything proved in this book is proved for honest clerks. The tally respects coming-before if every clerk advances it as the rule says. The berth is held by one house at a time if every clerk sends what the rule says to send. The columns detect unrelatedness exactly if every column was written honestly.

Not one of these proofs survives a single clerk who writes what suits him. They do not weaken; they cease to apply. A tally is a claim, and this book has been an examination of what follows from the claims, not of whether they are true.

Lamportios knew this perfectly well and did not regard it as a defect in the arrangement, any more than a lock is defective for being openable by its own key. The problem of a harbour where the writing hands cannot be trusted is a different problem, with a different shape and a much higher price, and it is not settled on the quay. It is settled — as far as it is settled anywhere — inside a wall on the crown of the mountain, within sight of this town and quite out of its reach.

In the Book, In the Paper

So that a reader may check the allegory rather than trust it.

The ideas, plainly

In a system with no shared clock, the only ordering that can be established is the one generated by local sequence and message-passing: the happened-before partial order. It is partial because two processes can act without either learning of the other, and that is a fact about the system rather than a gap in our knowledge of it.

A logical clock — one counter per process, incremented before each event and advanced past any timestamp it receives — guarantees that if one event happened before another then it carries the smaller number. The converse fails, and the failure is the whole subtlety: a smaller number does not mean an earlier event. Breaking ties with an arbitrary but fixed process order turns this into a total order that every process computes identically, which is enough to run distributed mutual exclusion with no coordinator — at a cost of messages to and from every participant.

Because logical clocks see only messages, information travelling outside the system can make the agreed order contradict reality. Removing that requires physical clocks whose drift is bounded and whose mutual disagreement is kept smaller than the minimum message delay.

Replacing the single counter with one counter per process — a vector clock — yields a test that characterises happened-before exactly in both directions, so concurrency can be detected rather than merely not-asserted. The price is message size linear in the number of participants.
