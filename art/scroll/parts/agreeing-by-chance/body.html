Agreeing by Chance

No fixed plan can guarantee that computers agree when one of them may stop and a message may take any time at all. If a machine that cannot decide flips a coin, no two machines ever decide differently, and all of them decide in the end with probability one.

Four mercenaries at the foot of Mount Phyle must settle on one answer by raven. A man who is stuck tosses a coin. The four never commit to different answers, and they commit in the end, though on no night anyone can name.

After Michael Ben-Or, Another Advantage of Free Choice: Completely Asynchronous Agreement Protocols, 1983

First, in machine terms

A coin where reasoning gives out

A group of computers must all settle on the same one of two values, and one of them may stop without a word.

  • Messages take any time at all. Every message arrives, but no delay is too long to be possible.
  • No fixed protocol can promise it. One that acts only on what it has received cannot guarantee that the group ever decides.*
  • Ben-Or (1983): let a machine that cannot decide flip a private coin. No two machines ever decide differently, and every machine that has not stopped decides with probability one, though no round can be named.

This edition tells it with four mercenaries, their ravens and a coin in each tent.

A split nothing breaks then a coin

The fixed rule is one example, used only to show the difficulty. The coins fall differently each time.

The foot of Mount Phyle

The same four tents

Four mercenaries camp at the foot of the hill, one to a tent, out of sight of one another, under nobody's command.* Bandits hold the top.

Only ravensThe four send word only by raven. No raven has a longest flight, and birds sent an hour apart arrive in either order.
Nobody liesNo man at the foot ever misreports anything.
Nobody is killedAny man may be, and a dead man's tent falls silent. No one is, in this book.
What the four want, and what bound them

Three demands, and one assumption

The four must all attack or all hold. Each formed an opinion at dusk, a sighting, and committing is final: a man leaves the tent and its birds for the night.

Nobody lies and nothing goes wrong, and still no arrangement guarantees that any of them ever commits. The birds can always be ordered so that the night stays open.*

First demand

No two commit differently

Nobody attacks while another holds.

Second demand

Matching sightings are followed

If all four sighted the same answer, that is the answer they commit to.

Third demand

Every man left alive commits

Every man who is not killed eventually commits.

The second and third ask more than the impossibility needs. The second says exactly how the answers stay possible, and the third asks committing of every man left alive. Even the weaker demands could not be guaranteed,* so these cannot be either.

The impossibility rested on one assumption, easy to pass over: the four may not draw lots. Each acts only on what has reached them, so that, given the same sighting and the same birds in the same order, each does the same thing every time.

What if that one assumption is dropped, and nothing else is changed? What could four men do with chance?

Dropping the assumption

A coin in each tent

Every man at the foot was paid in Attic silver, and every Attic coin has the goddess's head on one face and her owl on the other. The owl stands for holding and the goddess for attacking.

A man who cannot settle on an answer, by the rules that follow, tosses a coin high off the thumb, catches it, and favours the answer it shows.

There are four coins and four separate tosses. The four cannot toss one coin together, because nothing on the hill brings them to one place. One man tossing for all would have to send the result by raven, and that raven might be the one still in the pines, or the man might be the one who is killed. Nobody learns another's toss except by bird, if at all.

Four coins four tents

Over many tosses owl and goddess come up about equally often. In a short run they need not.

A silver coin tossed high and caught gives three things. A spun coin would not: it tends to fall to one face, and a struck coin is never quite even.

(1) Fair

Owl and goddess equally

Owl and goddess come up equally often.

(2) Fresh

No toss owes anything

Each toss owes nothing to any toss before it, in this tent or any other.

(3) Unforeseen

Unknown until made

Before a toss is made, no one knows how it will fall. After it is made, anyone may know it.

The third matters because of how the arguments of this book treat the birds. Without coins, the birds are taken in the worst order the argument can find: whatever the four arrange, the order of arrivals can be chosen against them.* The same is done here, and done harder. Think of the order in which the birds come down as arranged by someone who can see into every tent, read every strip and see every toss already made, and who arranges the arrivals to keep the four apart for as long as possible. What can this arranger not do? See a toss before it is made. The arranger is only the worst case, and no such person exists, but anything that holds against the arranger holds on every night.

A coin in each tent, then. But when does a man toss, and what does the toss do?

When a man tosses

Rounds of birds

The arrangement goes in numbered rounds, and every bird carries its round's number, so that a late bird of the second round is not taken for an early one of the third.

In each round each man sends two birds to each of the others, and counts their own two as though they had come. The first is the report: the answer favoured at the start of the round, beginning with the sighting. The second says one of two things: sure of an answer, or unsure.

Step one

Send the report

Send the answer favoured now, to all the others.

Step two

Wait for three reports

If all three name the same answer, more than half of all four, send sure of that answer. Otherwise send unsure.

Step three

Wait for three second birds

One that is sure of an answer: favour it from now on. Two or more: be settled on it. None: toss the coin and favour the answer it shows. Then the next round begins.

Three birds, not four

With four men and at most one silent, three birds always come. A man who waited for four would wait for ever whenever one of them had been killed, and could not know it.

One round at tent 1

Tent 1 has read three reports: its own and two ravens. Press a report to change what it says.

Then three second birds: its own, which the reports decide, and two ravens.

A settled man still has to commit, and that has a catch. A man who leaves the tent to attack or to hold falls as silent as a dead one, and the others are waiting for three birds each round. If two men committed and a third were killed, the last would wait for ever. So a settled man does one thing more: in the round of settling, at once, both birds of the next round go to all, the report and sure of that answer. This is the rule of parting. Then the man leaves the tent and commits.

The two birds sent early are exactly the two that would have been sent had the man stayed.

The round, stated formally

In the paper's terms the four tents are N = 4 processes, at most t = 1 of which may crash; a sighting is an initial value, a report is the message (1, r, x), sure of v is the D-message (2, r, v, D), unsure is (2, r, ?), being settled is deciding, and a process waits for N − t = 3 messages at each step. The three steps below are the paper's round, written for one process.

One round, r, at one tent.

  1. Send the report of round r, the answer favoured now, to all.
  2. Wait for three reports of round r. If more than two of them, that is all three, name the same answer, send sure of that answer to all; otherwise send unsure.
  3. Wait for three second birds of round r. Then: (a) if at least one says sure of an answer, favour that answer from now on; (b) if two or more say sure of it, be settled on it; (c) if none says sure, toss the coin and favour the answer it shows.
  4. Go on to round r + 1.

The same, for N men of whom t may fall silent. Wait each time for N − t birds. Send sure of v when more than N/2 of the reports received name v. Favour v on one sure of v; settle on v on more than t of them; toss otherwise. The arrangement needs N > 2t. On the hill, N = 4 and t = 1.

The rule of parting. A man who becomes settled on an answer in round r at once sends both his birds of round r + 1 to all: the report, that answer, and the second bird, sure of that answer. Then he leaves his tent and commits to it.

This rule is not in Ben-Or's paper. There a process that has decided simply goes on taking part, and nothing is said of stopping. On the hill deciding is committing, and committing is silence, so the rule is needed; the two birds it sends early are exactly the two the man would have sent had he stayed.

Each man now tosses whenever they cannot settle, alone and unseen. Could two coins falling differently commit two men to different answers?

Whatever the birds, whatever the coins

Never two answers

Two coins can never commit two men to different answers. The first two demands hold on every night. The whole of it rests on one small fact about majorities.

One sure answer a round

Every sure bird agrees

In any round, every sure bird names the same answer. Each man sends one report a round, so a round has at most four reports. Three of hold and three of attack out of four must share a man, and a man cannot have reported both.

Never two answers

No two commit differently

If one man is settled on an answer in a round, every man who finishes that round favours that answer, and every man not killed is settled on it in the next round.

The sightings respected

Matching sightings settle at once

If all four sighted the same answer, every man not killed is settled on it in the first round, and no coin is tossed.

Scroll to watch one round worked out by the rules, with each man missing one of the four birds.

1 · The reports

Tents 1, 2 and 4 favour hold

Tent 3 favours attack. Each man will read three of the four reports, and misses one.

2 · Who is sure

Two men read three alike

Tents 1 and 2 read three reports of hold, and write sure of hold. Tents 3 and 4 each read the attack among their three, and write unsure. A man cannot write sure of attack here, since that needs three of attack.

3 · The second birds

One man settles, the rest follow

Tent 1 reads two sure of hold and is settled. Tents 2, 3 and 4 each read one, and favour hold. A man misses at most one bird, so every man reads at least one when two were sent. Nobody tosses.

4 · Parting

Tent 1 has gone, and its birds are in

In the settling round tent 1 sent both birds of the next round: hold, and sure of hold. Those are the very birds it would have sent had it stayed.

5 · The next round

Every report names hold

Each remaining man reads three reports of hold, writes sure of hold, reads three such birds, and is settled on hold. No bird names attack.

6 · So

The coins can delay, never divide

A coin is tossed only by a man who read no sure bird at all, and while anyone is settled, no one is tossing against them. Chance does not come in anywhere in either result.

Proof · that once one man is settled, all end at his answer

In the paper's terms the four tents are N = 4 processes, at most t = 1 of which may crash; a sighting is an initial value, a report is the message (1, r, x), sure of v is the D-message (2, r, v, D), unsure is (2, r, ?), being settled is deciding, and a process waits for N − t = 3 messages at each step. Claim. If a process decides v in round r, then every process that completes round r adopts v, and every process that does not crash decides v in round r + 1. This is Theorem 1 (ii).

  1. Say Number 1 is settled on hold in round r. Then two or more of the three second birds that reached him said sure of hold, so at least two such birds were sent in round r.
  2. Any other man who finishes round r read three of the four second birds of that round, so he missed at most one. He therefore read at least one sure of hold. By one sure answer a round he read no sure of attack, so he favours hold: by rule (a) if he read one, and he may also be settled on hold by rule (b). He does not toss.
  3. So every report of round r + 1 names hold. Every man who reaches step (2) of that round sees three reports of hold and sends sure of hold; every man who reaches step (3) reads three sure of hold and is settled on hold.
  4. The birds a settled man sends early under the rule of parting are a report of hold and sure of hold. By step 3 these are exactly the birds he would have sent in round r + 1 had he stayed, whichever three reports he had read. So the rule changes nothing the others see, and each of them still gets three birds at every step, since at most one man is dead and the settled ones have sent theirs.
  5. No man is settled on attack in round r or any later round, since no sure of attack is ever sent from round r on. So no two men commit to different answers.∎
Proof · that four matching sightings settle the first round

In the paper's terms the four tents are N = 4 processes, at most t = 1 of which may crash; a sighting is an initial value, a report is the message (1, r, x), sure of v is the D-message (2, r, v, D), unsure is (2, r, ?), being settled is deciding, and a process waits for N − t = 3 messages at each step. Claim. If all N initial values are equal, every process that does not crash decides that value in round 1. This is Theorem 1 (iii).

  1. Every report of round 1 names that answer, so every man reads three reports of it and sends sure of it.
  2. Every man then reads three sure birds for it, more than one, and is settled on it. No coin is tossed.∎

The coins cannot divide the four. Can they bring them together, on a night when the sightings are split?

How the coin does its work

The toss

The coin does its work in the rounds where nobody is sure. Here is one way a night can go, with tent 4 silent from the start, so that every wait is for the three who remain.

The sightings were hold at tent 1, attack at tent 2 and hold at tent 3. The tosses below are the ones of this night in the story. The rounds are worked out by the rules.

One night press for the next round

The tosses did not have to agree with anybody's sighting. Tent 3 sighted hold and tossed the owl, tent 1 sighted hold and tossed the goddess. That is allowed, because the second demand speaks only of nights when all four sightings agree. And round 2 settled nothing, though two of the three favoured attack, because a man is sure only when more than half of all four agree, not more than half of those heard from. That keeps one sure answer a round true when a slow bird may be carrying the fourth report.

Nothing is to be read into which answer the coins chose. The coins pick an answer the way the birds' order would otherwise have picked it: by nothing anyone can know in advance. The night could as well have ended in attack.

That night ended in three rounds. Must every night end?

The end of the night

With probability one

Not on every course of birds and coins. The example could have taken thirty rounds, and there is a course of birds and coins, one among very many, in which it never ends: the tosses keep coming up split, round after round, for ever.

What can be promised is that such courses, taken together, have no chance whatever of happening. With at most one of the four silent, and however the birds are ordered, the chance that every man not killed eventually commits is one.

A fresh night every press is a new night

Choose what each tent saw at dusk. The birds here arrive in a random order, each man missing one of the other three, which is gentler than the arranger would be.

Press to run a night.

The coins keep giving the four a way out, and the birds cannot shut it for good. In any round at most one answer can be the subject of sure birds. If every man who tosses comes up with that answer, or, when there is none, all with the same answer, then every man starts the next round favouring one answer, and the next round settles everyone. The arranger can make that harder, round by round, but a way out that keeps coming back with some chance is taken in the end with chance one.

No night can be named by which the four will have committed. There is no round after which committing is certain, only a chance, growing towards one, that it has happened by then. No figure for how fast it grows is given here. A man who asks will we have committed by the fortieth round? can only be told almost certainly. And the courses that never end still exist. They have been given chance nothing, not abolished.

The short argument for this has a gap, said plainly in the panel below, and a full proof came much later.

Proof · a sketch, that the four commit with probability one

In the paper's terms the four tents are N = 4 processes, at most t = 1 of which may crash; a sighting is an initial value, a report is the message (1, r, x), sure of v is the D-message (2, r, v, D), unsure is (2, r, ?), being settled is deciding, and a process waits for N − t = 3 messages at each step. A local coin is step 3(c): set the value to 0 or 1 with probability 1/2 each. g is a value, defined in step 1 below. Claim. With N > 2t, every process that does not crash decides with probability 1. This is Theorem 1 (i), which the paper states without proof.

  1. Fix a round r in which no one is yet settled. Let g be the answer named by sure birds in round r, if any are sent, and let g be hold otherwise.
  2. Every man who finishes round r either reads a sure of g and favours g, or tosses. Each toss is fair and fresh, and the arranger cannot see it before it is made, so each comes up g with chance one half, whatever has gone before.
  3. At most four men toss in round r, so with chance at least 1/16 (the sketch's figure, not a proven bound) every one of them comes up g. Then every report of round r + 1 names g, and as in the proof of never two answers, every man who is not killed is settled on g in round r + 1.
  4. The rounds' tosses are fresh, so the chance that this fails in each of k rounds in a row is at most (15/16)k, which shrinks towards nothing as k grows. The chance that it fails for ever is therefore nothing.∎

Is the sketch airtight? No, and it should be said plainly where it fails. Step 1 treats the answer g as fixed before the round's tosses. But a slow man's report for round r comes from how his round r − 1 ended, and the arranger chooses which second birds he reads there, and may choose after seeing some tosses of round r. So the answer named sure in round r can be picked against the early tosses, and the one-in-sixteen of step 3 is not a bound anyone has proved. Ben-Or's paper states its theorems without any proof. A proof that closes every such gap was published only in 2012, by Aguilera and Toueg; it comes to the same conclusion by a longer road.

What is not claimed? No night can be named by which the four will have committed. There is no round after which committing is certain; there is only a chance, growing towards one, that it has happened by then. Ben-Or's paper gives no figure for how fast that chance grows, and this book gives none.

If endless courses still exist, what exactly did the coin change about the impossibility?

A fork the arranger does not steer

What the coin changed

The endless night without coins is built in three steps:* a way for the sightings to fall that leaves the night open; from any open standing, an arrival that can be deferred and taken without closing the night; and so an arranger who defers well can keep it open for ever.

Every step leans on the four being unable to draw lots. Given the same birds in the same order, each man does the same thing, so the arranger knows, before choosing an arrival, what it will lead to.

With coins

The arranger no longer knows

At a toss the night forks, one way for the owl and one for the goddess, and the coin takes the fork, not the arranger. The arranger can still be as clever about the birds, but cannot steer both forks at once.

Still there

Open standings and endless courses

Fix every toss in advance and the argument without coins runs unchanged.* What has changed is that an endless course now needs the coins to cooperate, round after round, and they do not.

What it bought

Every course, or probability one

No arrangement guarantees that anybody ever commits, and this one does not either. It guarantees that the four never commit differently, and that failing to commit has no chance at all. That difference is the whole of what the coin bought.

Three things would change matters at the foot of the hill: a longest flight, or some report of who has died, or all the deaths happening before the evening begins.* The coin is a fourth way, and a different kind. The other three give the four men more knowledge of the world. The coin gives them none. It gives the world less power over them.

The coin takes power from the world. What does it leave the world holding?

The limits of the coin

What chance does not buy

Could the coins carry the four past two silences? No. The arrangement needs the silent to be fewer than half, and that is not a weakness of the arrangement.

Half is too many. If half the men or more may fall silent, no arrangement, with or without coins, can meet all three demands: that no two commit to different answers, that matching sightings are followed, and that every man not killed eventually commits.

Proof · that with two of four silent, the answers can divide

In the paper's terms this is the case N ≤ 2t, here N = 4 and t = 2, a process being able to crash at any moment. Claim. No protocol, randomized or not, meets agreement, validity when all inputs are equal, and termination for every process that does not crash. This is the remark after Theorem 2: the schedule can simulate a partition.

  1. Suppose Numbers 1 and 2 sighted hold and Numbers 3 and 4 sighted attack, and take any arrangement that follows matching sightings and makes every man not killed commit eventually.
  2. Two men may be silent, so Numbers 1 and 2 must be able to commit without ever hearing from Number 3 or Number 4: for all they can tell, those two are dead. Keep every bird from Numbers 3 and 4 in the pines; Numbers 1 and 2, hearing only each other, commit. Whatever their coins show, what they see is exactly what they would see on a night when all four sighted hold and the other two were killed at dusk, and on that night the second demand has them hold. So they hold.
  3. In the same way keep back every bird from Numbers 1 and 2, and Numbers 3 and 4 attack.
  4. Do both at once, releasing the held birds only after all four have committed: all four are alive, every bird is merely late, and neither pair can tell. The pairs commit to different answers.∎

Four tents bear one silent man, and no more.

What about liars?

Six men against one liar

Nobody lies at the foot of the hill. For men who may lie as well as die there is a second arrangement of the same shape: each man must be able to tell whose bird they hold, and the liars must be fewer than one in five. Against one liar that is six men. The four bandits on the crown could not use it, and do not need to; they have their own methods.* Whether one in five is the best that can be done was not known.

Is it quick?

No

With many men and many possible silences, the tosses that must all come up alike are many too, and the expected number of rounds may grow very fast. One happier case: if the silent are few, no more than about the square root of the number of men, and the birds happen to come in step, the rounds needed on average do not grow at all. No arrangement without coins can do that, since those need at least one more round than there are men who may fail.

Does it choose a good answer?

Nothing says so

The coins say nothing about which answer is wiser. When the sightings agree, the four do what they sighted; when they do not, the answer is whichever the coins and the birds happen to reach. As always on the hill, nothing follows from it: no assault is told, and no one learns whether it was the right answer.

The paper behind this book

Michael Ben-Or, Another Advantage of Free Choice: Completely Asynchronous Agreement Protocols (extended abstract). Proceedings of the 2nd ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), Montreal, August 1983, 27–30. The randomized consensus protocol for crash failures with N > 2t (Theorem 1), the Byzantine protocol with N > 5t (Theorem 2), and constant expected rounds when t = O(√N) (Theorem 3), stated without proofs.

The ideas. Fischer, Lynch and Paterson proved that no deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system if even one process may crash: an adversary ordering the messages can always keep the outcome undecided. Their proof depends on determinism, since the adversary must know in advance what each step will lead to.

Ben-Or showed that randomness removes the obstacle. Processes run in rounds. In each round every process broadcasts its current value, waits for N − t values, and broadcasts a proposal marked D if a strict majority of all N processes hold the same value. It then waits for N − t proposals: it decides on more than t D-proposals for a value, adopts the value on at least one, and otherwise flips a private fair coin.

Safety holds on every run. Two strict majorities intersect, so only one value can be proposed with D in a round; a decision means more than t D-proposals exist, so every process that finishes the round sees one and adopts the value, and all decide it in the next round. Termination holds with probability 1 when N > 2t: once every correct process holds the same value, the next round decides, and the coin flips keep giving that a chance the adversary cannot remove. Ben-Or states this without proof; a complete proof, which must handle an adversary that sees earlier flips, is Aguilera and Toueg's (2012). Runs that never terminate still exist, but together they have probability zero.

The protocol does not contradict FLP; it changes a hypothesis. It needs a correct majority, which is necessary, and it may take many rounds. Later work replaced the private coins with a shared coin to make the expected number of rounds small.
