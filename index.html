Distributed Algorithms of Ancient Greece

An exploration of 31 papers in distributed computing employing the polarising ancient Greece allegory introduced by Leslie Lamport in his seminal paper, The Part-Time Parliament.

Arche · The Ring Road

Causality, State & Partial Synchrony

Book I · The Tally and the Column — In a distributed system, time as we know it ceases to make sense, because clocks are unreliable and local. An alternative way of tracking the order of events is explored as a problem of sorting purchase orders among three trading houses, linked by a cliff road on which runners cannot overtake one another. Along the way: logical clocks, mutual exclusion built on them, and the vector clocks needed to see what a single number cannot.
Lamport 1978 · Fidge 1988 · Mattern 1988

Book II · The Census of the Ring Road — A distributed system has no single instant at which its whole state can be read, and stopping it to look is rarely an option. How to record a consistent global snapshot while the system keeps running, and what such a snapshot can reliably tell you, is explored as a census of the island's goods taken without halting a single cart.
Chandy & Lamport 1985

Book III · The Convoy of Arche — Consensus cannot be guaranteed when messages may be delayed without bound, yet real networks are well-behaved most of the time. Partial synchrony, where a protocol stays safe through any amount of delay and decides once delays settle within a bound, is explored as three trading houses deciding whether their merchantmen should sail as one convoy while winter gales hold up their runners.
Dwork, Lynch & Stockmeyer 1988

The Auditor's Court Not yet written — When a shared object is spread over many machines, what should it mean for it to be correct? Linearizability requires every operation to appear to take effect at a single instant between its call and its return, so that anything finished before another began is seen in that order. It also composes: if every part keeps the rule, so does the whole. This is explored as a court outside the trading houses, which holds that once one trade has cleared before another is placed, no trader may ever be shown the second without the first.
Herlihy & Wing 1990

The Closed Road Not yet written — When a network splits, a replicated service must choose between answering every request and answering every request correctly; it cannot do both. The conjecture, and the proof that made it a theorem, are explored as a rockfall that closes the ring road between two houses. Each house must then either keep trading from a book it knows may be stale, or refuse to trade until the road reopens.
Brewer 2000 · Gilbert & Lynch 2002

Arche · Mount Phyle

Consensus & Faults

Book IV · Mercenaries and Bandits — Two limits on agreement. When messages are fully asynchronous, no protocol can guarantee consensus if even one process may crash, because a crashed process looks exactly like a slow one. When processes may lie, agreement is possible only if more than two thirds are honest, unless messages can be signed. Both are explored on one hill: mercenaries below who must go in at the same gate and cannot tell a dead comrade from a slow messenger bird, and bandits above who must hold the loot or scatter together and cannot tell a lying chief from a lying peer.
Fischer, Lynch & Paterson 1985 · Lamport, Shostak & Pease 1982 · Pease, Shostak & Lamport 1980

The Empty Row Not yet written — Consensus is impossible in an asynchronous system with a single crash, yet a failure detector that is often wrong can be enough to escape that result. It may suspect processes that are alive, provided it eventually suspects every crashed one and eventually stops suspecting some live one, and provided a majority never crash. This is explored in the same four mercenary camps, each keeping a board of which camps' ravens have stopped arriving, never sure whether an empty row means a dead commander or a bird still in the folds.
Chandra & Toueg 1996

The Standing Orders Not yet written — Replication can keep working even when some machines lie, the leader included, and even when messages take arbitrarily long. With 3f + 1 replicas for f liars, every step waits for a quorum of 2f + 1, so any two quorums share an honest member, and a leader that stalls or contradicts itself is replaced. This is explored with the same four bandits as the siege wears on for days. Their jars were started together only once, before the posts were first manned, and since no man may leave his post that meeting can never be repeated: the jars drift, and silence stops meaning anything. Still exactly 3f + 1 for a single liar, they keep one numbered sequence of orders by raven. The chief numbers each order, the guards echo it twice before it stands, and a chief whose numbering stalls or contradicts itself loses the job to the next man.
Castro & Liskov 1999

Paxos

Consensus & Replicated State Machines

The Part-Time Parliament Not yet written — A group of machines can agree on a sequence of commands, with no fixed leader, as long as any two majorities overlap. Whatever one majority has chosen, the next is bound to learn. The algorithm is always safe, but it is not guaranteed to finish: two proposers competing can block each other forever. This book is not retold. It is Lamport's paper in its original form, enhanced with visuals: the legislators of Paxos, their ballots and their ledgers, the allegory that every other book here borrows. Its plainer restatement, Paxos Made Simple, stands beside it, and later books return to its Chamber. The paper says that a foreign invasion destroyed Paxos, but for convenience we assume it didn't.
Lamport 1998 · Lamport 2001

The Worn Ledgers Not yet written — An algorithm that is correct on paper meets disks that corrupt data, logs that grow without end, and leaders that must answer reads without consulting anyone. Engineering Paxos into a production system is told back in Lamport's Chamber, as its ledgers wear out: the law book must be summarised, entries fade unread and must be found and recopied, and the president holds office for a fixed term so as to answer without calling a ballot.
Chandra, Griesemer & Redstone 2007

The Empty Counting House Not yet written — Letting every replica accept updates and reconcile them later looks cheap, but deadlocks and conflicts grow with the cube of the number of replicas. This argument for coordinated replication is explored as a counting house abandoned after splitting its decrees across independent regional councils, and kept empty as the assembly's reason to exist.
Gray, Helland, O'Neil & Shasha 1996

Skene

Leader-Driven Consensus

The Odeon Not yet written — Most practical replication puts one leader in charge, and the hard part is replacing it without losing anything it committed. Viewstamped replication numbers each leader's reign as a view, and changes view in a way that keeps every committed operation. It was developed independently of Paxos and published before it: a genuine alternative, not a descendant. So it is explored on an island of its own, Skene, which governs itself from a theatre. Where the Chamber on Paxos makes oratory impossible, the Odeon is built so that a single voice from the stage reaches every seat. When the speaker falls silent, the performance stops until someone else takes the stage under a higher number.
Oki & Liskov 1988 · Liskov & Cowling 2012

The Rehearsal Not yet written — A consensus algorithm that engineers cannot understand will be built wrong. Raft is leader-driven consensus in the same lineage as viewstamped replication, designed to be taught, and tested on students who had to learn it. It is told in the Odeon as the same performance rehearsed, and its refinements are stage directions: a random wait before claiming the stage, so that two rarely claim it at once, and no stage for a performer whose script is less complete.
Ongaro & Ousterhout 2014

Homonoia

Replication Without Coordination

The Stoa and the Festival Not yet written — Updates can spread through a large system the way rumours spread through a crowd: each node passes news to a few random others, and loses interest once most of those it tells have already heard. Periodic full comparisons catch what the rumours miss. Homonoia is an island of scholars and copyists. The traders of Arche cannot let their records disagree even for a moment, since a jar promised to two buyers is a real loss; the scholars can live with copies that differ for a while, as long as they agree in the end. This book, together with a survey of every optimistic replication practice in use, is explored as a colonnade where travelling scholars pass on a new finding, that the Earth goes round the Sun, each telling a few others until most of those they meet have already heard it, and a yearly festival where copyists collate whole texts line by line to catch whatever the talk missed.
Demers et al. 1987 · Saito & Shapiro 2005

The Ring of Libraries Not yet written — A store that must always accept writes can spread keys around a ring by hashing, let a neighbour stand in for a node that is down, and reconcile conflicting versions afterwards. How often such a store returns stale data can then be measured rather than guessed. This is explored as a ring of libraries around a lagoon, each holding the scrolls whose catalogue marks fall between it and its neighbour. When a library is closed, its copies are made next door with a note to return them, and a reader is sometimes handed an out-of-date copy.
DeCandia et al. 2007 · Bailis et al. 2012

The Wax Scriptorium Not yet written — A replica that is often disconnected can still accept updates, if each update carries its own check for conflicts and its own way of resolving them, and stays tentative until a primary fixes its place in the order. This is explored as a scriptorium on the beach where scribes draft their changes in wax, used at once but only provisionally, and copied fair in ink only when the head library has fixed their order.
Terry et al. 1995

The Far Terraces Not yet written — Some data types can be merged in any order, any number of times, and every replica still ends in the same state, so no coordination is ever needed. This is explored as the island's count of how many copies of each work exist, kept in voting pebbles on remote terraces. Each library adds pebbles only to its own column, and merging two counts takes the larger in each column.
Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero & Zawirski 2011 · the same authors' comprehensive study, 2011

The Sorting House Not yet written — A system spread across data centres can promise that no one sees a reply before the message it answers, without coordinating on every write, by having each write name the writes it depends on. This is explored through the scholars' letters: a sorting house that holds each letter back until every letter it answers has arrived.
Lloyd et al. 2011

The Reading Room Not yet written — Some transactional guarantees can be kept while the network is split, and others provably cannot. Which is which is explored as a library's reading room, asking what a librarian can still promise a reader while the couriers are stopped. A reader can be promised never to see half of a revision; no one can be promised that two readers will not both take the last copy.
Bailis et al. 2014

The Council Ground

When Coordination Is Needed

The Hall of Two Doors Not yet written — A program needs coordination exactly when it is not monotonic, that is, when a conclusion may have to be withdrawn as more information arrives. A question like "does the island hold at least one copy of this work?" can be answered as soon as it becomes true; "is this every copy there is?" cannot be answered without everyone. This is explored as a hall with one door facing the islands that assemble, Paxos and Skene, and one facing Homonoia, where each decree leaves by the door its own shape decides.
Hellerstein 2010 · Ameloot, Neven & Van den Bussche 2011
