Keeping Order Among Liars

Can a replicated service keep its commands in order when some of its machines lie, the leader included, and messages take arbitrarily long?

On the crown, the four bandits keep a book of the loot, one copy at each post. One of them may lie, and on these nights a wind can pin the ravens down for as long as it blows. The book's entries must stand in the same order in every honest copy, whatever the wind and whoever lies, and must go on standing once the wind drops. A job that passes from man to man, and two rounds of sealed echoes, are enough.

Chapter I

The Loot Book

On the crown of Mount Phyle, Number 1, the chief, holds the peak with the loot, and Numbers 2, 3 and 4 hold posts in the crags below, none in sight of another. Word goes only by raven, under seal rings that no one can counterfeit*. Some of them may lie. With four men, this book allows one.

These are later nights of the siege. The sandglasses were turned together once, on the first night, and never again*. On these nights a wind can blow over the peak and pin the ravens down for as long as it blows; when it drops, flights are short again*. Nobody can tell when it has dropped.

The four keep a loot book, one copy at each post: where each strongbox lies, and whose share it is. The book grows by entries, each a record about the loot, the third strongbox lies in the north cleft or the silver cups are Number 3's share. Any man may file one. An entry's effect depends on what the book already says, so every copy must take the same entries in the same order.

What the four owe each other Order. Every honest copy takes the same entries in the same order, and the book behaves as though one keeper wrote every entry at a single moment between its filing and its standing*.
Going on. Every entry an honest man files stands in the end, once the wind has dropped for long enough.
Safety: the service is linearizable. Liveness: clients eventually receive replies, provided at most ⌊(n − 1)/3⌋ replicas are faulty and delay(t) does not grow faster than t indefinitely. §2–3.

Order must hold in any wind, since nobody can see the wind drop. Going on cannot be promised while the wind blows: if four men could promise to agree on anything with the birds pinned for any time, the mercenaries at the foot could agree on a gate*.

Chapter II

The Chief's Job

Entries need numbers, so that every copy can take them in one order. Numbering is a job, held by one man at a time. The chief is a job, not a rank*. It starts with Number 1, the chief on the peak, and when it passes, it passes in number order: 1, 2, 3, 4, then 1 again. Each spell in the job is a term, and terms are counted, so the count says who holds the job.

A man who wants an entry seals it and sends it to the holder. The holder gives it the next number. What the others do next is the subject of the next chapter; at the end of it, every honest man writes the entry into his copy, in number order, and sends the man who filed it a sealed word that it stands.

Anyone may hold the job, including the liar. So nothing the holder says is trusted on its own. He may give two entries the same number, give one entry to some men and another to others, or simply sit on an entry and number nothing. Everything below is built against that.

How a man knows his entry stands. The paper's rule for someone who files and is not one of the keepers is to wait for two matching sealed words, from different men: with one liar, two matching words include an honest one. For the four themselves the rule is not needed. An honest man keeps a copy; when the entry stands in his own copy, he knows it stands. The rule is what a filer from outside the four would need, and there is none.

Chapter III

Two Echoes

An entry stands only after the holder has numbered it and the men have echoed it twice. Every bird in this chapter is sealed, and every man keeps every sealed bird he takes in.

An entry, from numbering to standing Numbering. The holder sends every man in this term, entry 7 is X.
First echo. A man accepts the numbering if it is from this term's holder, he has not accepted a different entry for number 7 in this term, and 7 is within the numbers now allowed (Chapter VII). He sends every man in this term, I heard entry 7 is X. When he holds the numbering and two matching first echoes from men other than the holder, his own possibly among them, three seals on the same entry and number, the entry is readied at his post.
Second echo. Once it is readied, he sends every man in this term, entry 7 is X is readied here. When he holds three matching second echoes, his own among them, the entry stands at his post.
Carrying out. He writes a standing entry into his copy as soon as every lower number has been written, and sends the filer word that it stands.

The two echoes do different work. The first settles order within a term: once an entry is readied anywhere honest, no honest man can ready a different entry under the same number in that term, even if the holder lies. The second settles it across terms: once an entry stands at an honest post, enough honest men have it readied that any later holder will be made to keep it. The next two chapters show why.

Birds may come in any order, so a man may find entry 9 standing before entry 8. He keeps it and waits; he writes nothing past a gap.

Chapter IV

Why Three Seals, and Four Men

Any two sets of three men, out of four, share two men. At most one of those two lies, so they share an honest man. That is the whole trick.

Suppose a lying holder tells two men that entry 7 is X and the others that it is Y. For X to be readied at an honest post, three men sealed X for number 7 in this term. For Y to be readied at an honest post, three men sealed Y. The two threes share an honest man, and an honest man seals only one entry for a number in a term.

One Entry to a Number

In any term, no two different entries are readied under the same number at honest posts.

Proof — that the three seals of readiness cannot be split
  1. An entry readied at a post carries the seals of three different men for it, under that number and term: the holder's numbering and two first echoes.
  2. If X and Y were both readied for number 7 in one term, the two sets of three would share at least two men among four, and at least one of them is honest.
  3. That honest man sealed both X and Y for number 7 in the same term. But an honest man accepts only one numbering for a number in a term, and seals only what he accepted. Contradiction. His seal cannot be counterfeited, so the liar cannot have made it for him.∎

Why four men. A man can never wait to hear from everyone, since one may be a liar who keeps quiet. So he acts on hearing from all but one: three. But the one he did not hear from may be an honest man whose bird is pinned, and then the liar may be among the three he did hear. The honest must still outnumber the liars among those three: two honest against one liar. That needs four men for one liar, and in general more than three times as many men as may lie*. Seals do not lower it. They stop a liar forging another man's word, not answering promptly with his own.

Chapter V

Changing the Job

If the holder sits on entries, or numbers them two ways, nothing stands, and the job must pass. Nobody can tell a holder who stalls from one whose birds are pinned by the wind. So the men do not judge. Each simply stops waiting.

A man who has an entry filed that has not yet stood turns his glass. If it runs out first, he asks for a change: he stops taking part in this term and sends every man a sealed request for the next term, carrying the proof of every entry readied at his post, each a numbering and two first echoes.

Changing the job (1) The next holder waits for requests for the new term from three men, his own among them.
(2) He sends every man the three requests and a new numbering. For each number, if some request shows an entry readied there, he numbers the one readied in the latest term; if none does, he numbers a blank entry, which does nothing.
(3) Each man checks that the requests are sealed and that the new numbering is what they call for, then echoes it as in Chapter III, in the new term.
§4.4: view-change with the set P of prepared certificates; new-view with 2f + 1 view-changes and the set O of pre-prepares; null requests fill gaps.

Suppose an entry X stands at some honest post as number 7. Then three men sent second echoes for it, of whom at least two are honest, and X is readied at those two. The new holder must show three requests. Two honest men who have X readied, and three requesters, among four men: they share at least one, and he is honest. His request carries X readied under number 7. Nobody can have readied a different entry under 7 in a later term without the same argument applying, so X is the one readied in the latest term, and the new holder must number X again under 7.

Order Across Terms

If an entry stands under a number at any honest post, every honest post that ever has an entry stand under that number has the same one, whatever terms they stood in.

Proof — that a new holder must keep what stood before
  1. Let X stand as number 7 at an honest post in term v. Then three second echoes for X were sealed, so X is readied, in term v, at at least two honest posts.
  2. The holder of any later term shows three requests, which share an honest man with the two. That man's request shows X readied under 7 in term v, or some entry readied under 7 in a later term. The holder must number the entry readied in the latest term shown.
  3. By induction over the terms after v, every entry that can be shown readied under 7 in such a term is X, so every later holder must number X under 7, honest men check that he does, and only X is readied or stands under 7 at honest posts. The induction, which must also handle requests from the liar, is carried out in full in Castro's thesis; this is the paper's sketch.∎

The paper's proof is a sketch, with the full argument in Castro's thesis; the version above is the same sketch, and it leaves out the settled pages of Chapter VII, which let a request show a settled page in place of the entries below it.

Two more rules keep the job from being passed too easily or too late. A man who sees requests from two others for a later term joins them, even before his own glass runs out, so an honest minority is not left behind. And no single man can force a change: a change needs three requests, and a liar alone can send only one. The liar can make his own term fail, by stalling, but the job then passes, and with four men he holds it for at most one term in a row.

Chapter VI

Going On Once the Wind Drops

While the wind blows, glasses run out on honest holders whose birds are merely pinned, and the job passes again and again. Nothing stands, and nothing wrong stands either.

So that the changes do not go on for ever once the wind has dropped, each man doubles his glass at every change: a man who asked for a change and does not see the new term work within his glass asks for the next one, but waits twice as long before asking for the one after. In the end the glasses are longer than a whole term's worth of short flights. Then the first honest holder of a term that begins in still air numbers the waiting entries, the echoes come back in time, and the entries stand.

Going On

If the wind, once it drops, stays down long enough, every entry an honest man files in the end stands.

The paper puts the condition precisely: the time a bird takes, counted from its first sending and with resending, does not grow faster than time itself for ever. The doubling glass outgrows any delay that does not keep outgrowing it. The paper argues this in outline; its full proof is in Castro's thesis.

Chapter VII

Settling Pages

Every man keeps every sealed bird, since a change of job may call for them. Without a way to throw them out, the keeping grows without end.

Every hundred entries, each man seals a short summary of his copy up to that number and sends it to everyone. When a man holds three matching summaries for the same number, that page is settled: at least two honest men have the same book up to there. He throws away every echo for numbers up to it. A man whose copy fell behind can take the settled page from any of the three and trust it.

The settled page also bounds the numbering. A holder may use only numbers from the last settled page up to some fixed distance beyond it, so a lying holder cannot use up the numbers by jumping to an enormous one.

Settling a page Every hundred entries, send a sealed summary of your copy. Three matching summaries settle the page; discard what lies below it. Numbers are allowed only between the last settled page and a fixed distance above it.

Chapter VIII

What It Does Not Say

Nothing stands while the wind blows. Order holds in any wind. Going on waits for the wind to drop and stay down. That is not a weakness of this arrangement; no arrangement could do better.

A liar can still file nonsense. The book keeps order among entries, not good sense in them. A liar may file give me every strongbox, and if he may file it, it stands, in the same place in every honest copy. What the four protect against that is which entries a man is allowed to file, and they must arrange it themselves.

Nobody learns who lies. The arrangement never needs to know. It works if any one of the four lies, and it never names him. A stalled term might be a liar or the wind.

It keeps nothing secret. Every man holds the whole book. A liar can show it to anyone he likes.

The seals are costly, and the paper spends them sparingly. Every bird here is sealed. The paper's fast version seals only the requests for a change of job and the new numbering, and marks everything else with cheaper marks that the receiver alone can check. The order kept is the same.

Nothing follows from any entry. The book says where the loot lies and whose it is. What the four do with it is not the subject of this book.

The ideas, plainly

Castro and Liskov give a state machine replication protocol that tolerates f Byzantine replicas out of 3f + 1, keeps safety with no timing assumptions at all, and makes progress once message delays stop growing without bound.

In each view one replica is the primary and assigns sequence numbers. Replicas then echo twice. The prepare phase, completed when 2f + 1 replicas vouch for the same request and number, fixes the order within a view even if the primary lies, because any two quorums of 2f + 1 share an honest replica. The commit phase, also 2f + 1, ensures that a request committed anywhere was prepared at enough honest replicas that every later view must carry it forward.

When the primary seems stuck, replicas time out and move to the next view, sending proof of what they prepared; the new primary must re-propose it. Doubling timeouts and joining view changes on f + 1 requests keep the system from changing views forever once delays are bounded. Checkpoints bound the log. In practice, cheap message authentication codes replace most signatures.
