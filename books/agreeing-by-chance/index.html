Agreeing by Chance

When messages can take arbitrarily long and even a single computer might crash without warning, no deterministic plan can guarantee that the group will ever reach agreement. But what if a machine that finds itself deadlocked simply flips a coin? Can pure chance break a tie that no fixed rule can escape?

To see how, we return to the four Greek mercenaries camped at the foot of Mount Phyle and watch how a silver coin, tossed privately by each man inside his tent, breaks their deadlock and brings the camp to a single decision.

After Michael Ben-Or, Another Advantage of Free Choice: Completely Asynchronous Agreement Protocols, 1983

First, in machine terms

A coin where reasoning gives out

Suppose a group of computers must agree on one of two values, and any one of them might crash without warning.

Every message between working machines eventually arrives, but there is no upper bound on how long a message can take—so a machine that has crashed looks identical to one whose message is merely delayed. As we saw in The Limits of Agreement, no deterministic protocol can guarantee a decision under these conditions: as long as each machine acts purely as a fixed function of the messages it has received, an unlucky schedule of message arrivals can keep the group undecided forever.* Yet a single change breaks the deadlock:

  • Ben-Or (1983): whenever a round of messages leaves a machine unable to decide, let that machine flip a private fair coin to pick its next preference.
  • Safety is unconditional: no two machines ever decide differently, regardless of how messages are delayed or how the coins land.
  • Termination holds with probability 1: every working machine eventually reaches a decision with probability 1, even though no upper bound on the number of rounds can be named in advance.

Nothing about the network or the crashes is relaxed—only the assumption that machines cannot roll the dice. We tell the story with four mercenaries, their ravens, and a silver coin in each tent.

A split nothing breaks then a coin

With a deterministic rule, worst-case message ordering preserves the split forever. With coin flips, the coins eventually land alike.

The foot of Mount Phyle

The same four tents

Four mercenaries camp at the foot of Mount Phyle, one to a tent, hidden from one another by the folds of the hillside and under nobody's command.* Bandits hold the summit above them, and the four men are known by the numbers of their tents.

Only ravensThe four communicate only by raven. Every raven eventually arrives with its strip intact, but there is no upper limit on how long a flight may take, and two birds released an hour apart may land in either order.
Nobody liesThe mercenaries are honest comrades: no one ever falsifies a message, so every strip that lands can be trusted.
Nobody is killedAt most one of the four might be killed during the night, leaving his tent permanently silent. Nobody actually dies in tonight's story, but their arrangement must work even if one man does.
What the four want, and what bound them

Three demands, and one assumption

Before dawn, all four mercenaries must either attack the hill together or hold their ground together. Each man formed an initial sighting at dusk, and committing is final: once a man steps out of his tent to attack or hold, he leaves his ravens behind for the night.

Because a silent tent could belong either to a dead comrade or to a living one whose raven is still sitting in the pines, no deterministic plan can guarantee that the four ever commit: the order in which ravens arrive can always be scheduled to keep the camp balanced on a knife-edge all night.* Let us state the three demands the mercenaries want to satisfy:

First demand

No two commit differently

Never does one man go out to attack while another stays to hold (agreement).

Second demand

Matching sightings are followed

If all four men saw the same sighting at dusk, that is the plan they commit to (validity).

Third demand

Every man left alive commits

Every man who has not been killed eventually commits to a plan (termination).

Notice that these second and third demands are even stronger than the minimum needed to trigger the impossibility proof of The Limits of Agreement (which only required that both outcomes be possible and that at least one man eventually commit).* Since even those weaker demands cannot be guaranteed by any fixed plan, these stronger ones certainly cannot be either.

Yet the impossibility proof quietly relied on one hidden assumption: the mercenaries are not allowed to draw lots. In that proof, every mercenary is a deterministic machine—given the same initial sighting and the same sequence of incoming ravens, he must make the exact same move every single time.

Nothing in the three demands forbids a mercenary from leaving a choice to chance. What happens if we drop that single assumption and give each man a coin inside his tent?

Dropping the assumption

A coin in each tent

Every mercenary at the foot of Mount Phyle was paid in Attic silver tetradrachms, which bear the head of Athena on one side and her owl on the other. Before pitching their tents, the four agreed on a simple convention: the owl stands for hold, and the goddess stands for attack.

Whenever the rules of a round leave a mercenary unable to settle on an answer from the ravens alone, he flips his silver coin high off his thumb, catches it in his palm, and adopts whichever answer the coin shows.

Crucially, there are four separate coins in four separate tents. The mercenaries cannot gather to watch a single shared coin toss, because the hillside keeps them apart. Nor can they appoint one man to flip a coin for everyone and send the result by raven—for that man might be the one who was killed, or his raven might be the one delayed in the pines! So each man flips his own coin privately inside his tent, and no one else knows how it landed unless a later raven reports it. Try tossing the coins in one tent or across all four below.

Four coins four tents

Over many tosses, owl and goddess come up equally often, though in any short run they can easily diverge.

For the protocol to work, each man's coin toss must have three properties:

(1) Fair

Owl and goddess equally

On every toss, the coin lands owl with probability 1/2 and goddess with probability 1/2.

(2) Fresh

Independent of the past

Each toss is completely independent of every earlier toss in that tent or any other.

(3) Unforeseen

Unknown until flipped

Before a toss is made, nothing in the world can predict how it will land; after it lands, its outcome is no longer a secret.

Why does the third property—that a toss cannot be foreseen before it is made—matter so much? Recall how the impossibility proof in The Limits of Agreement worked: we imagined a worst-case scheduler (an arranger) who can see inside every tent, read every message strip in flight, and choose the exact order in which ravens land so as to keep the camp divided.* Here we grant that adversarial arranger the exact same omniscience over all messages and all past coin flips.

The one power the arranger does not have is a crystal ball: he cannot see how a coin will land before the mercenary actually flips it! Now let us see how the mercenaries structure their rounds of ravens so that this one blind spot is enough to defeat the arranger.

When a man tosses

Rounds of birds

The mercenaries organize their night into numbered rounds (1, 2, 3, …), writing the round number on every message strip so that a slow bird from round 2 is never confused with a bird from round 3.

In each round, every mercenary sends two waves of ravens to all the other tents (and counts his own strip at each wave as arriving immediately). The first wave is his report: the answer he currently favors at the start of the round (in round 1, this is simply his dusk sighting). The second wave is his second bird, which says either sure of an answer (if the reports he read in step two showed a strict majority of the entire camp—at least three out of four—favoring that answer) or unsure. Each round follows three steps:

Step one

Send the report

Send a report naming the answer you currently favor to all tents.

Step two

Wait for three reports

Wait until three reports from this round have arrived (your own plus two others). If all three name the same answer—a strict majority of all four tents—send a second bird saying sure of that answer; otherwise send unsure.

Step three

Wait for three second birds

Wait until three second birds from this round have arrived. If two or more say sure of an answer, you are settled on that answer! If only one says sure of an answer, adopt that answer for the next round. If none say sure, flip your coin and adopt the answer it shows.

Three birds, not four

Why does a mercenary wait for three birds at each step, never all four? Because one of the four comrades might have been killed! Since at most one man can die and no raven is ever permanently lost, at least three birds are guaranteed to arrive at every step. Waiting for the fourth bird would risk waiting forever for a dead man.

Try running a single round at Tent 1 below. Click the reports and the second birds to change what they say, and see when Tent 1 settles, when it adopts an answer without flipping, and when it flips its coin.

One round at tent 1

Tent 1 has read three reports: its own and two ravens. Press a report to change what it says.

Then three second birds: its own, which the reports decide, and two ravens.

Once a man becomes settled at the end of Step 3, how does he actually leave his tent and commit? Remember that the comrades still inside their tents need three birds in the next round to finish their own steps. As we will prove in the next section, whenever one man settles on an answer in round r, every comrade who finishes round r is guaranteed to adopt that exact same answer for round r + 1, so in round r + 1 everyone will report that answer and everyone will send sure of that answer. Therefore, a man who settles in round r can immediately send out both of his round r + 1 birds in advance—his report and his sure of bird for that answer—and then step out of his tent and commit! We call this the rule of parting.

Because those two parting birds are the exact same birds he would have sent had he stayed inside his tent for round r + 1, the comrades who remain in their tents receive the three birds they need in round r + 1 and settle right behind him.

Now let us verify the crucial safety question: since each man flips his coin alone in his tent, why can different coin flips never cause two men to commit to opposite answers?

The round, stated formally

In the paper's terms the four tents are N = 4 processes, at most t = 1 of which may crash; a sighting is an initial value, a report is the message (1, r, x), sure of v is the D-message (2, r, v, D), unsure is (2, r, ?), being settled is deciding, and a process waits for N − t = 3 messages at each step. The three steps below are the paper's round, written for one process.

One round, r, at one tent.

  1. Send the report of round r, the answer favoured now, to all.
  2. Wait for three reports of round r. If more than two of them, that is all three, name the same answer, send sure of that answer to all; otherwise send unsure.
  3. Wait for three second birds of round r. Then: (a) if at least one says sure of an answer, favour that answer from now on; (b) if two or more say sure of it, be settled on it; (c) if none says sure, toss the coin and favour the answer it shows.
  4. Go on to round r + 1.

The same, for N men of whom t may fall silent. Wait each time for N − t birds. Send sure of v when more than N/2 of the reports received name v. Favour v on one sure of v; settle on v on more than t of them; toss otherwise. The arrangement needs N > 2t. On the hill, N = 4 and t = 1.

The rule of parting. A man who becomes settled on an answer in round r at once sends both birds of round r + 1 to all: the report, that answer, and the second bird, sure of that answer. Then the man leaves the tent and commits to it.

This rule is not in Ben-Or's paper. There a process that has decided simply goes on taking part, and nothing is said of stopping. On the hill deciding is committing, and committing is silence, so the rule is needed; the two birds it sends early are exactly the two the man would have sent by staying.

Whatever the birds, whatever the coins

Never two answers

No matter how the coins fall or how the ravens are delayed, two mercenaries can never commit to different answers. Both safety demands—agreement and validity—hold on every run, and the entire proof rests on a simple fact about overlapping majorities.

One sure answer a round

Every sure bird agrees

Within any single round, it is impossible for one man to send sure of hold while another sends sure of attack.

Never two answers

Settling locks the next round

If any man settles on an answer in round r, every man who finishes round r adopts that same answer, and every living man settles on it in round r + 1.

The sightings respected

Unanimous sightings settle in round 1

If all four men saw the same sighting at dusk, every living man settles on it in round 1 without ever touching a coin.

Why can a single round never produce both sure of hold and sure of attack? Because each man sends only one report per round, so there are at most four reports in total. Sending sure of hold requires seeing three reports of hold; sending sure of attack requires seeing three reports of attack. Out of four tents, any two groups of three must overlap in at least two tents—and no tent sends two different reports in the same round!

Now see why a single settled man locks the entire camp onto his answer. To settle on hold in Step 3, a man had to read at least two sure of hold birds. Every other mercenary reads three of the four second birds in Step 3, which means he misses at most one bird in the entire camp. Even if a comrade misses one of the two sure of hold birds, he is guaranteed to read the other one! And since no sure of attack bird can exist in that round, every single comrade reads at least one sure of hold bird, adopts hold (without flipping a coin!), and enters the next round unanimously favoring hold. 1 · The reports

Tents 1, 2 and 4 favour hold

At the start of the round, Tents 1, 2, and 4 favor hold, while Tent 3 favors attack. In Step 2, each man reads three of the four reports and misses one.

2 · Who is sure

Two men read three alike

Tents 1 and 2 happen to miss Tent 3's report, so each reads three reports of hold and sends sure of hold. Tents 3 and 4 see Tent 3's report of attack among their three, so they send unsure. Nobody can send sure of attack.

3 · The second birds

One man settles, the rest follow

In Step 3, Tent 1 reads both sure of hold birds and settles! Tents 2, 3, and 4 each miss one of the sure of hold birds, but because each man misses at most one bird out of four, each of them still reads the other sure of hold bird and adopts hold. Nobody flips a coin.

4 · Parting

Tent 1 has gone, and its birds are in

Before leaving his tent to commit, Number 1 sends his two parting birds for the next round: a report of hold and a second bird saying sure of hold—the exact birds he would have sent had he stayed.

5 · The next round

Every report names hold

In the next round, every report is hold. Each remaining man reads three reports of hold, sends sure of hold, reads three sure of hold birds, and settles on hold.

6 · So

The coins can delay, never divide

Because a mercenary only flips his coin when he sees zero sure birds, nobody ever flips a coin in a round where a comrade settles. Coins can never break agreement once it is reached.

So the coins can never divide the camp, and when all four sightings match at dusk, everyone settles in round 1 without flipping a coin at all. What remains is a night when the sightings start out split and nobody can get three matching reports. On that night, the coins do their work.

Proof · that once one man is settled, all end at that answer

In the paper's terms the four tents are N = 4 processes, at most t = 1 of which may crash; a sighting is an initial value, a report is the message (1, r, x), sure of v is the D-message (2, r, v, D), unsure is (2, r, ?), being settled is deciding, and a process waits for N − t = 3 messages at each step. Claim. If a process decides v in round r, then every process that completes round r adopts v, and every process that does not crash decides v in round r + 1. This is Theorem 1 (iii).

  1. Say Number 1 is settled on hold in round r. Then two or more of the three second birds that reached Number 1 said sure of hold, so at least two such birds were sent in round r.
  2. Any other man who finishes round r read three of the four second birds of that round, and so missed at most one. That man therefore read at least one sure of hold. By one sure answer a round the same man read no sure of attack, and so favours hold: by rule (a) on reading one, and perhaps settled on hold by rule (b). No toss is made.
  3. So every report of round r + 1 names hold. Every man who reaches step (2) of that round sees three reports of hold and sends sure of hold; every man who reaches step (3) reads three sure of hold and is settled on hold.
  4. The birds a settled man sends early under the rule of parting are a report of hold and sure of hold. By step 3 these are exactly the birds that man would have sent in round r + 1 by staying, whichever three reports were read. So the rule changes nothing the others see, and each of them still gets three birds at every step, since at most one man is dead and the settled ones have sent theirs.
  5. No man is settled on attack in round r or any later round, since no sure of attack is ever sent from round r on. So no two men commit to different answers.∎
Proof · that four matching sightings settle the first round

In the paper's terms the four tents are N = 4 processes, at most t = 1 of which may crash; a sighting is an initial value, a report is the message (1, r, x), sure of v is the D-message (2, r, v, D), unsure is (2, r, ?), being settled is deciding, and a process waits for N − t = 3 messages at each step. Claim. If all N initial values are equal, every process that does not crash decides that value in round 1. This is Theorem 1 (ii).

  1. Every report of round 1 names that answer, so every man reads three reports of it and sends sure of it.
  2. Every man then reads three sure birds for it, more than one, and is settled on it. No coin is tossed.∎
How the coin does its work

The toss

When the sightings start out split, the coins come into play in any round where no man sees a sure bird. Let us trace one such night with Tent 4 silent from dusk, so that Tents 1, 2, and 3 always hear from one another.

At dusk, Tent 1 saw hold, Tent 2 saw attack, and Tent 3 saw hold. Step through the rounds below and watch what happens when the three coins first land split, and then happen to land alike.

One night press for the next round

As long as the three reports are split 2-to-1, nobody sees three matching reports, every second bird says unsure, and each of the three men flips his coin. As soon as their three coins happen to land on the same face in the same round, all three men enter the next round with the same favored answer—and that next round settles the entire camp!

Notice two details in the run above. First, in round 1 two of the three living men favored hold, and in round 2 two of the three favored attack—yet neither round produced a sure bird. Why isn't 2 out of 3 enough? Because Tent 4 is not necessarily dead—its bird might simply be slow! If two matching reports out of three were enough to send a sure bird, then on a night when all four men are alive and split 2-to-2, two men could read two hold reports and send sure of hold while the other two read two attack reports and send sure of attack, shattering safety. Requiring three matching reports—a strict majority of all four tents—guarantees that at most one answer can ever be called sure in any round.

Second, in the example above the three coins happened to land alike in round 2, ending the night in three rounds. What if the coins keep landing split? Can we be certain the night eventually ends?

The end of the night

With probability one

No randomized protocol can promise to finish within a fixed number of rounds on every conceivable sequence of coin flips. Just as you could theoretically flip a fair coin and get heads a thousand times in a row, there is a theoretical run in which the mercenaries' coins keep landing split round after round forever.

What the protocol can promise is that the probability of staying split forever is zero. With at most one of the four tents silent, and no matter how maliciously the ravens' arrivals are scheduled, the probability that every living mercenary eventually commits is 1. Why does the probability of finishing reach 1? Because in every round, at most one answer can appear on any sure bird. Anyone who does not see a sure bird flips a fresh, fair coin that the arranger cannot foresee. Whenever all the coins flipped in a round happen to land on that round's single viable answer (or all land alike when there is no sure bird), every man enters the next round favoring the exact same answer, and the next round settles everyone! Because every round gives the camp a fresh, non-zero chance of snapping into unanimity, the chance of missing that exit for k rounds in a row shrinks exponentially toward zero as k grows.

Notice what this does and does not say. No fixed round number can be named in advance by which the camp is guaranteed to have committed. If a mercenary asks, “Are we guaranteed to have committed by round 40?” the answer is “Almost certainly—with probability very close to 1—but not with 100% certainty.” The infinite runs have not been erased from the tree of possibilities; their combined probability has simply been shrunk to zero.

Interestingly, the quick textbook proof sketch for this (given in the proof drawer below) has a subtle flaw when the arranger can stagger rounds—a gap that stood open for nearly thirty years until Marcos Aguilera and Sam Toueg published a complete proof in 2012.

Comparing the night with coins against the night without them shows the exact nature of what randomization changes.

Proof · a sketch, that the four commit with probability one

In the paper's terms the four tents are N = 4 processes, at most t = 1 of which may crash; a sighting is an initial value, a report is the message (1, r, x), sure of v is the D-message (2, r, v, D), unsure is (2, r, ?), being settled is deciding, and a process waits for N − t = 3 messages at each step. A local coin is step 3(c): set the value to 0 or 1 with probability 1/2 each. g is a value, defined in step 1 below. Claim. With N > 2t, every process that does not crash decides with probability 1. This is Theorem 1 (i), which the paper states without proof.

  1. Fix a round r in which no one is yet settled. Let g be the answer named by sure birds in round r, if any are sent, and let g be hold otherwise.
  2. Every man who finishes round r either reads a sure of g and favours g, or tosses. Each toss is fair and fresh, and the arranger cannot see it before it is made, so each comes up g with chance one half, whatever has gone before.
  3. At most four men toss in round r, so with chance at least 1/16 (the sketch's figure, not a proven bound) every one of them comes up g. Then every report of round r + 1 names g, and as in the proof of never two answers, every man who is not killed is settled on g in round r + 1.
  4. The rounds' tosses are fresh, so the chance that this fails in each of k rounds in a row is at most (15/16)k, which shrinks towards nothing as k grows. The chance that it fails for ever is therefore nothing.∎

Why is this only a sketch? Step 1 treats the target answer g of round r as if it were fixed before anyone flips a coin in round r. In an asynchronous network, however, a fast mercenary might finish round r and flip his coin while a slow mercenary is still in Step 3 of round r − 1! An adaptive arranger can wait to see how the fast mercenary's coin lands in round r, and then choose which round r − 1 birds reach the slow mercenary so as to steer round r's majority g against the coin flip that already happened. Ben-Or's 1983 paper stated Theorem 1 without proof; a complete proof that defeats even this adaptive arranger was given by Aguilera and Toueg in 2012.

What is not claimed? No upper bound on the number of rounds can be guaranteed in advance; termination holds with probability approaching 1 as the number of rounds grows.

A fork the arranger does not steer

What the coin changed

Look back at how the endless night in The Limits of Agreement was constructed:* the arranger found an initial dusk sighting that left both outcomes open, and from each open state carefully picked which raven arrived next so the camp stayed open forever.

That entire strategy depended on determinism: because each mercenary followed a fixed rule, the arranger could look ahead along every branch of the tree and know with certainty where delivering a bird would lead. A coin takes that foresight away.

With coins

The arranger cannot steer both forks

Whenever a mercenary flips a coin, the night forks into two branches—one for the owl and one for the goddess—and the coin chooses which branch is taken, not the arranger.

Still there

Endless runs still exist

If you fixed the sequence of coin outcomes in advance, the deterministic impossibility proof would work all over again.* With live coin flips, keeping the night open forever requires the coins themselves to keep landing against agreement round after round—an event of probability zero.

What it bought

Probability one vs. every path

The FLP theorem remains completely true: no protocol can terminate on every path. Randomization trades certainty on every path for termination with probability 1, while keeping safety 100% guaranteed on every path.

Notice how different the coin is from the other ways out of the impossibility at the foot of the hill—a wind that eventually drops (Agreeing When Messages Run Late) or a slate that marks the dead (Telling the Dead from the Slow).* Those other escapes give the mercenaries more information about the outside world. The coin gives the mercenaries no new information about the hill at all—instead, it gives the adversarial schedule less power over them.

Even with coins, however, the schedule retains one power that no protocol can overcome: what happens if two of the four mercenaries can fall silent?

The limits of the coin

What chance does not buy

The first limit on randomized agreement is the number of silent tents. Coins cannot save a camp of four if two men may die: any protocol—randomized or not—requires a strict majority of living participants (N > 2t).

Why half is too many. Suppose up to two of the four mercenaries might die during the night. Then if Tents 1 and 2 never hear a word from Tents 3 and 4, they must still eventually commit (with probability 1), since 3 and 4 might be dead. And if Tents 1 and 2 both saw hold, they must commit to hold—because for all they know, 3 and 4 also saw hold and died at dusk, in which case the second demand requires the survivors to choose hold regardless of how their coins fall! By the exact same reasoning, if Tents 3 and 4 both saw attack and hear nothing from 1 and 2, they must commit to attack. Now let all four live, hold back all ravens between {1, 2} and {3, 4} until both pairs have committed, and watch them commit to opposite plans below.

A hill where two may fall silent the argument

Nobody actually dies here—every cross-camp bird is simply held in the pines until both pairs have committed.

Because each pair hears only from itself, neither pair can tell this split night from a unanimous night on which the other pair died at dusk. No coin can help them, because on a unanimous night the second demand forbids the coins from overturning the shared sighting!

Proof · that with two of four silent, the answers can divide

In the paper's terms this is the case N ≤ 2t, here N = 4 and t = 2, a process being able to crash at any moment. Claim. No protocol, randomized or not, meets agreement, validity when all inputs are equal, and termination for every process that does not crash. This is the remark after Theorem 1: the schedule can simulate a partition.

  1. Suppose Numbers 1 and 2 sighted hold and Numbers 3 and 4 sighted attack, and take any arrangement that follows matching sightings and makes every man not killed commit eventually.
  2. Two men may be silent, so Numbers 1 and 2 must be able to commit without ever hearing from Number 3 or Number 4: for all they can tell, those two are dead. Keep every bird from Numbers 3 and 4 in the pines; Numbers 1 and 2, hearing only each other, commit. Whatever their coins show, what they see is exactly what they would see on a night when all four sighted hold and the other two were killed at dusk, and on that night the second demand has them hold. So they hold.
  3. In the same way keep back every bird from Numbers 1 and 2, and Numbers 3 and 4 attack.
  4. Do both at once, releasing the held birds only after all four have committed: all four are alive, every bird is merely late, and neither pair can tell. The pairs commit to different answers.∎

Three further questions round out what a coin can and cannot do:

What about liars?

Six men against one liar

At the foot of the hill nobody lies. Ben-Or also gave a second randomized protocol for systems with Byzantine traitors (N > 5t, or at least six participants to tolerate one liar), which Gabriel Bracha later improved in 1987 to the optimal N > 3t (four participants for one liar).*

Is it quick?

Private coins vs. shared coins

When N is large and the adversary schedules messages worst-first, waiting for N independent coins to land alike can take an exponential number of rounds on average (though when faults are at most O(√N) and messages arrive in lockstep, it takes only a constant number of rounds!). Later protocols replaced private coins with a cryptographic shared coin to finish in a small constant number of rounds on average.

Does it choose a good answer?

Agreement, not wisdom

When all four sightings agree at dusk, the protocol preserves that unanimous choice in round 1. When sightings are split, the coins simply break the tie so that all four men act together rather than staying paralyzed in their tents.

At the foot of the hill
In computing

When an asynchronous machine cannot decide from messages alone, flipping a random coin guarantees that no two machines ever decide differently and that every working machine decides with probability one.

The FLP impossibility theorem still holds for deterministic rules. Randomization escapes it by changing one assumption—requiring a working majority, and trading worst-case termination for termination with probability one.

Later randomized consensus protocols replaced independent local coins with a cryptographic common coin, bringing the expected number of rounds down to a small constant.

The paper behind this book

Michael Ben-Or, Another Advantage of Free Choice: Completely Asynchronous Agreement Protocols (extended abstract). Proceedings of the 2nd ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), Montreal, August 1983, 27–30. Presents the randomized consensus protocol for crash failures with N > 2t (Theorem 1), the Byzantine protocol with N > 5t (Theorem 2), and constant expected rounds when t = O(√N) (Theorem 3).

The ideas. Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson proved that no deterministic protocol can guarantee consensus in an asynchronous system if even a single process may crash: an adversarial scheduler can always delay and order messages to keep the system bivalent forever. Crucially, their proof relies on determinism, which lets the adversary predict how each process will react to any message delivery.

Ben-Or showed that local randomness breaks the adversary's control without requiring any timing assumptions at all. Processes execute numbered rounds of two message phases each. In Phase 1 of round r, each process broadcasts its preference and waits for N − t reports; if strictly more than N/2 reports favor the same value v, it broadcasts a Phase 2 decision proposal D(v), and otherwise broadcasts “?”. In Phase 2, it waits for N − t Phase 2 messages: if it sees more than t proposals D(v), it decides v; if it sees at least one D(v), it adopts v for round r + 1; and if it sees no D-proposals at all, it flips a private fair coin to choose 0 or 1 for round r + 1.

Safety holds unconditionally on every execution: because two strict majorities of N must overlap, at most one value v can be proposed with D in any round; and if any process decides v (seeing at least t + 1 messages D(v)), every other process (missing at most t messages) sees at least one D(v), adopts v without flipping a coin, and decides v in the next round. Termination holds with probability 1 whenever N > 2t (rigorously proved against an adaptive adversary by Aguilera and Toueg in 2012). Non-terminating executions still exist, but their total probability measure is zero.
