Four Mercenaries Who Must Agree

Four mercenaries, one to a tent, who must all attack or all hold, and whose only word to one another is a strip carried by raven.

Read this first, and any book set at the foot can be read next, in any order. It says who the four are, what carries word between their tents and what goes on a strip, which every book here takes for granted, and what each book changes. The hill they are camped on comes last.

The Four Mercenaries

Four mercenaries are camped one to a tent, and the tents are numbered 1 to 4. Each mercenary goes by their tent's number: Number 1 to Number 4. They are alike. The houses of the ring road hired each of them separately and pay each separately; none commands another, and none of them lies. No mercenary can see another, and no mercenary can hear another.

Each mercenary holds two things: what they saw of their own quarter of the slope at dusk, their sighting, which is either attack or hold; and whatever has reached them by bird since. They act on nothing else.

The four have one question, with two answers: attack or hold. The bandits on the hill above are few and scattered, too far apart to see or help one another. An attack wins by beating them one at a time, all four together on whatever they meet first. A hold wins if the bandits break out, with each mercenary lying by the path down their own gully. Neither answer is the better one, but half attacking while half hold is worse than either: too few to climb, and a way off the hill left open. So all four must do the same thing, and the answer must be one that somebody sighted.

Committing is a definite act. A mercenary who attacks strikes their tent and takes the path round the slope to the meeting place agreed beforehand; a mercenary who holds leaves their tent for the path down their gully and stays there. Either way they have left their tent and its birds, and they will not be back before dawn. What they think before that does not count.

Any of the four may be killed. A dead mercenary's tent simply falls silent. Nobody succeeds them and no bird goes out to report it, so to the others a dead mercenary looks exactly like a live one whose bird has not yet come.

Ravens

Word passes between the tents only by raven. A mercenary writes on a strip, rolls it into a bronze tube, ties it to the leg of a bird belonging to the tent they are writing to, and lets it go. A bird carries one strip to one tent. What it carries is exactly what was written, and it is known whose it is.

How long a bird takes is where the books at the foot differ. On the first nights a bird may sit among the crows of the hill for an hour or for the night: there is no longest flight, and no timer at any tent. Two birds let go an hour apart may land in either order.

Nothing else carries word. The mercenaries light no fires and sound no horns, which would tell the hill where they are and that they are talking. No mercenary leaves their tent to carry a message themselves, because leaving it is how they commit. A goatherd crossing the mountain by the smugglers' tracks may pass between the tents, but they take as long as they take and may never come down at all; a strip given to them would only be one more slow bird, less sure than a raven, and nothing on the hill needs them.

Where the allegory leaks

Real ravens do not carry messages home; homing doves do, and a homing bird flies one way only, to its own loft. So each mercenary really does need birds belonging to the tent they write to, and their stock of them is limited. This makes the mercenaries's lot harder than the model's, which allows as many messages as an arrangement cares to send. It does not help them, and nothing in any book turns on it. The birds here are called ravens and fly as many times as they are needed.

What a Strip Says

A strip carries what its writer knows and nothing more: their sighting, what has reached them, and whatever the night's arrangement tells them to add.

Every book at the foot keeps the same four mercenaries at the same tents and the same ravens between them. What changes from book to book is one condition of the night, and with it what goes on a strip.

Whatever the night, nothing is ever settled by force, and the arrangements are judged only by what they guarantee.

The Hill

The four are camped round Mount Phyle, the limestone massif at the middle of Arche, broad enough to block every line of sight across the island. Eight wooded spurs run down from its top, with gullies between them, and the hill's own crows roost in the pines of the folds, which is where a slow raven sits. On the very top is the crown, a small ruined ring wall, solid all round, held by the bandits with their loot.

The tents stand at the foot of four gullies, at the four quarters of the hill. Two wooded spurs stand between any two of them, which is why no mercenary can see another. The four gullies with tents are the only ways off the hill, since the folds between them end in crags nobody can climb down, which is why a hold with all four in place lets nobody off unseen.

The hill is under siege, and the siege has no end in any book: no assault is made, no wall is carried, and nobody surrenders. The books describe arrangements, not events, and nothing follows from any decision anyone reaches.

Reality Checks

What each part of this place has in common with the real Greek world, so that a reader can tell what is borrowed from what is made up. Close means the real thing is much like it; loose means it is borrowed and changed; invented means the setting made it up; anachronism means the real thing is of another age. A dagger (†) marks a citation given from memory and not checked against the text.
