Taking Stock Without Stoppinghow three houses count what the road is carrying

How do you take stock of a whole system that never stops moving, when no one can see all of it at once? And what can such a count tell you for certain?

On the island of Arche, they sent a messenger in a red sash.

First, in machine terms

Recording a system that is still running

A distributed system is many machines passing messages. No machine can see all the others, and stopping the whole system to look is costly.

  • The aim is a global state: every machine's own state, plus the messages still on their way between machines.
  • The method (Chandy and Lamport, 1985): each machine records its own state, then sends a marker down every outgoing channel. Messages that arrive on a channel after the machine recorded and before that channel's marker are the channel's state.
  • The result may be a state the system never passed through, yet it settles every stable property, such as deadlock or termination.

This edition tells it with three trading houses and their carts on the ring road of Arche.

The houses' question

How much do we hold between us?

In a winter of busy trade, the houses kept hearing that their goods had been stolen. A cart that left one house and had not come in at the next was said to have been taken on the cliff road. Was it taken, or only late?

The houses want to know how much grain and oil is really there, everywhere at once, to set against what the account scrolls say they should hold. Whatever falls short is what the bandits took.

Some of it sits in the three storehouses. The rest is on the road, in carts that take as long as they take.*

The scribes' first plan is to send an auditor to each house, count each storehouse, and add the three counts. Two mornings show why it fails.

Morning one

Counted twice

House 3's auditor counts at dawn and House 2's late in the day. In between, a cart carries two hundred jars of oil from House 3 to House 2. House 3 counted them, and House 2 counts them again. The total is two hundred jars too high.

Morning two

Counted by nobody

The counts are taken as before, but the cart carries two hundred jars from House 2 to House 3. They left House 2's storehouse before its auditor looked, and had not reached House 3 when its auditor closed the slate. The jars have vanished, and no bandit took them.

The error, stated once

Wrong by what moved

Counting the houses one after another is wrong by exactly the goods that moved along the road between one counting and the next. It can run either way, no one can say in advance by how much, and no account scroll shows that it happened.

The first thought

A road that cannot be stopped all at once

The houses' first thought is to stop every cart for a morning, count every storehouse while everything stands still, and then let the trains move again. With nothing on the road, the three counts add up.

First objection

It is costly

The houses feed the three port towns, and a road that stops moving food stops serving the island. A count that halts all trade will be tolerated once and never repeated.

Second objection

The order travels by road

The order to stop goes by messenger and takes as long as the road takes. House 2 may hear it before House 3, and House 3, knowing nothing, quite properly sends a cart. That does no harm. Each house stops sending when the order reaches it, keeps taking in whatever arrives, and counts when its incoming roads are empty.

The catch

Empty how?

A house cannot see the road, and a cart may be out round the next headland behind a lame mule. So each house, the moment it stops, sends down each of its roads a last messenger who means nothing behind me. Nobody overtakes, so when that messenger comes in, the road behind them is empty.

Stopping the island needs a messenger on every road anyway. The keeper of the customs rolls saw it: once the houses have such messengers, why stop at all?

The keeper of House 1's accounts asked a different question. Must the count catch the island at one instant? What the houses need is a total that is sound: no cart counted twice, none gone missing. Whether the total was ever true of the island at a single moment is a separate question, and the houses have no need to ask it.

What is being counted

Nine places, not three

Oil in a cellar is stock. Oil in jars lashed to a mule on the cliff road is in no house's storehouse and on no house's slate. Yet it exists, it is owned, and it is exactly what the auditors counted twice or lost.

So the island is counted in nine places: the three storehouses, and, separately, what each of the six tracks is holding.*

Nine places to count 0 of nine looked at

Three storehouses and six tracks. Choose any of them.

A method that records only the storehouses does not give an incomplete account of Arche. It gives a complete account of a different island, one where carts take no time to travel.

A scribe at a desk cannot count a road. A cart rounding a headland is hidden behind rock, and nobody can post men along the surf. What a house can do is write down what comes in at its own gate. That is enough, if the house knows exactly when to start writing and when to stop.

When to start, and when to stop

The red sash

A house knows when to start: when it counts its own storehouse. What can tell it when to stop? The same messenger the stopped road needed, wearing a plain red sash.

The messenger carries no goods and no orders. Their whole purpose is to divide what was sent down a track before them from what was sent after.

Two rules govern the sash, one for sending and one for receiving. They are the entire method.

The rule of sending

Record, then send the sash first

A house records the inventory of its own storehouse. At once, before it sends any further cart or order down any road, it sends one messenger in a red sash down each of its roads.

The rule of receiving

A sash, and no record yet

The house records its storehouse at once, writes down that this road holds nothing, and then follows the rule of sending, with a sash down each of its own roads.

The rule of receiving

A sash, and a record already made

The house writes down, as that road's holding, every item that came in along that road between its own recording and this sash's arrival.

Any house may start it, and it makes no difference which. No cart is delayed and no mule stopped, and no house needs to know another's stock. Scroll to watch it run.

Three stocks and two slates: jars. Before anything began, the stocks and carts on the road came to . Nothing was bought, sold or taken on this day. No jar was counted twice and none was missed.

A house that has recorded goes on taking in carts as usual, but from then on it keeps a slate for each incoming road, listing every jar and sack that arrives along it. When the sash comes through the gate, that road's slate is closed, and its sum is the road's holding. The house never saw the road. It only needed to know when to open the slate and when to close it, and the sash marked both.

The method relies on two things about the road.* Nothing overtakes on a track. A sash sent after a cart cannot pass that cart, and a cart sent after the sash cannot pass the sash. Every sash arrives. If either fails, the count fails.

Carts are another matter, because bandits do take them. A taken cart does not spoil the count. Its jars leave the island's goods as surely as jars sold into a port town, and the count counts what is left. A taken messenger would spoil it, because the slate they were to close would never be closed.

Do the nine numbers fit together?

Cuts that hold and cuts that do not

To see whether the numbers fit, draw the island's morning in the round: each house a line running outward, later moments further out, each cart an arc from its departure to its arrival.

The count draws a loop through each house at the moment it recorded. The loop is a cut, and everything inside it is counted as past. The houses record at different moments, so the loop is not round.

A cart that leaves from inside the loop and arrives outside it is exactly goods in transit, and the slates catch it. A cart that crosses the loop inward is the fatal error: goods received that, by the other house's record, had not yet been sent.

The morning's carts choose a cart

Each line is one house. Each dot marks when that house recorded its storehouse, and the dashed loop joins the dots. Each arc is a cart, and the number is its jars.

Does anyone have to watch for the inward crossing? No, because the rule of sending rules it out. A house sends the sash down a track before any further cart. No cart can overtake the messenger, so no cart sent after a house records can reach a neighbour before the sash does, and none can be counted as arrived before the neighbour recorded.

Does the count ever finish? Every house has a road to every other, so a sash sent by any one house reaches both the others. Each house that receives one records and sends sashes down all its roads. The island is finite and every messenger arrives in the end, so every house records and every slate is closed.

Proof · no message crosses the cut upward

The marker-sending rule: a process records its state, then sends a marker on each outgoing channel before sending any further message on it. The marker-receiving rule: on receiving a marker, a process that has not recorded records its state, and a process that has recorded closes that channel's record. Channels are FIFO and every marker arrives. Claim. No message is sent after its sender recorded and received before its receiver recorded.

  1. Call an event at a house early if it occurred before that house recorded its own storehouse, and late otherwise.
  2. Suppose some cart were dispatched late at House 2 and arrived early at House 1. This is the upward crossing.
  3. House 2 recorded its storehouse before dispatching that cart, since the dispatch was late. By the rule of sending, House 2 dispatched a red sash messenger down the track to House 1 at the moment of recording — prior to dispatching any further carts down that track.
  4. The ring road is strict FIFO: carts and messengers cannot pass one another. Therefore, the red sash messenger reached House 1 before that cart did.
  5. By the rule of receiving, House 1 recorded its storehouse no later than the arrival of that red sash messenger — either it had already recorded, or the sash forced it to record then.
  6. Thus, House 1 had recorded before the cart arrived, making the cart's arrival late, not early — contradicting step 2.∎
At what moment?

A morning nobody lived through

The nine numbers describe the island at no single moment. Each house recorded at its own hour, and the total describes a morning the island never had.

House 1 recorded first and House 2 some while later. Soon after House 1 recorded, it sold forty jars of oil into its own port town. Later, and still before House 2 recorded, House 2 milled eighty measures of barley into flour. Neither touched a road, a cart or a sash.

So the total describes an island in which House 1 still holds the forty jars and House 2 has already milled its barley. Was the island ever in that state? No. While House 1 still held the jars, the barley was not yet milled, and by the time it was, the jars were gone.

The same four entries in two orders

The morning as lived
A morning the count could have lived

Is the total false, then? It is not. Call the island as it stood when the count began its start, the island as the scribes recorded it the recorded island, and the island when the last slate closed its end.

First, the recorded island can be reached from the start by the very events that happened, taken in an order that keeps every chain of carts and messengers intact. Second, the end can be reached from the recorded island by the rest of those same events.

So the recorded island lies on a lawful path from the start to the end. It is not an idle fiction. It is a state Arche could have passed through, had independent events on opposite sides of Mount Phyle come in another, equally lawful order.

Why may the count reorder the sale and the milling? Because nothing joins them. Two events at different houses with no chain of messengers between them stand in no natural order,* and the count may take either first. What it may never do is reverse events that one chain of messengers joins, and the red sash sees to it that it never does.

Proof · the recorded state lies on a valid execution path

Let Sbegin be the global state when the snapshot was initiated, S* the global state recorded, and Send the global state when the last channel's record was closed. Theorem. (1) S* is reachable from Sbegin by a valid execution sequence consisting of the events that actually occurred, in an order that preserves causality. (2) Send is reachable from S* by the remainder of those events. Hence S* lies on a valid path from Sbegin to Send.

  1. Write down the complete sequence of actual events that occurred on the island during the census: e1, e2, …, em. Each event is classified as early or late, depending on whether the house at which it occurred had already recorded its storehouse.
  2. In the actual execution, some late events may precede early events (for example, if House 1 records early while House 2 continues normal operations before recording).
  3. Claim: If a late event ei is immediately followed by an early event ei+1, they may be transposed in the sequence without altering any resulting state.
  4. They cannot occur at the same house: a house transitions from unrecorded to recorded exactly once, so at any single house, every early event precedes every late event.
  5. Neither can depend on the other. ei cannot have caused ei+1: that would require a cart dispatched after recording at one house to arrive before recording at another, which we proved impossible. And ei+1 cannot have caused ei, because it follows it in physical time.
  6. Because ei and ei+1 are concurrent events at different houses, they commute: executing them in reverse order produces the identical state at all houses and on all roads.
  7. Repeatedly transpose adjacent (late, early) pairs. Each swap shifts an early event to the left. This sorting terminates in a valid sequence where all early events precede all late events.
  8. Execute the early events from Sbegin. Because every early event has occurred and no late event has occurred, each house reaches its recorded local state, and each road segment contains exactly the items recorded on its slate. This state is precisely S*.
  9. Executing the remaining late events from S* leads directly to Send.∎

What such a total is good for

Start with what it cannot do. If a merchant swears that House 2 held three hundred jars of oil at the moment a ship dropped anchor, can the count settle it? No. The count was taken at no one moment, so it cannot speak for that moment.

It can answer questions whose answer, once it becomes true, stays true for ever. The keeper of the customs rolls called these settled properties. Counting only the trade between the houses, with the port towns' buying and selling set aside, some examples are:

  • Everything delivered: every cart has arrived and all houses are idle.
  • Standstill: every house is waiting for carts from another.
  • Exhaustion of supply: a vintage has run out, and no trade between the houses can bring it back.
  • Deficit: jars are gone that no trade between the houses can bring back, as jars taken by bandits are.

For these, the count decides. If the property holds on the recorded island, it holds on the island now, because the end can be reached from the recorded island and a settled property, once true, remains true. If it does not hold there, it did not hold when the count began, because had it held at the start, it would still hold on the recorded island.

Either way, the count gives a sure answer about a real state of the island. That the recorded island itself never happened does not matter, because a settled property cannot tell the path the island took from a path it could have taken.

Have the bandits robbed us? a day with no buying or selling

The account scrolls say the houses hold 270 jars between them. Choose when a bandit takes one cart of 30 jars off the road.

The houses' own question was whether the bandits have robbed them. The count gives a sound inventory, with no jar lost and none counted twice. A cart that was only late turns up on a road slate and is counted. A cart that was taken turns up nowhere.

So the houses take their count on a day when nothing is bought from or sold into the port towns. Then goods only move between the houses, and every move takes jars from one of the nine places and adds the same jars to another. Only the bandits can change the total. The account scrolls say what the houses should hold between them, and the houses set the count against it. A shortfall is a deficit, and a deficit is settled. If the count shows one, the houses have been robbed, and the shortfall is what the bandits hold now. If it shows none, nothing had been taken when the count began.

On a day of buying and selling, the account scrolls move with every sale, and the recorded total is true of the recorded island and of no moment anyone lived through. What was true at one particular moment, nothing on an island without a common hour will ever tell.

The decision rule, in the paper's terms

A property y is stable if for every state S where y(S) holds, y(S') holds for all states S' reachable from S. Let Sbegin, S* and Send be as in the theorem. Rule. Evaluate the stable property y on S*. If y(S*) is true, then y(Send) is true. If y(S*) is false, then y(Sbegin) is false.

  1. If y(S*) is true: Send is reachable from S*, and a stable property, once true, remains true. So y is true of the system now, at Send.
  2. If y(S*) is false: S* is reachable from Sbegin. Had y been true at Sbegin, it would still hold at S*. So y was false when the snapshot began.∎

Examples counting only the trade between the houses: distribution termination (every cart has arrived and all houses are idle), deadlock (every house is blocked waiting for carts from another), exhaustion of supply, and deficit.

The system, stated formally

The system is a set of processes (the houses) connected by directed channels (the one-way tracks of the ring road). A channel delivers messages in the order sent (FIFO), without corruption, after an arbitrary but finite delay, and never loses a marker. There is no shared clock, and no process can directly observe another's state. A global state consists of the local state of every process together with the state of every channel.

The paper behind this book

The paper behind this book. K. Mani Chandy and Leslie Lamport, “Distributed Snapshots: Determining Global States of Distributed Systems,” ACM Transactions on Computer Systems 3, 1 (February 1985), 63–75. The definition of global state, the marker rules, the theorem, and the detection of stable properties.

The ideas. A distributed system has no single vantage point from which its total state can be observed simultaneously. Halting all processes and waiting for the channels to drain does work, but it is costly, and detecting the drain already needs a marker on every channel. The Chandy–Lamport snapshot algorithm records a consistent global state, both process states and channel states, while normal computation proceeds uninterrupted.

By dispatching markers along all outgoing channels immediately upon recording local state, and before sending further application messages, processes partition each channel's stream. Because channels are FIFO, markers cannot overtake messages, and messages cannot overtake markers. This guarantees that no message sent after a sender records can be received before a receiver records, the fundamental condition of cut consistency.

The resulting snapshot S* may never have occurred in physical time. However, it is causally valid: reachable from the initial state Sbegin, with the final state Send reachable from it. This makes it sufficient to evaluate stable properties like deadlock, garbage collection, and termination: if the property holds in S*, it holds in the running system.
