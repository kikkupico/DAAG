Changing Leaders Among Liars

When some machines in a replicated system may lie, replacing a stalled leader has traditionally been complex and expensive. Can a new leader take over as cheaply as any ordinary step — and move at the speed of the network rather than waiting out a timer?

To find out, we follow four bandits on a hilltop who rotate the job of keeping their loot scroll at every single entry, with each leader gathering the others' wax seals into a compact bundle instead of everyone writing to everyone else.

After Maofan Yin, Dahlia Malkhi, Michael K. Reiter, Guy Golan Gueta and Ittai Abraham, HotStuff: BFT Consensus with Linearity and Responsiveness, 2019

First, in machine terms

Replacing a leader that may lie

Suppose a group of computers maintains a shared log of commands, led by one machine at a time, when up to a third of the machines may be faulty or actively lying.

Whenever a leader stalls or misbehaves, the group must replace it. In classic protocols like PBFT, normal rounds and leader changes are both expensive: every replica broadcasts its votes to every other replica — so message traffic grows quadratically with the number of machines — and replacing a leader requires gathering and broadcasting even heavier sets of proofs. In 2019, Maofan Yin, Dahlia Malkhi, Michael K. Reiter, Guy Golan Gueta, and Ittai Abraham introduced HotStuff, removing both bottlenecks with two ideas:

  • Star communication through the leader: instead of everyone broadcasting to everyone, replicas send their signed votes only to the leader, who combines them into a single compact quorum certificate (QC) and broadcasts it out — so the number of messages per phase grows linearly with the number of machines.
  • One extra phase of voting: adding a third phase before a command commits ensures that a new leader can take over using just a single round of messages and without waiting out a worst-case timer (optimistic responsiveness).

Together, these two ideas make switching leaders so cheap and uniform that the group can rotate the leader on every single command. We tell the story with our four bandits on the crown of Mount Phyle and their trained rats.

The arrangement so far

What a change of job cost

In our previous book, each entry in the loot scroll was numbered by whoever held the registrar's job, echoed twice across all the posts, and written in number order at every honest post. Whenever the registrar stalled, the job passed to the next bandit.*

That two-echo arrangement (PBFT) keeps the scroll completely safe regardless of who lies or how hard the wind blows, but the bandits pay for it in two heavy costs:

First cost · All-to-all echoes

Every man writes to every man

In each echo phase, every bandit sends a rat to every other bandit. With 4 bandits, that is 12 rat trips per stage; with 40 bandits, a single stage would take more than 1,500 trips.

Second cost · Heavy handovers

A change of job is complicated

To take over, a new registrar must gather change-of-job requests from three bandits — each stuffed with sealed proofs of readied entries — and broadcast all of that evidence back out so the other posts can verify the new numbering.* Changing leaders costs far more than ordering an entry, and the more often the job rotates, the worse that penalty becomes.

The crown, the scroll, the wax seals, the wind, and the four bandits all stay exactly as they were. Only the protocol changes — and it eliminates both costs at once. Our goal is to make changing the registrar as simple and cheap as proposing an ordinary entry, so that the bandits can pass the registrar's job around the circle at every single entry.

Let us tackle the rat traffic first. Why send a dozen rats criss-crossing the hill in every stage when the registrar can act as a hub?

One stage, two ways

Votes to one man, bundles to all

Every bandit does not need to write to every other bandit. Instead, each bandit sends their sealed vote to the registrar alone, and the registrar sends back the combined proof.

As soon as the registrar holds three matching sealed votes (including their own), they tie those three seals together into a single bundle (a quorum certificate, or QC) and send that bundle out to the other three posts. Any bandit who receives the bundle can trust it immediately, because no one can forge an honest bandit's wax seal: three distinct seals on one entry prove that three bandits voted for it — and with at most one liar among the four, at least two honest bandits must have approved it.

Cutting the rat traffic in each stage was the easier half of the problem. The harder half is simplifying the change of registrar: how can a new registrar take over without having to gather and re-broadcast a huge pile of past proofs from every post?

The new registrar asks for one thing

Three stages and a lock

A new registrar can pick up cleanly where the previous one left off if each bandit needs to send the new registrar only a single item: the latest bundle they have seen. To make one bundle summarize the entire history behind it, entries are linked into a tree of chains: every new entry explicitly names its parent entry, so committing an entry automatically commits the entire chain of ancestors behind it. Two entries conflict if neither is an ancestor of the other (they lie on different branches).

In Basic HotStuff, each entry passes through three stages of voting (prepare, pre-commit, and commit), producing a first bundle (prepareQC), a second bundle (precommitQC), and a third bundle (commitQC). Each bandit needs to remember only two bundles at their post: the highest-term first bundle they have ever seen (highQC), and their lock — the highest-term second bundle they have ever voted on (lockedQC). Scroll through the six steps of a term below to watch how an entry progresses through the three bundles.

1 · Starting the term

Every man sends the registrar their latest first bundle

To begin a term, each bandit sends the new registrar the highest-term first bundle (prepareQC) they hold. The registrar waits for three such reports, picks the one from the latest term (highQC), and proposes a new entry extending the entry named in that bundle — sending the proposal and that justifying bundle out to all posts.

2 · First vote (Prepare)

A man votes if the proposal is safe for his lock

Each bandit checks whether the proposal is safe with respect to their current lock: they send a sealed first vote if the proposal extends the entry they are locked on, or if the justifying bundle carried by the proposal comes from a later term than their lock.

3 · Second vote (Pre-commit)

The first bundle

Once the registrar collects three first votes, they tie them into a first bundle (prepareQC) and send it to all posts. Every bandit who receives this first bundle saves it as their latest first bundle and replies with a sealed second vote.

4 · Third vote (Commit)

The second bundle, and setting the lock

Once the registrar collects three second votes, they tie them into a second bundle (precommitQC) and send it out. Every bandit who receives this second bundle sets their lock to it and replies with a sealed third vote.

5 · Standing (Decide)

The third bundle

Once the registrar collects three third votes, they tie them into a third bundle (commitQC) and send it out. Upon receiving the third bundle, every bandit writes the entry — and all of its ancestors in the chain — into their loot scroll: the entry stands.

6 · Moving on

If a sandglass runs out

If a bandit's sandglass expires before the term finishes, they abandon the current term, advance to the next term, and send the next registrar their latest first bundle — starting Step 1 all over again.

When a term runs smoothly, it ends with the new entry standing at every honest post. When a term stalls partway through, each bandit is left holding whatever latest first bundle and lock they reached, and the incoming registrar needs only the latest first bundles from three posts to propose the next entry. Everything hinges on when an honest bandit is willing to cast a first vote for a new proposal — and that rule has two halves.

The rule for the first vote

Two halves, two jobs

The voting rule for Step 2 (the paper's safeNode predicate) has two halves because it must balance two competing duties: protecting entries that have already stood (safety) and freeing posts whose locks are out of date (liveness). A bandit votes for a proposal if either half holds:

First half · Safety branch

The proposal extends my locked entry's chain

Second half · Liveness branch

The proposal carries a bundle from a later term than my lock

The first half protects a committed chain. If a proposal builds directly on top of the entry a bandit is locked on, voting for it continues that exact same chain and cannot help any conflicting branch win.

The second half prevents a stranded bandit from staying stuck forever. Suppose one bandit locked on an entry in an old term that stalled before standing, while the rest of the hill moved on in a later term. If a new proposal carries a valid first bundle from a term later than the bandit's lock, that bundle proves that three bandits (including at least two honest ones) already voted for a later branch — so it is safe for the locked bandit to unlock and vote for the new proposal.

Because the second half of the rule allows a bandit to override an old lock, we must be certain of one thing: can unlocking ever allow a conflicting entry to stand alongside an entry that already stood?

The first promise

Never two entries

The second half of the voting rule can never allow two conflicting entries to stand. The proof comes in two steps: first within a single term, and then across terms.

Within a single term, two bundles of the same stage can never vouch for conflicting entries. Every bundle requires three seals out of four, any two sets of three out of four share at least two bandits (at least one of whom is honest), and an honest bandit votes only once per stage in any given term.*

Across different terms, the locks prevent a split. Suppose entry W stands in Term a. Because W reached a third bundle in Term a, three bandits cast third votes for W — and a bandit casts a third vote only after setting their lock on W's second bundle in Term a. Thus at least two honest bandits are locked on W at the end of Term a. For any conflicting entry to ever get a first bundle in a later term, three bandits would have to cast first votes for it, which requires the vote of at least one of those two honest bandits locked on W. Yet the first time a conflicting proposal appears after Term a, it neither extends W nor carries a first bundle from a term later than a — so neither half of the voting rule allows a locked honest bandit to vote for it!

Never Two Entries

No matter how the wind blows or which bandit lies, no two conflicting entries can ever both stand at honest posts.

Notice something curious about the safety proof above: to prove that W could never be contradicted in a later term, we used the fact that W's bundle put honest bandits under a lock. Why, then, did we need three stages of bundles instead of just two? The clearest way to see what the third stage buys is to take it away.

Proof — that the locks keep conflicting entries from both standing

Theorem 2 (Safety). Conflicting nodes cannot both be committed by correct replicas. Here W and B are conflicting entries (tree nodes); a, b, and s are terms (views); and r is an honest bandit (correct replica). A third bundle is a commitQC, and a first bundle is a prepareQC.

  1. Suppose for contradiction that W stands on a third bundle (commitQC) from term a, and a conflicting entry B stands on a third bundle from term b. By single-term quorum intersection (Lemma 1), a ≠ b; without loss of generality, assume a < b.
  2. For B to stand in term b, there must be a first bundle (prepareQC) in term b for B. Among all first bundles from terms strictly after a for entries that conflict with W, let term s (> a) be the earliest term that produced such a conflicting first bundle, say for entry C.
  3. W's third bundle from term a and C's first bundle from term s each carry three seals out of four, so they share at least one honest bandit r. Because an honest bandit casts a third vote in term a only after locking on W's second bundle in term a, bandit r held a lock on W (at term a) at the end of term a. Moreover, by the minimality of s, no first bundle (and hence no second bundle) for any entry conflicting with W was formed in any term between a and s, so r's lock at the time of voting in term s was still on W (or a descendant of W) at a term ≥ a.
  4. Now inspect r's first vote for C in term s: by construction, C conflicts with W (so the first half of safeNode is false), and by the minimality of s, the justifying first bundle carried by C's proposal cannot come from a term > a (unless it is on W's branch, in which case C extending it would not conflict with W). Thus neither half of safeNode allowed r to vote for C in term s, contradicting r's seal in C's first bundle.∎
What two stages would do

The hidden lock

If we cut the protocol down to just two stages — so that a bandit locks as soon as they see a first bundle, and an entry stands on the second bundle — safety still holds, but a fatal liveness trap appears: a lock can hide from the next registrar.

Here is how the trap springs under two stages. Suppose an entry reaches its first bundle in some term, and the registrar sends that first bundle to Bandit 3 alone just before everyone's sandglasses run out. Bandit 3 sees the first bundle and locks on it, while Bandits 1, 2, and 4 have never seen it. Now the next term starts. The new registrar waits for the first three reports to arrive — and hears from Bandits 1, 2, and 4 (missing slow Bandit 3). None of those three knows about Bandit 3's first bundle! So the new registrar innocently proposes an entry built on an older bundle, conflicting with Bandit 3's lock.

What happens next? Only two honest bandits can vote for the new proposal, because Bandit 3 is locked on a conflicting branch from a later term and refuses to vote. If the liar withholds their vote while the honest men wait, the sandglasses run out — and at the last instant the liar slips in a third vote to form a new first bundle that only one honest bandit sees and locks on! Under two stages, this deadlock can repeat forever.

Two-stage protocols (like Tendermint) can avoid this deadlock only by forcing every new registrar to wait out a full sandglass (the worst-case network delay) before proposing, ensuring that even the slowest honest bandit's report has arrived. That fix works, but it chains every leader change to the length of a worst-case timer, no matter how fast the rats are actually running.

How the third stage eliminates the wait: with three stages, a bandit locks only when they see a second bundle. And how could a second bundle exist in the first place? Only because three bandits saw the first bundle, saved it, and cast second votes! At least two of those three bandits are honest. Therefore, whenever any honest bandit holds a lock (a second bundle), at least two honest bandits already hold the matching first bundle. When the next registrar waits for the first three reports out of four posts, those three reports are guaranteed to include at least one of those two honest bandits! The new registrar learns of the highest first bundle immediately from the first three replies, without ever waiting for a timer.

A lock that can never hide is the exact prize that the third stage buys. It lets every honest registrar move at the natural speed of the rats.

The second promise

Going at the rats' pace

We can now state our second promise (which the paper calls optimistic responsiveness). While safety holds in any wind, progress requires the rats to arrive, so this promise describes what happens once the wind has dropped:

Going at the Rats' Pace

Once the wind has dropped, any term led by an honest registrar in which all honest bandits remain long enough ends with an entry standing — and each stage waits only for the first three replies to arrive, never for a sandglass timer to run out.

Notice the phrase "in which all honest bandits remain long enough." As in PBFT, the sandglasses ensure that the honest bandits synchronize onto the same term: the registrar's job rotates in a fixed order by term number, and each bandit doubles their sandglass whenever a term expires without progress.* HotStuff cleanly separates this view-synchronization mechanism — which it calls the pacemaker — from the core voting rules, because the pacemaker affects only liveness and can never violate safety.

Still, adding a third stage of voting seems to carry a latency price: if every entry must wait through three stages of bundles instead of two, won't the scroll take in entries more slowly?

Proof — that an honest registrar after the wind drops gets their entry through

Lemma 3 & Theorem 4 (Optimistic Responsiveness). After GST (once the wind drops), in any view with a correct leader in which all correct replicas remain long enough, the leader collects a highQC at least as high as every correct replica's lockedQC, every correct replica votes for the leader's proposal, and each phase completes upon receiving the first n − f = 3 responses without waiting for any timeout.

  1. Consider the highest-term lock (lockedQC, a second bundle) held by any honest bandit at the start of the term, say from term v*. For that second bundle to exist, three bandits must have cast second votes in term v* after receiving and storing the matching first bundle (prepareQC) of term v*. At least two of those three bandits are honest.
  2. To open the new term, the honest registrar waits for the first three new-view messages, each carrying that sender's highest first bundle. Any three bandits out of four share at least one bandit with the two honest bandits holding term v*'s first bundle, so the registrar's selected highQC comes from a term ≥ v*.
  3. When the registrar proposes a new entry extending highQC, every honest bandit votes for it: if an honest bandit's lock is from term v* and highQC is also from term v*, by single-term quorum intersection they refer to the exact same entry, so the proposal extends the bandit's lock (satisfying the first half of safeNode); otherwise the bandit's lock is from a term < highQC's term (satisfying the second half of safeNode). In calm air all three stages complete as fast as the first three honest responses travel.∎
The stages run on top of one another

A new registrar every entry

The scroll does not have to take in entries more slowly at all. Although each individual entry still passes through three stages of bundles before it stands, notice that all three stages have the exact same shape: the registrar sends a proposal with a bundle, the bandits send votes, and the registrar ties three votes into the next bundle. Because the three stages are identical, we can pipeline them along the chain of entries (Chained HotStuff)!

In Chained HotStuff, every term has only a single stage of voting, and the registrar's job passes to the next bandit at every term. Each term's registrar proposes a new entry carrying the latest bundle they collected. When the bandits vote for that new entry, their single vote simultaneously serves as the first vote for the new entry, the second vote for its parent entry, and the third vote for its grandparent entry! Every new bundle advances the three entries behind it by one stage at once.

In this chained pipeline, a bandit can tell which entries are locked or standing simply by inspecting the chain of bundles behind each new proposal (the Three-Chain rule): if a proposal carries a direct bundle for its parent, that parent has its first bundle; if the parent also carries a direct bundle for the grandparent, the grandparent has its second bundle (so the bandit locks on the grandparent); and if the grandparent also carries a direct bundle for the great-grandparent, that great-grandparent has completed all three stages and stands! (If a term stalled and left a gap in the term numbers, the contiguous three-chain is interrupted and the three-step count simply resumes after the gap.)

Because starting a new term under a new registrar requires nothing more than proposing on top of the latest bundle — the exact same step an existing registrar takes — rotating the registrar at every single entry costs zero extra messages.

With Chained HotStuff, the four bandits rotate the registrar's job on every entry and move at the speed of their rats. Let us finish by noting five practical questions about what this design changes and what remains the same.

Five questions

What it does not say

Here are five questions that clarify what HotStuff changes compared to earlier protocols and what conditions it relies on:

  • Does anything stand before the wind drops? No. Safety (Never Two Entries) holds in any wind, but no entry can finish its three stages until the wind drops and the rats can complete their runs — a fundamental limit shared by every partially synchronous consensus protocol.
  • Is a bundle always the size of a single seal? A bundle is as small as a single signature if the bandits use a threshold signature scheme, where any three partial seals combine mathematically into one fixed-size signature. With ordinary individual signet rings, a bundle is simply three wax seals tied together — which still cuts the number of rat trips from quadratic (O(n²)) to linear (O(n)), while threshold signatures make the total number of signature verifications linear as well.
  • Does the third stage make entries slower? An individual entry takes three stages of voting rather than two to stand, adding one round-trip of latency from filing to standing. Because Chained HotStuff pipelines those three stages across consecutive entries, however, throughput remains one committed entry per stage.
  • Does anyone learn who lies? No. Just as before, the protocol never needs to identify the liar, and an authorized liar can still propose valid-format nonsense that gets ordered along with everything else.
  • What follows once an entry stands? As always, the protocol guarantees only that every honest post writes the exact same sequence of entries into its scroll.

Setting The Crown of Mount Phyle

The paper behind this book

Maofan Yin, Dahlia Malkhi, Michael K. Reiter, Guy Golan Gueta and Ittai Abraham, HotStuff: BFT Consensus with Linearity and Responsiveness. Proceedings of the 38th ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC), 2019. — Introduces Basic and Chained HotStuff, proves safety and optimistic responsiveness, demonstrates the two-phase hidden-lock liveness failure, and compares communication complexity with PBFT, Tendermint, and Casper.

The ideas. HotStuff is a Byzantine fault-tolerant state-machine replication protocol for n = 3f + 1 replicas under partial synchrony that achieves two properties simultaneously: linear view change (a new leader takes over using only O(n) messages and authenticators, no more than an ordinary round) and optimistic responsiveness (once the network stabilizes, a correct leader drives consensus at actual network speed rather than waiting out a worst-case timeout Δ).

Instead of all-to-all broadcasts, replicas send signed votes solely to the leader, who combines n − f = 2f + 1 votes into a single quorum certificate (QC, implemented with threshold signatures) and broadcasts it out. Where PBFT and Tendermint use two voting phases (prepare and commit), HotStuff uses three (prepare, pre-commit, and commit). Replicas update their lock (lockedQC) only upon receiving a second-phase precommitQC, and vote for a new proposal if it extends their locked node or carries a prepareQC from a higher view than their lock (safeNode). Because any replica's lock on a precommitQC implies that at least f + 1 correct replicas hold the matching prepareQC, a new leader hearing from any n − f replicas is guaranteed to learn of a prepareQC at least as high as every correct replica's lock without waiting for a timeout. In two-phase protocols, by contrast, a single replica can lock on a first-phase QC that no other replica has seen, creating a "hidden lock" that forces either a liveness deadlock or a mandatory Δ timeout wait on every leader change.

Because all three phases of Basic HotStuff have identical structure, Chained HotStuff pipelines them across views: every view proposes a new block carrying a single QC, the leader can rotate on every view at no extra cost, and a block commits as soon as it is followed by a contiguous Three-Chain of blocks.
