Distributed Algorithms of Ancient Greece

25 landmark papers in distributed computing explained using the same ancient Greece allegory employed by Leslie Lamport in his seminal paper, “The Part-Time Parliament (Paxos)”.

Every book reads two ways. Allegory tells the papers as stories of ancient Greece; Computing tells the same pages in the papers' own terms, with processes, messages and their figures. Switch at any point in any book with the switch at the top of the page: you stay where you are on the page, and the choice follows you from book to book.

The Part-Time Parliament

Any two majorities share a legislator.Any two majorities intersect.

How can a group of machines agree on a sequence of commands when any of them may wander off at any time, and no one is in charge for long?

Lamport answered this himself, with the part-time legislators of the Greek island of Paxos. We present his paper as he wrote it, with interactive figures. It is the paper that inspired this entire project. Lamport ends it with the parliament's fall; the other books set that aside, and in them the parliament is still sitting.

Lamport 1998 · Lamport 2001

One Writer at a Time

The latest seal and line becomes writer.The highest viewstamp becomes primary.

Many systems put one leader in charge at a time. When the leader falls silent, how does another take over without losing anything the last one promised?

We find out by exploring how Schedia, a Greek city on Paxos with laws of its own, kept its law with one legislator at a time serving as the writer, and how a new writer took over when the last one fell silent.

Oki & Liskov 1988

Ordering Without Clocks

A cause always carries the smaller number.If a → b, then C(a) < C(b).

If no two machines agree on the time, how can they agree on what happened first? And when two things happen far apart, how can anyone tell whether one led to the other or neither knew of the other?

We find out by exploring how ancient Greek trading houses kept the order of trades when sundials became useless on cloudy days.

Lamport 1978 · Fidge 1988 · Mattern 1988

Many Copies, Acting as One

As if one steward kept one board.One instant inside every span.

When data is held in several copies, what does it take for them to act as one? And who could ever tell the difference?

We find out by exploring how three ancient Greek trading houses filled one another's orders in the sequence they really came, though none of them could read the hour.

Herlihy & Wing 1990

Taking Stock Without Stopping

A morning nobody lived.A consistent cut, never an instant.

How do you take stock of a whole system that never stops moving, when no one can see all of it at once? And what can such a count reliably tell you?

We find out by exploring how ancient Greek trading houses, hearing that bandits were taking their carts, counted every jar they owned, including those in transit, without interrupting ongoing trades.

Chandy & Lamport 1985

The Limits of Agreement

A dead mercenary looks exactly like a slow bird.A crashed process looks exactly like a slow one.

Can machines always agree when one of them may stop silently and messages take any time at all?

We find out by exploring how four mercenaries at the foot of a hill, unable to see one another, tried to agree whether to attack or hold when any of them might be dead.

Fischer, Lynch & Paterson 1985

Agreeing When Messages Run Late

Safe in any wind. Done once it drops.Safe always. Live after GST.

If messages can run late without limit, agreement cannot be guaranteed; yet real networks are prompt most of the time. Can a protocol stay safe however late messages run, and still decide once they arrive on time again?

We find out by exploring how the same Greek mercenaries, on a windy night when no one could tell how long a raven would take, agreed whether to attack or hold once the wind had dropped.

Dwork, Lynch & Stockmeyer 1988

Answering While Cut Off

Answer, or answer as one.Available, or linearizable.

When one part of a system is cut off from the rest, should it keep answering and risk being wrong, or stay silent until it can be sure? Can it ever have both?

We find out by exploring what the same Greek mercenaries could promise one another about their standing plan when ravens were lost and one tent was cut off.

Brewer 2000 · Gilbert & Lynch 2002

Agreeing by Chance

Sooner or later, the coins land alike.Termination with probability 1.

If no plan can guarantee agreement when a machine may stop without warning, can chance succeed where planning cannot? What if a machine that is stuck simply tosses a coin?

We find out by exploring how Greek mercenary camps, deadlocked over a plan, let a coin, tossed by each mercenary alone in their tent, break the tie.

Ben-Or 1983

Telling the Dead from the Slow

Bad slates can stall the camp, never split it.Safe under any detector.

A machine that has crashed looks exactly like one that is merely slow, and that alone makes agreement impossible. How well must we tell the dead from the slow before agreement becomes possible again?

We find out by exploring how the same Greek mercenaries, each keeping a slate of the tents they thought dead, agreed whether to attack or hold though the slates were often wrong.

Chandra & Toueg 1996

Agreeing Among Liars

Three cannot bear one liar. Four can.n ≥ 3m + 1.

Can machines agree when some of them may lie, telling one thing to one and something else to another? How many honest machines does each liar cost?

We find out by exploring how four bandits holding a hilltop, some of whom might lie, agreed whether to hold it or scatter, first by plain word and then under seal.

Lamport, Shostak & Pease 1982 · Pease, Shostak & Lamport 1980

Keeping Order Among Liars

Any two groups of three share an honest bandit.Any two quorums of 2f + 1 share a correct replica.

Can a replicated service keep its commands in order when some of its machines lie, the leader included, and messages take arbitrarily long?

We find out by exploring how four bandits holding a hilltop held one book of their loot when any of them, their chief included, might be lying.

Castro & Liskov 1999

Changing Leaders Among Liars

A change of hands costs no more than an entry.A linear, responsive view change.

When some machines may lie, replacing a leader has been slow and costly. Can a new leader take over as cheaply as any ordinary step, and at the pace of the messages rather than of a timer?

We find out by exploring how four bandits holding a hilltop took turns keeping their loot scroll, each gathering the others' seals into one bundle instead of every bandit writing to every other.

Yin, Malkhi, Reiter, Gueta & Abraham 2019

Spreading by Word of Mouth

Rumour is quick. The festival is thorough.Rumor is fast; anti-entropy is complete.

Can an update reach every machine in a large system just by each passing it on to a few others, with no one organising the spread?

We find out by exploring how a change to the scholars' compendium spread from library to library by word of mouth, and how the libraries caught whatever the gossip missed.

Demers et al. 1987

Probably Up to Date

The odds of an old copy fall with every moment.Staleness, in probability.

When a store reads from only some of its copies, how likely is an answer to be out of date, and by how much? Can we promise that it is probably not?

We find out by exploring how the libraries of the scholars' coast, each entry answered for by three of them, worked out the odds that a reader who asks only a few is handed the newest version.

Bailis et al. 2012

Changes That Never Clash

Any order. Any number of times. Always agree.Commutative, associative, idempotent.

Can some changes be combined in any order, any number of times, and still come out the same everywhere, so that they never clash and no one ever needs to coordinate?

We find out by exploring how the libraries of the scholars' coast keep one compendium of knowledge, each changing its own copy at once and merging the copies the couriers bring, without ever meeting to settle anything.

Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero & Zawirski 2011 · the same authors' comprehensive study, 2011

What You Can Promise Alone

What a librarian can promise alone.What survives a partition.

When a machine cannot stop to check with the others on every change, what can it still promise on its own? That no one sees a reply before the message it answers? That no one sees a change half made? That two writers who each read a value and write it one higher never lose one of the two?

We find out by exploring what the libraries of the scholars' coast could still promise the readers of their compendium on days when the couriers between them stopped running.

Bailis et al. 2014

Knowing When to Wait

Some questions only grow.Monotone means coordination-free.

Which problems can machines solve without ever waiting for one another, and which force them to wait?

We find out by exploring how an oracle at the fork of Paxos told groups of students whether their work let them live as scholars, apart, or made them live as legislators, waiting for everyone.

Hellerstein 2010 · Ameloot, Neven & Van den Bussche 2011
